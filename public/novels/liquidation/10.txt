The Birth of the First Slave, and the Signature

The forest, stripped of light, was itself a colossal, gaping maw. The moment the last flicker of the camp's firelight vanished behind the dense wall of tree trunks, what descended upon Preston Huntington III was a bone-freezing pitch darkness and a chorus of alien hallucinations.

Crack—

The snap of a single rotting twig beneath his foot sent Preston's heart slamming against his ribcage as if it meant to tear through. For ten years inside the bunker's perfectly soundproofed ten-square-meter cell, he had heard nothing but white noise. Now his hearing amplified even the faintest friction of the wild into the thunderous roar of a massive predator, driving it like a spike into his brain.

"Hngh… hk…"

Preston crawled through the dirt clutching a jagged concrete shard. To fulfill his quota, he needed to gather dry kindling—but his frostbitten fingers, robbed of all sensation, could not distinguish twigs from animal bones. The stench of mud mixed with filth stabbed at his nostrils.

Just days ago, he had considered himself the sole rightful heir to a ruined world. The dazzling night skyline viewed from his New York penthouse, the tops of countless subordinates' heads bowing before him—those feeble memories of power offered no shield in this wild forest. If anything, those hothouse recollections were gnawing his psyche into ever-greater fragility.

THUD—

Suddenly, from somewhere deep in the darkness, something immense crashed through the undergrowth. Perhaps it was the same colossal boar that had demolished the camp the day before. Or perhaps something worse—an unknown apex predator, ten years evolved under radiation and mutation.

"AAAAAGH!"

Preston's reason snapped completely. He abandoned the firewood, abandoned everything, and sprinted wildly toward the faint glow of the camp. Running blind through a lightless forest was tantamount to suicide. His ankle caught on a massive kudzu vine; he was launched airborne and slammed face-first into a rotting tree stump. His nose shattered, and hot blood poured over his bespoke hazmat suit. But Preston felt no pain. He dropped to all fours and crawled toward the light like a beast, his only utterance a continuous, animal scream for help.

At that same moment, David Sterling sat before the campfire, diary open, deep in thought.

Listening to Preston's screams echoing from the darkness, David was recalling the notorious subprime mortgage-linked insurance products he himself had designed fifteen years ago.

Back then, banks had extended massive loans against their homes to urban poor who had no capacity to repay. On the surface, it was packaged as "the opportunity for everyone to own a home." But on David's spreadsheet, those borrowers had been nothing more than prey whose default had been predetermined from the start. When interest rates soared and the poor could not make their payments, the capitalists had seized their homes through forced auctions at fire-sale prices, reaping astronomical profits.

In capitalist society, the most effective method of creating a perfect slave is not the whip. It is engineering a debt that can never be repaid, then lowering a rotten lifeline called a "bailout." When a human being is cornered at the edge of survival, they will willingly loop that rope around their own neck and tighten the knot—even knowing full well that it is a hangman's noose. That is the refined murder known as "contract."

RUSTLE—!

The boundary brush of the camp shook violently, and Preston—drenched in blood—tumbled into the circle of firelight.

"Help me…! There's something out there! I can't do it, please, help me…!"

Preston had returned empty-handed—not a single twig in his grasp. His hazmat suit was shredded to ribbons, and blood dripped steadily from his broken nose. The billionaires who just yesterday had addressed him as "Representative" now looked down at him with the contempt reserved for vermin.

"Representative Preston. You have failed to meet the labor quota stipulated by the Survival Contribution Special Act. This constitutes a clear default on your contract."

Arthur Lindley delivered the verdict in a frigid voice.

"Effective tomorrow, all water and fire rations allocated to you are fully suspended. Please remove yourself from this camp."

"No! I'll die out there! I'll freeze or be torn apart by animals!"

Preston seized Arthur's trouser leg and wailed. It was the moment the last remaining dignity of the fourth-generation heir was ground into the mud. And then—arriving like a guardian angel, or rather, a perfect devil—Nathaniel Pierce approached with his unhurried stride and crouched before Preston.

"Preston. I take no pleasure in watching someone go bankrupt. Considering the friendship our families once shared—the Huntingtons and the Pierces—I cannot simply leave you to die."

At the honeyed sound of Nathaniel's voice, Preston jerked his head up. His eyes burned with the desperation of a man who has found his savior. Nathaniel produced from his breast pocket a single page, pre-prepared, torn from his leather diary.

"I will execute a bailout: full forgiveness of your debt, plus provision of a tent and water. But there are conditions."

On the paper Nathaniel extended, three lines of crude charcoal text were written:

[1. Preston Huntington III hereby transfers all independent property rights and Survival Contribution points to Nathaniel Pierce.]
[2. All products of Preston's future physical labor shall be the exclusive property of Nathaniel Pierce.]
[3. This contract shall remain in permanent effect for as long as the undersigned draws breath on this earth.]

David read those lines and recoiled inwardly. This was not an employment contract. It was a body-surrender affidavit—the kind of document that belonged in a nineteenth-century slave market.

"This… this is telling me to hand over everything to you! You're asking me to become your servant!"

Preston's voice trembled as he protested. Nathaniel smiled with infinite benevolence.

"Servant? This is an 'exclusive labor management trust.' Would you prefer to go out into the forest and become animal feed? Or would you rather stay in my camp, safely receiving warmth and water rations, in exchange for chopping firewood? The choice is entirely, perfectly yours."

Freedom. That fraudulent word split the air of the ruins.

Behind Preston, the freezing darkness of the beast-haunted forest pressed in. Before him, a warm campfire and a canteen of clear water. The truth was, Preston had never had a choice at all.

Jules, who had been watching chin-in-hand from the side, cackled and interjected.

"Hey, Preston. If you're not going to sign, step aside. I need to set up a camera in those bushes so I can record you getting mauled."

Preston's entire body buckled as if collapsing inward. The sound of the last remnant of Huntington pride crumbling to dust seemed audible even to David's ears.

With violently trembling hands, Preston picked up a charcoal stub from the ground. At the bottom of the leather page, he scrawled his name.

P. H. III

That signature—once reserved for hundred-million-dollar real estate acquisition documents—was now branded onto the page as the most degrading mark of voluntary enslavement.

"An excellent decision."

Nathaniel carefully tucked the contract into his breast pocket with a satisfied smile. He nodded to Arthur, who immediately produced a tin cup of boiled water—Preston's allotment. Preston, prostrate on the ground, lapped at the water like a dog and wept without sound.

Not one person watching felt pity. On the contrary, the remaining ninety-eight billionaires were silently, fiercely resolving that when morning came, they would be the first to comply with Nathaniel's system and scavenge firewood—anything to avoid falling to the same "junk" rating as Preston.

David closed his diary.

Act One was over.

On the ruins of a dead world, capitalism had leapt across a decade-long void and resurrected itself in its most grotesque and violent form. A dictator who monopolized real assets. An administrator who wrote laws and rules. Speculators drunk on illusion. And the first legally created slave.

In the wild forest, the great gears of an economy had begun to turn, lubricated with blood and sewage. Now it was time for the bone-grinding dawn labor of the indebted to begin. David's gaze drifted beyond the forest's edge into the darkness. If the rules had been set, then soon someone would exploit the gaps in those rules to trigger a catastrophic inflation. That moment was approaching.
