4
Nocturnal Visitors and the Brutal Lesson of the Food Chain

The third dawn arrived with a sound that none of them would ever forget.

It began as a low, rhythmic thudding—like the heartbeat of something immense pulsing through the earth itself. Then came the squealing: high-pitched, alien, and utterly inhuman, tearing through the predawn fog.

David Sterling's eyes snapped open in the darkness of his crater. He pressed his ear to the ground. The vibrations were growing stronger, closer. Something large—no, several somethings—was stampeding through the undergrowth at terrifying speed.

Then the screaming started.

"WHAT IS THAT? WHAT THE HELL IS THAT?"

The campsite above erupted into chaos. David scrambled up the crater wall just in time to see them: three massive boar-like creatures—each easily the size of a small car—crashing through the perimeter of the camp with the unstoppable momentum of freight trains. Their hides were mottled and thickened by ten years of radiation-warped evolution, and their tusks, curving upward like scimitars, gleamed wetly in the firelight.

The billionaires scattered like roaches exposed to light.

One of the beasts plowed directly through the supply cart, sending purification filters, protein cubes, and medical kits flying in every direction. Another trampled a cluster of tents, the fabric and poles crumpling beneath its bulk like paper. The third—the largest—caught the scent of something warm and alive, wheeled with surprising agility, and bore down on a cluster of screaming figures.

"RUN! JUST RUN!"

Cohen was the first to be caught. The old resort magnate stumbled over a root and went sprawling. The beast's tusk caught the hem of his hazmat suit and flung him sideways into a collapsed wall. He hit with a sickening crack and crumpled, whimpering.

Preston Huntington III, in a display that would have been comical under any other circumstances, attempted to climb a tree. He made it exactly one meter before his arms—atrophied by a decade of VR treadmills and AI-served meals—gave out, and he crashed back to the muddy ground. The boar thundered past him, close enough that its coarse hide scraped against his back, and Preston went rigid with terror, his designer boots planted in a puddle of his own urine.

As quickly as they had come, the beasts were gone—crashing back into the darkness of the forest, leaving behind a camp that looked as though it had been hit by a localized hurricane.

David surveyed the aftermath.

The supply cart was overturned. At least a third of the purification filters—their most critical resource—were smashed or scattered into the mud. Two people had broken bones. Cohen had fractured his wrist. Several tents were destroyed beyond repair.

But the real damage was psychological. These were men and women who had never in their lives been physically powerless. Their bodies had been decorative—maintained by personal trainers and cosmetic surgeons for aesthetic purposes, never for function. They had controlled the world through screens, contracts, and phone calls. And now a mindless animal had reduced their entire settlement to rubble in under ninety seconds.

In the stunned silence that followed, Nathaniel Pierce was the first to speak.

"We need defenses. A perimeter. Walls."

His voice was calm, almost academic. He stood amid the wreckage, untouched and unruffled, as if the stampede had been nothing more than a mildly inconvenient board meeting.

"And we need to establish clear rules. Resource allocation, task delegation, a system of contribution and reward. What happened tonight proves that we cannot survive as individuals. We need structure. We need a system."

Several heads nodded. In their terror and confusion, Nathaniel's composure was magnetic, his corporate authority reflexively reassuring. They did not notice—or perhaps chose to ignore—the way his eyes flicked toward the scattered purification filters with the focused hunger of a man who had just identified the scarcest commodity in a new market.

Arthur Lindley, former Chief Human Resources Officer of a Fortune 10 corporation, stepped forward. A thin, precise man who had spent his career designing evaluation frameworks that determined whether thousands of employees kept their jobs, Arthur now surveyed the traumatized billionaires with the same clinical detachment he'd once applied to performance reviews.

"Nathaniel is right. I'll draft a contribution system by morning. Every individual will be assessed on their capacity to provide labor. Those who contribute will receive resources. Those who don't—" He paused, letting the implication hang.

David closed his diary and lay back against the cold stone of his crater. Above him, the fires had died, and the forest was absolutely still—as if the massive beasts had never existed. But David knew better. He had seen them, measured them, assessed them with the eye of a man trained to calculate catastrophe.

The boars were not the threat. The boars were merely the excuse. The real danger was unfolding right now, in the aftermath, as Nathaniel Pierce and Arthur Lindley stood before the terrified billionaires and offered them the oldest bargain in the history of power: surrender your freedom, and we will keep you safe.

It was the social contract of Hobbes, the protection racket of the Mafia, the founding myth of every empire that had ever risen. And these brilliant, ruthless captains of industry were falling for it—not because they were stupid, but because they were afraid. Fear, David knew, was the most liquid currency in any market.

Day Three. The boar attack has given Nathaniel the crisis he needed. By tomorrow, there will be rules. By the day after, there will be ranks. Within a week, this camp will have its own economy—built not on gold or paper, but on water and fear. I have seen this before, in the spreadsheets of every civilization that ever collapsed. The formula never changes: monopolize necessity, manufacture scarcity, and debt becomes indistinguishable from slavery.

He closed the diary and turned on his side.

Somewhere in the deep forest, the boars grunted and settled. They had eaten well tonight—not flesh, but the last remnants of these people's illusion that their old world's rules still applied.
