The Night They Ate Their Stocks

Bank run. The most primal, most terrifying term in the history of capitalism is born, ironically, not when money is scarce, but when there is far too much of it.

"Let go! I'm first! I've got three vouchers right here! Give me three liters of water NOW!"
"Don't push! I've got five! I can clear all my debt and still have two cups left!"

The billionaires who had scrambled through the mud collecting Jules's counterfeit Water Vouchers now swarmed toward the purification filter like a zombie horde. Even Richard, with his shattered wrist dripping blood, crawled forward clutching a fistful of paper.

Just an hour earlier, they had been paralyzed with terror at the prospect of forest labor. Now a few scraps of paper fallen from the sky had stripped them of all reason, transforming them into a rioting mob. Preston swung his club to hold them back, but the mania of a crowd drunk on greed could not be stopped by brute force alone. The mob trampled Preston without mercy and surrounded the purifier.

"Nathaniel! Give us water as promised! These vouchers have your signature!"

Dozens of hands thrust mud-soaked vouchers toward Nathaniel's face. He stood with his back against the purifier's valve, which he had sealed shut, and gazed down at the crowd with eyes gone cold as stone.

"These are counterfeits manufactured by Jules. Payment is refused."

At Nathaniel's frigid declaration, a venture capitalist snapped back, veins bulging:

"How exactly do you prove which are counterfeit? The signature, the ink, the paper stock—they're identical to the ones you handed out yesterday! Can you actually distinguish your 'real' vouchers from Jules's fakes?"

It was an unanswerable point. This was wilderness currency—no serial numbers, no holograms. The instant genuine and counterfeit became indistinguishable, even Nathaniel's original vouchers had lost all value.

It took exactly one second for market trust to collapse. Everyone had simultaneously realized the terrifying truth: the currency had always been nothing more than worthless scraps of paper.

"If you don't hand over the water right now, we'll smash the machine!"

The mob began shaking the purifier by its handle. In that instant, Nathaniel drew a heavy metal wrench from his suit and, with a single motion, detached the purifier's critical component—the pressure pump valve.

Hissss—

Internal pressure bled away, and the machine went dead. The mob froze. Without that valve, the filter was nothing but scrap metal.

"Smash it if you like."

Nathaniel tucked the extracted valve deep inside his hazmat suit and lifted his chin with imperious calm.

"Those papers in your hands are now garbage. And my purifier is closed for business. You want water? Then go ahead—chew on those scraps of paper and see if they quench your thirst."

Nathaniel strode back to his tent with Arthur in tow, leaving behind a snarling, impotent crowd. The abandoned billionaires clutched the dead machine and screamed into the void. The dozens of Water Vouchers clenched in their fists finally revealed themselves for what they had always been—worthless paper—and drifted limply to the mud.

From a fallen log at a safe distance, David Sterling watched the entire debacle unfold and let out a dry, hollow laugh.

Twelve years ago, when hyperinflation had struck a South American nation, David had been dispatched for a field audit. The government, printing money without limit to service its debt, had watched its currency's value plummet by tens of thousands of percent overnight. When David arrived in the capital, what he saw was street sweepers using brooms to shovel mountain-sized piles of banknotes into storm drains.

Citizens needed a wheelbarrow full of bills to buy a loaf of bread, yet merchants refused to accept the money. The banknotes were worth less than toilet paper. When cold nights came, people stuffed the meaningless bills into their fireplaces and burned them for warmth.

The horrifying reverse alchemy of money losing trust and reverting to its physical mass—mere paper. David had signed off on a 100 percent probability of national bankruptcy. And now, in this narrow forest, this miniature capitalism had compressed and replicated that national catastrophe in half a day.

"Idiots."

A presence approached David's side. It was Chloe Bennett—the bunker's chief structural engineer—who had kept her distance from the group, spending her days collecting soil samples and cataloguing mutated vegetation.

"While they were scrambling for counterfeit bills, they forgot the most important thing they needed to do today."

Chloe pointed a cold gaze toward the empty camp perimeter.

David followed her line of sight and felt a jolt of realization.

She was right. All afternoon—between scooping up fake vouchers and rioting at the purifier—not a single one of the hundred billionaires had entered the forest to gather firewood.

And the sun was plunging mercilessly below the horizon.

"Cold… it's so cold!"

When darkness consumed the forest and the wild's murderous night wind arrived, the adrenaline-soaked billionaires were hit by violent, shuddering chills. They instinctively flocked to the central campfire Dennis Chow had maintained. But with no firewood supplied, the fire had dwindled to a dying bed of red embers.

"Dennis! Fire! Stoke the fire! We're going to freeze!"

Cohen screamed, shaking violently.

But Dennis stood with his back to the embers, arms crossed.

"How am I supposed to stoke a fire with no firewood? Every fee you paid me today was in those garbage Water Vouchers. I'm closed. This ember is mine. Everyone else, get lost."

"You heartless bastard! Do you know how much we paid you yesterday?"

The crowd surged toward Dennis in fury, but he whipped out a sharpened stone knife and shrieked:

"Don't come any closer! If the currency is worthless, then it's back to barter! You want warmth? Strip off those hazmat suits and hand them over!"

It was the brutal regression to a real economy. But no one coveted hazmat suits caked in sewage and mud. The only tangible assets they had left were hundreds of counterfeit Water Vouchers no one would accept and useless old-world documents hauled from the bunker.

The wind intensified, and the campfire's last flickering flame began to gutter dangerously.

Perfect darkness and the terror of freezing to death seized them by the throat. The chattering of someone's teeth filled the forest's silence.

Then Cohen began crawling across the ground like a man possessed.

He scooped up a fistful of the counterfeit Water Vouchers that had been trampled into the mud during the day. With trembling hands, he hurled the wad of paper onto the campfire's dying embers.

FWASH—

The premium vellum caught fire, and a bright, warm flame leapt momentarily to life.

"Fire… the fire's alive!"

A deranged light entered the eyes of every billionaire who witnessed it.

They frantically emptied their pockets. The Water Vouchers they had clawed from each other's hands, drawing blood—now they stuffed them frantically into the campfire to survive.

The paper burned, painting the forest darkness in shades of red. But premium vellum was not firewood; it flared brilliantly and disintegrated to ash in moments.

"More… we need to burn more!"

Preston Huntington III, eyes wild, tore open his backpack. What he pulled out were not Water Vouchers. They were the documents he had guarded since before the end of the world: the Huntington family's ownership deeds for fifteen Manhattan buildings, and a bundle of bearer bonds printed on the finest vellum.

"Burn it all! Every last scrap of this garbage!"

Preston shoved hundreds of millions of dollars' worth of old-world property certificates into the flames. The others followed suit. Swiss bank account certificates, last wills and testaments, rare stamp collections, and stock certificates—all fed into the campfire one by one, becoming kindling for survival in the wild.

David set down his diary and walked toward the grotesque bonfire.

The smell of hundreds of billions of dollars' worth of assets burning was nothing more than the acrid smoke of cheap paper combusting. Humanity's hundred most powerful individuals were shivering around a fire fueled by the very symbols of capital they had spent their lifetimes erecting—burning them for warmth, trembling like animals.

Paper wealth proved its only genuine value through the meager thermal energy—the BTUs—released as it burned.

"The most beautiful funeral capitalism has ever received," David murmured, gazing at the glowing ash of stock certificates.

That night, they survived hypothermia only by burning every last stock, every title deed, every certificate of rights they possessed—devouring them as if they were chewing and swallowing their own wealth. When morning came, all that remained at the fire pit was a mound of ash, announcing the death of vanity and the collapse of the system.

The credit economy had been incinerated in a single night. All that was left now was the most primal, most violent struggle for survival—in a muddy wasteland where neither currency nor rules existed any longer.
