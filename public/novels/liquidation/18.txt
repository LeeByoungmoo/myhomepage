18
Humans Reduced to Numbers on a Ledger

The shape that materialized from the dense fog was unlike any living thing Preston Huntington III had seen in his entire life.

A massive skeleton, well over two meters tall. Its body was sheathed in a grotesque suit of armor—raw animal hides layered with crude plates of unidentifiable scrap metal. Its face was hidden behind a metal mask, seemingly fashioned from a retrofitted gas mask, and behind the lenses glinted not the mindless hunger of a beast but a cool, intelligent killing intent.

Preston's heart froze solid.

A radiation-mutated wild animal he could have understood. But the weapon in the creature's hand was no product of nature. Mounted on the tip of a long, sleek steel shaft was an ultra-high-temperature plasma cutter, sparking faint blue arcs—affixed like a blade. That weapon was the secret behind the cleanly severed animal bone Chloe had discovered the day before.

*Human… It's human… A survivor…!*

The realization struck Preston like a thunderbolt. But what flooded him was not relief—it was overwhelming terror. Unlike the billionaires who had spent ten years degenerating inside a sterile bunker, subsisting on cultured meat, these beings had endured the hell of radiation and wilderness with nothing but their bare bodies, evolving into the deadliest apex predators. They were the neo-humans—the true survivors.

The creature took one slow step toward Preston.
Preston tried to scream, but his vocal cords, paralyzed by terror, could not produce even the whistle of escaping air. The creature glanced down at Preston's rusted military hatchet lying in the dirt—the same way one might glance at a broken toy—then, instead of seizing him by the throat, delivered a single dismissive kick to Preston's chest with its massive combat boot.

CRACK—!

"Gkh—!"

It was like being struck by a freight truck. Preston's body lifted off the ground and slammed into a mud pit three meters away. The wet crunch of two or three ribs snapping hammered against his eardrums.

The creature did not even bother to finish him off—as though extinguishing his life was not worth the expenditure of time. It turned away from Preston's crumpled form with the kind of absolute indifference reserved for vermin, pivoting toward the steel-disguised dead tree. The fact that he was not even worth killing shattered the last remnants of Preston's sanity.

"AAAAAAGH!"

Forgetting the agony of his broken ribs, Preston scrambled on all fours and began clawing his way back toward camp in a frenzy of animal terror.

At that same hour, the camp's morning was seething with a grotesque vitality.
Through the night, the elderly and ailing billionaires had gathered around the trading-floor ledger scratched into the dirt, buzzing with anticipation over how massive a log their invested "gladiators" would haul from the forest. The most excited among them was Cohen. He was the majority shareholder—the man who had staked his entire supply of precious antibiotic ointment on the "blue-chip underlying asset" named Preston.

"It's about time. He should be dragging a log out any minute now."
Cohen licked his cracked lips and craned his neck toward the tree line. The other investors around him did the same. They had no interest whatsoever in the fact that a human being was being brutalized by predators and ground down by labor. Their sole concern was the three hundred milliliters of water they would receive as today's dividend.

Then the underbrush at the forest's edge thrashed violently, and a mud-and-blood-soaked bundle of rags tumbled into the center of camp. It was Preston.

"Preston! You're back!"
Cohen let out a whoop and rushed forward. But his elation curdled into fury in less than a second. Preston's hands held no log—not even a single rotting branch.

"Hey, you goddamn idiot! Where's the wood? Where's MY wood! I bet everything I had on you, and you come back empty-handed?!"
Cohen seized Preston by the collar, screaming into his face.

Preston's eyes had lost all focus, drifting aimlessly through empty space. As though Cohen's rage did not exist, he began panting like a cornered animal and babbling incoherently.

"Steel… there were trees made of steel… No, a monster. It was a monster! It had a spear that shoots fire! It's watching this camp! We're all going to die—every last one of us is going to be feed for those things!"

Preston's ghastly warning echoed across the camp.
For one instant, silence fell. But only David Sterling and Chloe Bennett grasped the horrifying truth behind his words. The billionaires' reaction was, as David had predicted, far more grotesquely chilling than he had imagined.

"Cut the bullshit, you fraud!"

Cohen backhanded Preston across the face.
"A monster? Trees made of steel? You couldn't find any wood so you're putting on this lunatic act to cheat me out of my dividends! Give me back my ointment! Cough up my investment right now!"

Whether Preston had encountered a monster in the forest, whether he had nearly been killed—none of it mattered to Cohen and his fellow investors. To them, Preston's terror was merely "a lie fabricated to excuse a decline in returns."

Arthur Lindley, the former Chief Human Resources Officer, approached with a cold expression, bark-ledger in hand.
"Mr. Huntington. Do you have any timber to deliver?"

"No… I'm telling you the truth! There's something else out there in that forest besides us!"
Preston clawed at the air, bloody tears streaking his face, but Arthur pushed his glasses up his nose and drew a single decisive line through the ledger.

"Judging by your condition, you have suffered a total loss of labor capacity. Preston Huntington III, effective today, I hereby declare you bankrupt. All futures options and derivative contracts linked to Preston are, as of this moment, in full default."

That declaration was a financial bomb detonated in the dead center of the camp.

"De—default?! That's insane! I sold my tent as collateral against Preston's water vouchers!"
"Margin call! You're going to turn every Preston-linked product into toilet paper?!"

The camp descended into pandemonium in an instant. The investors forgot all about Preston's shattered mind and the monster in the forest, seizing each other by the collar and throwing punches over the evaporated value of their assets. The moment Preston—the "most blue-chip asset"—collapsed, the web of water derivatives tangled around him like a spider's silk triggered a cascade of defaults.

David watched from one corner of that frenzied trading pit, looking down at Preston, who sat on the ground bleeding from the mouth and laughing like a man who had lost his mind.

Before the apocalypse, David had once investigated the explosion of a massive offshore oil rig. When the rig blew and eleven workers burned to death, when the sea was smothered beneath a blanket of black crude, what was the first thing the board of directors confirmed when they convened in their crisis room? The fate of the workers? A plan to restore the marine ecosystem?

No. They had screamed at the plummeting graph of their stock price on the screen. Human tragedy registered on their monitors only as red negative numbers. Within the system of capitalism, a human being was not recognized as a creature of bone and flesh—only when reduced to a number drifting across a ledger was its existence granted any value.

And now, here in this forest, the psychological disintegration and mortal terror of the human being called Preston Huntington III was being processed on Cohen's ledger as nothing more than a loss of "negative three hundred milliliters."

"The uncontrollable risk has finally shown its true form."
Chloe drew close to David's side and whispered.

"They've just heard a warning that neo-humans carrying burning spears are closing in from the forest, and they're too busy raging over the evaporation of numbers on a ledger to see reality."

David nodded and fixed his gaze on Nathaniel Pierce, who had emerged from his tent.
An unprecedented displeasure was etched across Nathaniel's face—fury at the outbreak of an uncontrollable event, a "cascading derivative default," in the market he had so meticulously governed. Nathaniel still could not even conceive of the true threat beyond the tree line. He was barking orders at his enforcers to drag Preston away, intent on restoring market order.

"That petty god of finance still doesn't understand."
David tore a page from his diary and muttered.

"Preston coming back alive was no miracle. It means the predator in that forest let the bait go—so it could pinpoint the exact location of this pathetic, disorganized pantry of ours."

One hundred billionaires, blinded by numbers, deaf to the approaching footsteps of slaughter.
At the very moment their grotesque ledger game of capitalism reached its crescendo, from beyond the tree line at the forest's edge, dozens of heavy combat-boot footfalls—accompanied by an eerie mechanical hum—were slowly tightening their encirclement around the camp.
