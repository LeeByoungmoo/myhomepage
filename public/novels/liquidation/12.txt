The Invisible Currency in an Empty Forest

The most terrifying morning in capitalism begins not when the sun rises, but when a due date arrives.

Dawn of the fourth day on the surface. Before the ashen fog had even lifted from the forest, a sharp metallic clanging shattered the camp's stillness. Arthur Lindley was hammering a rusted iron pipe, dragging the billionaires out of their tents.

"Rise and shine! The hour of debt repayment has arrived!"

The billionaires who had borrowed Water Vouchers from Nathaniel the night before—relieving their thirst on credit—crawled out one by one, their faces bloated, their backs screaming. Yesterday's sweet liquidity party was over. Now they had to venture into the forest and physically repay four points of labor per voucher—three points of principal plus one point of interest—with their own bodies.

David Sterling had risen early and sat among the damp brush, observing their "morning commute."

A hundred billionaires stood in a daze, each clutching a military hatchet, a saw, or an empty sack. Before them stretched a forest of colossal kudzu vines and grotesquely oversized ferns tangled together—a merciless jungle that devoured physical stamina with every step. For men who had spent their lives signing documents in air-conditioned comfort, the notion of felling timber in that jungle approached physical impossibility.

At that moment, the capitalist instinct imprinted in their marrow conjured another grotesque solution: subcontracting.

"Listen—I'll give you one Water Voucher, the one I got for trading my Swiss watch. Would you gather my share of firewood too?"

Cohen, the resort magnate, seized the sleeve of a former IT startup founder and whispered conspiratorially. Rather than crawling through the forest himself, he intended to outsource the labor using surplus capital—the counterfeit currency he had accumulated through speculation.

But the startup founder snorted and shook off Cohen's hand.

"Are you insane? Just gathering four points' worth of wood to pay off my own debt is going to blow out my lumbar disc today. You want me to do eight points of backbreaking labor for one more slip of paper? My spine is worth more than your lousy voucher."

Cohen was staggered. The Water Voucher—the very thing they had clawed at each other's throats to obtain just last night—had plummeted to worthlessness the instant daylight arrived and real physical labor loomed.

David swallowed a hollow laugh. This miniature capitalism had collided head-on with a perfect contradiction.

In the civilized era, capitalism had functioned because an infinite supply of cheap laborers stood ready to grind their bones to dust for money. The dangerous, filthy work had always been the lot of developing-world poor or gig-economy day laborers chained to apps.

But in this camp, there was no "other" to exploit. All one hundred people were hothouse orchids of the top 0.001%, elderly or physically decrepit. Everyone held money—vouchers—and wanted to hire others. No one possessed the physical stamina—the supply—to actually sweat. In the end, the substanceless currency called the credit voucher was being systematically demolished by the laws of thermodynamics, which demanded that actual calories be burned.

"Forget your subcontracting schemes and get moving! Anyone who fails to deliver four points' worth of firewood to camp center by sunset will be charged compound late fees!"

At Arthur's barking, the debtors—Cohen among them—reluctantly dragged their hatchets and dispersed into the forest's darkness.

Two hours later, a terrible scream echoed from the forest's shallow perimeter.

"AAGH! My hands! My hands!"

David followed the sound and found Cohen sprawled beneath a massive oak stump. He had apparently swung the rusted military hatchet a few times at the firewood. But hands that had received nothing but premium manicures their entire lives could not withstand the rough friction of the axe handle.

Cohen's palms were covered with blisters the size of ping-pong balls. Several had burst, and a mixture of blood and serum had stained the axe handle crimson.

"This can't be happening… this is insane…!"

Cohen clutched his ruined palms and sobbed like an animal. His tears were not solely from physical pain. Ten swings of the axe. A mere ten swings had utterly incapacitated his body. The titanic ego that had believed—after a lifetime of commanding others' labor—that he was the most competent, most powerful being on earth, had been shattered to pieces before a single tree root. It was a perfect, total humiliation.

The other billionaires fared no better. One was bleeding from the calves where thorns had shredded his hazmat suit. Another had thrown out his back carrying firewood and was crawling face-down through the dirt.

For ten years on the bunker's intranet, they had jogged on VR treadmills, smugly boasting that their BMI ranked in the top 1 percent. But that artificial exercise may have sculpted sleek abdominal muscles—it had never callused their palms or built the spinal erector savagery needed to bear heavy loads. Their bodies had been purely decorative. Display models, not survival equipment.

As David watched Cohen—trembling, gathering bloodstained scraps of firewood—he recalled an industrial accident compensation case he had read as a junior actuary twenty years ago.

When an Amazonian logger was crushed under a falling tree and paralyzed from the waist down, the multinational corporation assessed his lost-labor compensation at fifteen thousand dollars. The executives had sneered in their conference room: "Their spines exist as parts to be consumed for our benefit. Fifteen grand is generous for a part."

Cohen—who had treated other people's spines and bloodied palms as fifteen-thousand-dollar consumable parts—was now crawling through the dirt on his own burst palms, begging for half a cup of water.

By afternoon, the battered billionaires staggered back to camp. The amount of firewood they dragged in was pitiful. A few twigs. A single broken log. That was the sum total of labor purchased with their torn palms and herniated discs.

Preston stood like a slave before the purification filter. Arthur held a scale, appraising their haul.

Cohen limped forward and deposited three bloodstained bundles of firewood. Arthur pushed up his glasses and nudged the bundles with his toe.

"These branches have absorbed far too much moisture. They won't produce adequate heat. I'd assess this at 1.5 points."

"WHAT?! Are you joking? My palms are shredded! I chopped for four hours straight to bring those back!"

Cohen screamed with bulging eyes, but Arthur merely smiled coolly.

"Chairman Cohen, the market does not evaluate your 'effort' or 'process.' It evaluates only the value of the delivered result. Whether you labored four hours or bled doing it, this firewood is worth 1.5 points. With principal and interest totaling four points, you are 2.5 points in arrears today."

Arthur raised a charcoal stub and increased Cohen's debt on the bark ledger.

"Compound interest will be applied to the outstanding balance starting tomorrow. You will owe a total of six points."

"You… you swindling bastards! You're all thieves!"

Cohen raged, but when Preston stepped forward brandishing his club, Cohen collapsed to his knees. He was not alone. Not a single billionaire who had entered the forest that day had met the four-point quota. They had confirmed, with bone-deep certainty, just how worthless their bodies truly were—and wept in despair before the exponentially ballooning ledger of their debts.

Inside Nathaniel's tent, having received the full report, Nathaniel sipped clear water from a makeshift wine glass and gazed at David.

"Was I not right, David? They are trapped in a spiral of debt they will never repay. Tomorrow, the debt doubles. The day after, it quadruples. Eventually, they will prostrate themselves at my feet and sign body-surrender affidavits—voluntarily—just to obtain a single Water Voucher."

Nathaniel had created credit from nothing and was engineering mass default through impossible KPIs. The hundred billionaires were destined to become biological batteries—hauling firewood until their hearts stopped—just to maintain Nathaniel's infrastructure.

"A flawless design," David replied dryly.

But David's gaze had drifted past Nathaniel's tent, fixing on the back of Julian "Jules" Cross, who was assembling something amid bizarre mechanical sounds.

A perfect system is always brought down by the most absurd bug imaginable. While everyone cowered under the terror of debt, the uncontrollable joker—who had finished preparing to upend the forest's entire economy—was beginning to move.
