Credit Creation Collateralized by Thirst

If cold is a torture that gnaws at the body from the skin inward, then thirst is a punishment that desiccates the soul from the deepest viscera outward.

On the third morning on the surface, the sun pierced through ashen clouds, and as the forest's moisture evaporated, a sticky heat blanketed the camp. The lips of the billionaires—who had spent the night shivering sleeplessly in the cold—were cracked like the shell of a tortoise. Through parched throats so dry that saliva had ceased to flow, they drew rough, rasping breaths. Their eyes were fixed on a single object at the center of camp.

The silver-gleaming communal water purification filter, enthroned atop Nathaniel Pierce's supply cart.

Before that machine sat Preston Huntington III, crouching. The first slave—who had signed his body-surrender affidavit the previous night—had been granted half a cup of clean water in exchange for spending the night as Nathaniel's watchdog, guarding the cart with a club. The fourth-generation heir who had once sipped champagne in a New York penthouse was now smeared in blood and mud, clutching a bludgeon, protecting another man's property.

Preston's wretched appearance served as the most powerful visual deterrent imaginable for the remaining ninety-eight billionaires. "Refuse Nathaniel's system, and this is what you become." As that silent pressure reached its peak, Arthur Lindley took his position beside the purifier.

"Attention, everyone. Beginning today, the Survival Contribution system is fully operational."

Arthur unfurled his bark ledger and made the declaration.

"Operating the purification filter to produce one liter of drinking water requires enormous physical effort and pump labor. Therefore, one liter of water is valued at three Survival Contribution points. Those who wish to drink must immediately enter the forest and retrieve firewood equivalent to three points, or clean the camp's waste."

A chorus of despairing groans erupted from the billionaires.

They were already depleted by dysentery and cold. The demand to perform physical labor with axes and shovels without having consumed a single drop of water was physically impossible.

"This is absurd! You have to let us drink first so we have the strength to gather wood!"
"We're dying of thirst right now, and you want us to work first? Are you trying to kill us?"

Just as the fury threatened to erupt into open revolt, Nathaniel Pierce calmed the crowd with an elegant gesture.

"I fully understand your hardship. When a liquidity crisis strikes, if capital cannot be circulated, the market collapses. Isn't what you're experiencing right now a depletion of the capital called 'physical stamina'?"

Nathaniel was as composed as if delivering a presentation in a Wall Street conference room. From his pocket, he produced several dozen small, business-card-sized slips of paper—torn from the premium vellum notebook he had brought from the bunker. At the center of each slip, Nathaniel's initials, "N.P.," were stamped in vivid red ink.

"Therefore, I have decided to assume the role of a central bank and extend credit loans to you all."

Nathaniel fluttered one of the paper slips in the air.

"This is a 'Water Voucher' guaranteeing one liter of future drinking water. For those who currently lack the stamina to perform labor, I will lend you this voucher using your 'future labor capacity' as collateral. Present this voucher, and you may drink from the purifier immediately."

The crowd stirred. At the words "drink immediately," eyes glittering with animal hunger converged on Nathaniel's fingertips.

"However, in accordance with the rules of finance, interest will accrue. Anyone who borrows one voucher today must deliver firewood equivalent to four points before sunset tomorrow. Three points of principal plus one point of interest. A perfectly reasonable rate, wouldn't you say? Refusal or deferral of repayment will result in the same 'bankruptcy proceedings' that Representative Preston experienced last night."

It was insanity.

David Sterling watched the blood-chilling swindle unfold, arms folded.

In sixteenth-century Europe, goldsmiths had issued "receipts" in place of the gold coins deposited with them. People began trading the lightweight receipts as if they were money. The goldsmiths soon realized: "Not everyone comes to withdraw their gold at the same time." They began printing far more counterfeit receipts than the actual gold in their vaults, lending them out at exorbitant interest. That was the origin of the modern banking system's "fractional reserve system"—and the birth of credit creation.

What Nathaniel was doing now was precisely the same.

The purification filter could physically produce, at most, thirty liters of water per day. But the paper slips in Nathaniel's hands numbered well over a hundred. Nathaniel was converting imaginary future water into present value, printing infinite currency from thin air. The moment those vouchers flooded the camp, this narrow stretch of forest would become Nathaniel's perfectly controlled empire of inflation and debt.

"Those scraps of paper are worthless! You're just telling us to work double tomorrow!"

One of the former hedge fund managers protested roughly.

But as David had predicted, the majority reacted differently. For men who had spent their entire lives accumulating wealth not through physical labor but through numbers on ledgers, the "credit transaction"—deferring present pain by pushing debt into the future—was an irresistibly sweet and familiar narcotic.

"Fine. Lend me one. I'm dying of thirst. I'll get the wood tomorrow."

The first to step forward was Cohen, chairman of the resort chain. He pressed his thumbprint onto Arthur's ledger and received a single Water Voucher. Cohen presented the voucher to Preston at the purifier, and Preston—obeying his master's command like a trained dog—poured half a cup of water and presented it to Cohen.

When the others saw this, their reason finally snapped.

"Me too! Give me two!"
"I'll take three! I need to pay Dennis his campfire seat fee tonight!"

Pandemonium. The billionaires swarmed around Nathaniel and Arthur, begging for slips of paper. They ignored the fact that what they were signing was a slave contract mandating fourteen-plus hours of grueling physical labor per day. "Tomorrow-me will somehow pay off the debt." That ruinous optimism consumed the camp.

What happened an hour later was even more grotesque.

"Hey! I'll give you my Swiss Patek Philippe watch—hand over one of those Water Vouchers you borrowed!"

Someone shouted from a corner of the camp. David turned to see a former oil tycoon prying a hundred-thousand-dollar limited-edition watch from his wrist and thrusting it at a venture capitalist. The venture capitalist snorted and waved his paper Water Voucher.

"Are you joking? The world's over—what good is a lump of metal? You'll need to offer two. Actually, factoring in tomorrow's interest, throw in the diamond cufflinks."

Unbelievable. A slip of paper with zero intrinsic value—the Water Voucher—had begun to reign as the reserve currency, buying luxury goods that had once commanded hundreds of thousands of dollars at fire-sale prices. The billionaires sold cashmere coats, gold rings, and premium whiskey to acquire Nathaniel's vouchers, conducting transactions that resembled a frenzied speculative exchange. They were not hoarding water. They were hoarding the right to buy water—and finding deranged comfort in that distinction.

David watched the frenzy and felt a chill of genuine horror.

The conditioning Nathaniel had quietly conducted over ten years—the virtual chip gambling ring inside the bunker's ten-square-meter cells—was now bearing perfect fruit. These fools felt zero resistance to treating paper scraps as currency. Without a single act of violence, armed with nothing more than the cautionary spectacle of Preston and a few torn strips of vellum, Nathaniel had seized absolute control of a hundred lives.

Nathaniel leaned against the cart, surveying his grotesque flea market with serene satisfaction. David approached him.

"You've completely ignored the reserve requirement ratio," David whispered. "What happens when they all show up at once with vouchers demanding water? The purifier's processing capacity won't come close."

Nathaniel showed no alarm whatsoever. He mimed clinking a wine glass in David's direction.

"Worrying about a bank run, Mr. Actuary? But people never come to withdraw all at once. Why? Because now that those scraps of paper have begun to function as 'currency,' they'll become addicted to the pleasure of collecting the vouchers themselves—drinking only the bare minimum of actual water to survive."

A deep darkness pooled in Nathaniel's eyes.

"Do you know the greatest magic trick of capitalism? If you give a starving man not bread but 'stock that can buy bread,' he will gladly starve to death while praying for the stock price to rise. They will fall in love with my vouchers."

David could not rebut that arrogant declaration. In a far corner of the camp, a man who held three Water Vouchers was indeed gasping with dehydration—too miserly to redeem them for actual water.

This miniature capitalist society, sculpted from mud and sewage, had spawned the monster of credit creation in a single day. And tomorrow at dawn, the truly blood-soaked tragedy of billionaires driven into beast-infested forests with axes—compelled to repay their interest—was gestating in the darkness.
