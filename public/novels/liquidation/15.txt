The Colosseum Called Market Competition

When the morning sun rose, the most expensive ashes in human history scattered on the wind.

Overnight, to stave off hypothermia, the hundred billionaires had fed into the campfire their old-world stock certificates, real estate title deeds, and the countless counterfeit Water Vouchers. All of it had become soft, white ash rolling across the muddy ground. The bitter smell of burned paper—once worth tens of billions—mingled with the dank morning fog of the forest.

The survivors who had endured the night crouched like animals around the fire were a ghastly sight. Faces coated in soot, hazmat suits torn to ribbons exposing bare skin. Worst of all was the thirst. Since the purifier had shut down during yesterday's bank run, not a single drop of water had passed their lips. Their cracked mouths oozed blood. The moment they opened their eyes, their gazes locked instinctively on the dead purifier at camp center—its pressure pump valve ripped out, reduced to useless scrap.

Standing before it, face immaculate and untouched, was Nathaniel Pierce. In one hand he held a personal titanium canteen brimming with clear water. In the other, the critical valve he had extracted the day before.

"The credit economy has failed."

Nathaniel took a languid sip from his canteen and spoke in a low voice. The Adam's apples of the prostrate billionaires convulsed involuntarily.

"Illegal quantitative easing has destroyed monetary trust. Thanks to those who broke the rules, you are all bankrupt. But capitalism always finds a more efficient answer within crisis, does it not? Beginning today, we return to the most primal and intuitive rule of all: the Perfect Competition Market."

Nathaniel raised the valve and pointed toward the forest's edge.

"The boar attack made it painfully clear: this camp needs defensive walls. Go into the forest. Cut the heaviest, strongest logs you can find and build a barricade around the camp."

"Damn it… we told you yesterday, we don't have the strength! Give us water first, then we'll cut wood!"

A former media tycoon rasped through his cracked voice.

Nathaniel shook his head with elegant finality.

"There will be no credit extended today. Only results guarantee water. Listen carefully: my purifier can produce a limited quantity per day. Therefore, only the top ten individuals who deliver the largest, heaviest logs before sunset will receive one liter of water each. The remaining ninety will spend tonight without water, exposed on bare ground, as targets for the beasts."

The air in the forest solidified.

Not absolute evaluation—relative evaluation. A perfect survival-of-the-fittest colosseum, where only the top 10 percent of predators survived and the bottom 90 percent were culled.

David Sterling sat on a fallen tree stump, listening to Nathaniel's brutal presentation. Behind his eyes flashed the blood-red ledger of the "stack ranking" evaluations conducted every year-end at the mega-financial group where he had once worked.

In those days, corporations had adopted a forced-curve system that mandated the firing of the bottom 10 percent regardless of performance. Executives had packaged it as "maintaining organizational tension through healthy market competition." But the moment the system was implemented, the company had gone insane. Instead of cooperating for the firm's collective benefit, employees sabotaged colleagues' projects, deleted data, and drove knives into each other's backs. In a structure where you had to trample the person at the next desk or face termination yourself, human beings willingly became monsters.

David had calculated the industrial accident insurance surcharges for the survivors of that ruthless competition, under executive orders. "The most efficient market is completed when it burns the blood of its participants as fuel." That had been the chilling statistical truth David derived.

And now, Nathaniel was transplanting that blood-soaked stack ranking directly into this wild forest.

"Top ten… only ten…"

The instant someone muttered those words, as if a spell had broken, all hundred billionaires launched themselves to their feet simultaneously.

"Axe! Where's my axe?"
"Move! I grabbed that saw first!"

Pandemonium. The equipment brought from the bunker was woefully insufficient. The billionaires clawed at each other's hair and threw punches, fighting like animals over the remaining axes and saws.

Richard—the former private equity executive with the broken wrist—reached for a rusted saw with his one good hand and took a boot to the abdomen from the IT startup founder. The startup founder stepped over Richard's blood, seized the saw, and sprinted into the forest. Those who failed to secure tools snatched up sharp stones and raced into the darkness.

Thirst and the terror of death were a potent narcotic that erased the body's physical limits.

Hours later, the sounds echoing through the forest were not falling timber but human beings cursing and screaming at one another.

A former oil tycoon and a Silicon Valley investor were locked in a blood-soaked tug-of-war over a single massive oak trunk.

"I felled this tree first! Let go!"
"Bullshit! You dropped it and I'm hauling it! Ownership belongs to the transporter!"

There was no logic, no reason. The oil tycoon slammed his fist into the investor's face. The investor sank his teeth into the oil tycoon's exposed flesh through a tear in his hazmat suit. In the grotesque melee of blood and torn skin, they were becoming monsters—driven by a single imperative: to land in the top ten and drink a mouthful of water.

At the center of this colosseum, the most overwhelming violence was being dealt by none other than Preston Huntington III.

Having tasted blood when he shattered Richard's wrist the day before, Preston was fully awakened. He did not chop a single tree himself. Instead, he shouldered his thick club, planted himself at a forest chokepoint, and blocked the path.

"Pay the toll."

Preston jutted his chin imperiously at Cohen, the resort magnate, who was struggling to drag a log back to camp.

"Wh—what? A toll? This is just forest—what toll?"

"This trail connects to the infrastructure road of my former latrine—my private property. Leave half that log you're dragging and move along. Otherwise, I'll break your legs."

The intellectual property suits and patent litigation of Wall Street had evolved, in this wilderness, into the brute reality of a club. Cohen tried to resist, but when Preston swung the club through the air in violent threat, Cohen had no choice but to saw his log in half and lay the tribute at Preston's feet.

Without performing a single stroke of labor, Preston amassed the largest woodpile by plundering others. He was proving, with his own body, that monopoly capitalism—when stripped of law and contract—completes itself in its most primal form: naked violence.

By sundown, the bloodied billionaires dragged their logs to camp center. Their palms were torn to the bone. Their bodies were mottled purple from each other's fists. Yet their eyes burned with a grotesque need to confirm whether their log was bigger than the next man's.

Nathaniel and Arthur took up torches and began the ranking.

Logs were laid at Arthur's feet in sequence, measured by size and weight, and ranked from first place downward.

"First place: Preston, who secured an overwhelming quantity through toll collection. Second… third…"

Those whose names were called wept tears of transcendent joy. Those who fell below the cutoff collapsed in animal howls of despair.

At last, Arthur announced the tenth survivor.

"Tenth place: Steven, who delivered an intact oak post."

"YES! I'm alive! I'm in the top ten!"

Steven threw himself to the ground and kissed the mud.

At that moment, the former airline CEO—ranked eleventh—charged at Steven with an axe, eyes rolled back in his skull.

"I reject this! The inside of that log he brought back was rotten! He faked the weight! I should be tenth!"

The airline CEO brought the flat of the axe down on the back of Steven's skull with savage force. Steven screamed and crumpled. The CEO then kicked Steven's oak post, sending it rolling out of the camp boundary.

"See?! His results are voided! I'm tenth now! Give me my water!"

An axe-wielding, blood-spattered CEO, panting like a beast. Before that grotesque display of violence, no one condemned him. Even Nathaniel nodded with a satisfied smile curling at his lips.

"Eliminating a competitor is also an excellent market strategy. Tenth place—ranking adjustment approved."

David watched the monstrous ranking ceremony and rose quietly to his feet.

In the colosseum Nathaniel had designed, there was no "production." The billionaires had not cooperated to build defensive walls for the camp. Instead, to secure a single mouthful of water, they had attacked and plundered one another, destroying whatever remained of their colleagues' physical reserves and sanity.

Where the sacred label of "market economy" had peeled away, only a perfect savagery remained—humans tearing at each other's flesh. Tomorrow, the competition for the top ten would grow even more vicious, and the forest's true predators, drawn by the scent of blood, would begin to approach this hellscape of a camp. A cold wind swept through the ruins.
