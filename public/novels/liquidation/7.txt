7
Seed Investment to Dig a Latrine

When dawn broke, the billionaires who had spent the night huddled around Dennis's campfire were confronted by yet another brutal physiological crisis. The aftermath of the collective dysentery triggered by contaminated stream water the day before was far from over. At the first ominous rumbling from their bowels, the survivors instinctively scrambled toward the camp's outskirts.

Their destination was the three-by-three-meter "latrine pit" that Preston Huntington III had excavated the previous afternoon with frostbitten hands, in exchange for his water ration credits.

"Move! I'm about to explode!"

Cohen, the elderly resort magnate, clutched his waistband and sprinted toward the pit—only to find a mud-caked, hideous figure blocking his path with a shovel held crosswise. Preston.

"Where do you think you're going? This is private property."

Preston's eyes glittered with sleeplessness and a feral madness. His fifty-thousand-dollar bespoke hazmat suit was so filthy it was unrecognizable, but his upturned chin retained every ounce of the Huntington dynasty's fourth-generation arrogance—the same look he had worn gazing down at the world from his penthouse in New York.

"Have you lost your mind? You're gatekeeping a toilet?"

"Did you forget Nathaniel's notarization? This pit was built with my labor as collateral for a seed investment. It's a 'sanitation infrastructure plant.' Trespassing on this facility—into which I poured my sweat and blood—constitutes a clear violation of property rights. If you wish to use it, pay the appropriate usage fee."

Cohen's face contorted in disbelief. Preston calling a latrine a "plant" was absurd enough, but what made it truly horrifying was the sight of Nathaniel Pierce and Arthur Lindley approaching from behind Preston, smiling warmly.

Arthur Lindley—former Chief Human Resources Officer of a Fortune 10 global corporation—held a strip of bark densely inscribed with charcoal.

"Preston is correct. Since intellectual property rights over fire were recognized last night, property rights over infrastructure must be equally protected."

Arthur cleared his throat and raised the bark strip.

"I have just completed a 'Survival Contribution' system integrating Nathaniel's water resources, Dennis's energy, and Preston's sanitation infrastructure under unified management. From this moment forward, each of you must earn 'points' through labor—collecting firewood or conducting reconnaissance. The fee for one use of Preston's facility has been set at… 0.5 points."

An icy stillness fell over the forest. The billionaires gaped at one another in mute disbelief.

Just three days ago, they had held billions in Swiss bank accounts. Now, unless they performed menial physical labor—scavenging firewood from the wilderness—they could not so much as relieve themselves. They had been reduced, with perfect completeness, to the lowest tier of servitude.

"This is insane! I refuse! I'll just go squat in the bushes over there!"

Cohen screamed and wheeled to run. In that instant, Preston drove his shovel into the earth with a bestial roar.

"Anyone who dumps waste on land outside my infrastructure will be considered a terrorist threatening this camp's sanitary environment! I will file a claim for environmental damages—consider yourselves warned!"

What a blood-curdling farce of capitalism. David Sterling sat on a toppled log at a safe distance, watching the spectacle unfold, and let out a dry, soundless laugh.

Twelve years ago, in a developing nation in South America, a massive privatization project overlapped in David's memory with this appalling comedy.

At the time, global venture capital firms and hedge funds had purchased the nation's water and sewage system—its most critical infrastructure—at fire-sale prices, as a condition of providing bailout loans to the bankrupt government. The moment they secured the operating rights, the capitalists hiked water rates by 400 percent. In the slums, people collected rainwater to drink. Unable to pay sewage processing fees, they poured waste into the streets. That summer, a cholera epidemic killed thousands of children.

David had sat in his premium penthouse office in New York, cross-referencing the mortality rate from cholera against the record-breaking dividend yields the fund had earned from the privatized water system, cell by cell on his Excel sheet. The capitalists were monsters who could privatize even the most basic human rights—to defecate, to survive—and reduce them to figures on a ledger.

And now, on the mud-soaked surface of a dead planet, those same monsters were inflicting exactly the same atrocity upon each other. The only difference was that the victims were no longer powerless slum-dwellers, but billionaires themselves.

"Fine! Half a point! I'll bring firewood later—just get out of my way!"

Cohen, unable to withstand the agony of his gut any longer, capitulated. Arthur recorded Cohen's debt on his bark ledger, and Preston—like a sentry guarding a great fortress gate—magnanimously stepped aside with his shovel and opened the way.

That broke the dam. One by one, the billionaires queued up and took on debt. The grotesque spectacle of men mortgaging their future physical labor in exchange for the right to empty their bowels. They did not see the collar labeled "Survival Contribution Score" for what it truly was—an elaborate trap of exploitation set by Nathaniel and Arthur—and walked willingly into its embrace.

An hour later, the absurdity reached its pinnacle.

The volcanic demand for Preston's "infrastructure" sent his vanity into grotesque, metastatic growth. He dragged planks to the edge of the latrine pit, puffed out his chest, and bellowed:

"The value of my infrastructure is skyrocketing! I intend to conduct a Series A funding round to expand this sanitation plant! Anyone who invests one water tube in me today will receive a complimentary usage pass and equity dividends going forward!"

Then the unthinkable happened. Several venture capitalists—the very men who had been stripped of equity by Dennis's campfire licensing scheme the night before—gathered around Preston's pit and began earnestly discussing its business viability.

"Not a bad proposition. It's the only monopoly operation in this camp. I'll put up my designer watch as collateral—give me 10 percent."

"I'll trade you my cashmere coat for 15 percent!"

A frenzy of derivative products and venture investment, all centered on a single latrine. In the reeking mud, they were trading fictional shares and equity stakes in a property that did not exist, their adrenal glands pumping with the old euphoria. The terror of civilizational collapse had evaporated, replaced entirely by the ancient pleasure of owning and trading.

David turned his back on the lunatic marketplace.

If the bunker's ten years of sterile confinement had turned their bodies into hothouse flowers, then a lifetime of capitalist greed had turned their souls into zombies. David's gaze drifted upward to the cold sun piercing through ashen clouds.

The wild shows no mercy, and nature will not be deceived by imaginary numbers. That the pit Preston had dug was not merely a toilet but the beginning of a grave that would swallow them all—that the "credit notes" Nathaniel had conjured were nothing but scraps of paper waiting to be torn apart—none of them could yet begin to imagine.

David pressed his pen firmly against the cooling page of his diary.

"Humanity's last stock exchange has opened for trading. The listed commodity is other people's excrement."
