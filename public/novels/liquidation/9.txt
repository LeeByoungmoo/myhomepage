The Board's Elegant and Grotesque Majority Vote

In the calm of early evening, amid the ruins, there lay an open clearing where colossal slabs of concrete had toppled into a rough geometric circle. Around this ashen roundtable—once the lobby floor of some glittering skyscraper—the hundred billionaires gathered one by one.

They were a wretched sight. Hazmat suits that had gleamed pristine white just days ago were now stained with mud, vomit, and animal blood, reduced to little more than rags. Yet they arranged themselves around the circle with the careful precision of board directors filing into a mahogany conference room on Wall Street, each claiming a seat according to their "asset size" and "rank."

At the head position—the flattest, highest boulder—stood Arthur Lindley, former Chief Human Resources Officer of a global Fortune 10 corporation, and beside him, Nathaniel Pierce, the architect of this entire game.

"We have a quorum. I hereby call to order the First Emergency Shareholders' Meeting and Board Session."

Arthur cleared his throat and declared the proceedings open with solemn gravity. A board meeting conducted against the backdrop of unidentified animal cries emanating from the dark forest. David Sterling, leaning against a tree stump at a safe distance, let out a cold, silent laugh at this grotesque linguistic performance.

"As you are all aware, our camp's resources are critically limited. The drinking water generously provided by Representative Nathaniel is running low. Firewood to maintain Dennis's campfire is woefully insufficient. And we require a night watch detail to guard against further boar attacks."

Arthur tapped the bark strip in his hand for emphasis.

"Therefore, I propose a rational, transparent set of rules to which all members can agree, for the sustainable survival of this community. The title: the 'Survival Contribution Special Act.'"

Arthur's presentation was, on its surface, impeccably rational and democratic.

All physical labor would be converted into "points" based on risk and difficulty. Camp sanitation: one point. Water transport: three points. And the high-risk assignments—venturing deep into beast-infested forest to gather firewood, or standing night watch—were valued at ten points. These earned points could then be used to purchase Nathaniel's water and Dennis's fire.

"I have a question."

One of the venture capitalists raised his hand.

"The theory is elegant, but who exactly performs those ten-point high-risk assignments? One wrong step and a boar guts you. No sane person would do it for ten points—or even a hundred."

It was a precise objection. No matter how generous the reward, if voluntary labor could not be generated, the market would not function. As if he had been waiting for exactly this moment, a cold light flickered behind Arthur's spectacles.

"An excellent question. In a market economy, when supply is insufficient, 'mandatory allocation' must be employed to maintain infrastructure. To that end, I have drafted Article 4, Section 2 of the Special Act: 'Any individual whose current debt ratio exceeds their assessed asset value—a state of complete capital impairment—shall be mandatorily assigned to high-risk duties for the survival of the community.'"

A chilling silence descended on the roundtable.

David's eyes darted rapidly, updating the ledger in his mind. Complete capital impairment. The individual who met that qualification in this camp numbered exactly one.

Every gaze swiveled simultaneously to a single point. There, at the outermost edge of the circle—the man who had squandered even the water invested in his latrine infrastructure on Dennis's campfire seat fees, and now sat buried in debt. Preston Huntington III.

"Wh—what? Why is everyone looking at me?!"

Preston screamed, the blood draining from his face.

"Representative Preston. Regrettably, you are currently the camp's sole credit-delinquent debtor."

Arthur's voice was devoid of mercy.

"The community has extended you loans of water and warmth. It is now your turn to repay the interest through physical labor. Tonight's firewood collection and overnight perimeter patrol in the forest exterior have been assigned to you."

"You're insane! Did you not see those boars yesterday?! Sending me into that forest at night is telling me to become animal feed! I am a Huntington—"

"Preston."

Nathaniel, who had been silent, spoke in a voice so smooth and elegant it raised gooseflesh.

"There is no Huntington dynasty here. Only contracts and ledgers. If you refuse this obligation, we will have no choice but to suspend all water and fire provisions to you, effective immediately. This is not violence. It is a demand for the fulfillment of a contract to which you yourself consented."

Preston's face crumpled with terror and despair. He gasped for breath and turned pleading eyes to the billionaires around him—men who, mere years ago, had groveled before him, begging for invitations to his charity galas.

"Cohen! You used my latrine yesterday! Dennis! Do you know how much I paid for your fire? Somebody say something! They're trying to kill me!"

Preston's wailing echoed through the ruins, but the only answer was the cold averted gaze of every billionaire present.

David understood exactly what their silence meant. The billionaires did not pity Preston. They were intoxicated with the overwhelming relief that they would not have to perform the terrifying nighttime forest labor themselves. As long as a legitimate sacrificial lamb named Preston existed, their own safety was guaranteed. The deceptive rules Nathaniel and Arthur had crafted exploited that petty selfishness with surgical precision.

"Then let us proceed to a vote."

Arthur raised his hand high, solidifying the atmosphere.

"All those in favor of adopting the Survival Contribution Special Act and the mandatory labor assignment for debtors, please raise your hands."

Across the darkened roundtable of ruins, gaunt hands rose one by one. No one shouted. No one resorted to violence. The billionaires raised their hands with the same cultivated, elegant composure they might have displayed approving a charitable donation at a foundation gala.

The majority threshold was crossed in moments. A death sentence on Preston's life had been passed—legally, legitimately—through a flawless democratic majority vote.

Then—

"Wait, wait, wait! You can't end a vote this boringly."

Julian "Jules" Cross, who had been fiddling with the severed head of a robot dog just outside the circle, abruptly interjected. Arthur's eyebrow twitched at Jules's appearance. Among the five key figures, Jules—the unpredictable lunatic—was always the most dangerous variable.

"Hey, Arthur. Majority votes are only fun when they're close. What if I dumped all my remaining assets into poor Preston over there and cleared his debt? Then he wouldn't be a debtor anymore, so he wouldn't have to go into the forest, right?"

For an instant, a miraculous spark of hope flickered in Preston's dull eyes.

"Jules…! Oh God, Jules! Yes, if you pay off my debt, I'll give you the gold bars I left in the bunker—"

Preston crawled through the dirt toward Jules. But Jules gazed down at Preston's desperate face for a long moment, then erupted into a grotesque peal of laughter.

"BAHAHAHA! I'm kidding, you moron! Who in this world would spend money on you?"

Jules hurled the robot parts to the ground and threw both arms skyward in mock celebration.

"I vote one hundred percent in favor! Enthusiastically! A fourth-generation real estate heir chased by boars in the middle of the night, soiling his pants as he runs—that's premium content worth a hundred-million-dollar Netflix exclusive deal! Give the man a club and chase him into the woods already!"

Jules's psychopathic, sadistic declaration of assent rang across the ruins. Preston's legs gave out completely. He collapsed to the ground, his lips quivering violently, and began to sob like a wounded animal.

David picked up his pen and silently recorded the day's board meeting in his diary.

In the old world, every time a major corporation laid off thousands of workers, they used the elegant phrase "restructuring for management rationalization." A refined murder that severed countless livelihoods through paperwork and ballots alone, without getting a single drop of blood on anyone's hands.

The first majority vote conducted in this camp was exactly the same. To keep their own hands free of dirt and blood, they had hidden behind the most civilized shield available: the vote. And that violence—dressed in the mask of reason—had legally pushed a man into the gaping maw of pitch-black darkness and prowling beasts.

"The resolution has passed. Preston, if you do not return with firewood, you will receive no rations tomorrow."

At Arthur's dry adjournment, two men seized Preston roughly by both arms and hauled him to his feet. They pressed a single jagged shard of concrete into his hands, then shoved him without mercy into the dense darkness of the forest.

Beyond the tree line, where the canopy choked out every last photon of light, lay perfect, absolute blackness.

Watching Preston's stumbling silhouette vanish into the dark, David closed his eyes. The foolish majority who believed they had created the rules for their own survival did not yet see it: Nathaniel's invisible noose was already cinching tight around every one of their necks. The new world's first "restructuring"—grotesque and merciless—was complete.
