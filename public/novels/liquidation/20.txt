Miniature Capitalism, Ruled by False Gods

The column of ultra-pure water erupting from the ruined purifier stood like a transparent tombstone planted at the center of the annihilated world.

The mud pit had transformed into a vast oasis in an instant. Billionaires who, only days before, had flicked the rims of vintage wine glasses and debated appellations now lay prone on a floor of mud laced with filth, bellies distended like frogs, gulping water.

"Ha ha ha! Water! We don't have to pay back our debts!"

At someone's deranged cheer, the others erupted in grotesque laughter and flung mud at one another. They were savoring freedom atop a thoroughly collapsed system. The virtual currency of "water vouchers" that Nathaniel Pierce had conjured was now worthless confetti before the brute physics of water pressure, and the blood-soaked charter Arthur Lindley had drafted had devolved into crude scribbling—rendered impotent the instant it could no longer leverage water as collateral.

David Sterling stepped back from the mud-pit bacchanal and studied Nathaniel, who knelt beside the wrecked purifier.

The face of the once-elegant, once-imperious hedge-fund manager was a ruin. In Nathaniel's hand he still clutched the leather diary in which every debt and every instrument of power had been recorded—but the ledger he gripped was now nothing more than a graveyard of dead type that no one would ever honor again.

Into David's mind, a memory from ten years ago superimposed itself with chilling clarity—events inside "Eden," the bunker's virtual-reality network.

Back then, Nathaniel had reigned as a perfect god within Eden, printing limitless virtual chips at will. One day a critical bug had struck the system, randomly duplicating billions of chips into every resident's account. Nathaniel had screamed at the virtual sky like a madman: *"This is MY world! If they aren't MY chips, they have no value!"*

But no one obeyed his control. The moment chips became commonplace, people stopped bowing to Nathaniel. He had locked himself in his private cell for days, refusing food and water. What he could not endure was not the loss of power itself—it was the collapse of the perfect religious ego that told him he was the god who set the rules.

The CEOs of the massive financial groups David had served during the age of civilization had been no different. Even as their stock prices cratered and their companies were delisted, even as the FBI kicked down the doors of their penthouses, they clung to the glorious charts of bygone days on their monitors and denied reality. Capitalists are a deformed species—they do not inhabit physical reality, the realm of flesh, but dwell instead in the virtual world of credit that lives upon a ledger.

"Nathaniel."
David called out, his voice low.

Nathaniel slowly lifted his soaked head and looked at David.
"This is a riot, David. A crude, primitive riot, nothing more. By tomorrow morning, the motor on that machine will burn out from overload, and the severe drought will return. When it does, these swine will come crawling back beneath my ledger—"

"No. Tomorrow morning will never come."

David's glacial pronouncement made Nathaniel's pupils tremble.
At that precise moment, Chloe Bennett, standing at David's side, seized his arm and pointed into the darkness.

"David! There!"

Drowned out by the roar of the gushing water column and the billionaires' crazed cheering, the shadows advancing from the tree line had already reached the camp's doorstep.

Shhhhk—TSSSSSSS!

The first thing to materialize from the pitch-black fog was a plasma-cutter spear, spitting blue lightning. Then the silhouettes of the neo-humans rose like specters in the torchlight—towering far past two meters, bodies encased in grotesque patchwork armor of scrap metal and raw animal hide.

The laughter of the billionaires wallowing in the pool was severed as if by a blade.

"Wh—what is that."
"Is that a rescue team? There were other survivors out here?!"

A handful of the billionaires, in their staggering stupidity, gasped with something close to relief. After ten years sealed inside a bunker, cut off from civilization, they could not begin to imagine how swiftly and savagely radiation and the violence of the wild had evolved the human beings left outside.

They seemed to regard the approaching neo-humans as something like "primitive natives" or "lower-class laborers" from the old world. Dennis Chow—the Silicon Valley founder who had once descended into madness claiming a patent on fire—brushed mud from his wet hazmat suit and rose to his feet.

"Everyone calm down. They're obviously savages scrounging scrap metal in this forest. I don't know if they speak English, but let's try to negotiate."

Dennis strode toward the neo-human pack with the swagger of Steve Jobs walking onstage for an investor-relations keynote. David did not even have time to stop him.

"Hey! Can you understand me?"
Dennis spread his arms wide and planted himself in front of the towering figure at the head of the neo-human column. The creature's face was hidden behind a filthy retrofitted gas mask; only its breathing filled the forest—a mechanical, rasping friction.

"We are the owners of this camp. From what I can see, you've got some pretty useful gear there. That plasma cutter—I have no idea where the IP for that tech originated, but let's make a deal."

Dennis fumbled from his coat a crumpled sheet of paper—a water voucher—and a solid-gold lighter he had smuggled out of the bunker, waving them before the creature.
"We'll ration you clean water from our purifier—it puts out an unlimited supply. In return, you use that firepower of yours to escort us from the beasts in this forest. A security subcontracting agreement—"

It was the most horrifying and ignorant arrogance that a man standing at the pinnacle of capitalism could muster. He was attempting to collar the apex predator of the food chain with a leash woven from numbers on a ledger.

The negotiation ended with a perfect, overwhelming demonstration of physical force.

Before Dennis finished speaking, the neo-human swung the plasma spear from his shoulder in a lazy, viper-quick arc.

THWOCK—ZZZZZT!

There was no time even to scream. The blue-sparking blade punched through Dennis's chest like paper, erupting from his back. The ultra-high-temperature heat instantly cauterized the wound, and the nauseating stench of seared flesh diffused through the camp's wet air.

"Gk—ugh…"
Blood-flecked foam seeped from Dennis's lips. His eyes were blown wide—not with pain but with sheer, disbelieving bewilderment; he had not yet registered that he had been impaled. The solid-gold lighter slipped from his fingers and sank uselessly into the mud.

The neo-human lifted the spear—Dennis's full weight still skewered upon it—as casually as one might hoist a scarecrow, and hurled him toward the pool at the center of camp like a sack of cargo.

SPLAT!

The instant Dennis's corpse hit the water, the frozen billionaires finally erupted in ear-splitting screams.

"AAAAAAGH! He killed him! He killed a man!"
"Help! Somebody help us!"

The greatest delusion of the capitalist class is the belief that the wealth they hold guarantees the absolute safety of their flesh. But in this perfect wilderness—devoid of law, police, or hired muscle—the value of their lives was lighter than the thin mist of blood that had sprayed from Dennis's back.

The neo-humans did not so much as blink at the billionaires' screaming. To them, the noise was no different from the squealing of pigs penned in a slaughterhouse. The lead figure flicked his hand, and the dozen neo-humans arrayed behind him drew steel cables fitted with lasso loops and hooks fashioned from animal bone, then advanced into the camp without mercy.

"Run! Into the forest!"

At someone's cry, the billionaires scattered like spiders. But days of forced labor, dysentery, and the grotesque derivative-trading frenzy had drained every last reserve of their stamina. Men collapsed under their own weight mid-stride; others stepped into animal traps and snapped their ankles.

The neo-humans flung steel lassoes at the fleeing backs without pity.

FWIP—SNAP!

"GAAAAAH!"
A former airline CEO, lassoed around the neck, was dragged brutally across the ground. Another neo-human shattered a resisting venture capitalist's kneecap with a saw-toothed club, then hooked the unconscious man's ankle and hauled him away like game.

It was not a battle. It was a one-sided harvest.

David, pulled by Chloe's hand, concealed himself deep in a thicket of dense ferns at the camp's perimeter. Through the gaps between fronds, he watched this grotesque, merciless pandemonium unfold, holding his breath.

In the age of civilization, these men had been gods who ruled the world. With a single click on a ledger they could raze a forest on the far side of the globe, fire tens of thousands of workers, and shake the lives of others like dice. They had believed, without a shred of doubt, that they were the lords of creation.

But now, billionaires clad in fifty-thousand-dollar bespoke hazmat suits were being captured at the neo-humans' feet—literally rounded up like livestock. Preston, clutching his broken ribs, was licking a neo-human's combat boot and begging for his life. Arthur Lindley, architect of the blood-soaked charter, was trussed in a lasso and being dragged across the mud floor.

And one man watched this spectacle with the most grotesque expression of all. Nathaniel Pierce.

Nathaniel stood frozen beside the destroyed purifier. A neo-human approached and kicked him in the shoulder, driving him to his knees, but Nathaniel did not resist. His eyes oscillated between Dennis's corpse and the billionaires being ruthlessly dragged away, trembling with a deformity that went beyond shock.

*My market… The perfect empire I built…*

Strength drained from Nathaniel's hand. The leather diary he had clutched as though it were his life slipped from his fingers and dropped into the mud. That ledger—inscribed with Preston's personal-collateral waiver and the debt records of countless derivatives—was swept up by the purifier's raging water pressure. The ink washed away, and the diary was trampled into the filthy muck until it lost all form.

It was the perfect moment of physical and permanent liquidation for Nathaniel Pierce's miniature capitalism.

WHIIIIIRR—BANG!

As if detonating a final firework, the motor Jules had jury-rigged inside the purifier reached its breaking point. It belched black smoke with a deafening roar and exploded into ruin.

The gushing column of water cut off as if it had never existed, and the camp was transformed into a hellscape filled with spreading black smoke and bestial screams.

David closed his eyes quietly in the darkness.
The age of ledger games ruled by false gods was over. Where the thin shell of civilization had been stripped away, only blood, torn flesh, and the absolute violence of the food chain remained. In the depths of the forest where the captured billionaires were being dragged—alive—an abyss of true wilderness waited, its maw gaping wide. Compared to it, the hell of capitalism would seem a quaint memory.
