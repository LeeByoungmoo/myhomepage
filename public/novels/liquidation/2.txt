The Penthouse Floor's Hypothermia Victims

The sun, as merciless after the apocalypse as before, plunged beyond the horizon, and the warmth that had heated the air evaporated as if it had never existed. Between the skeletal remains of the concrete forest, a wind sharp as a blade began to howl. The temperature crashed so rapidly that each exhaled breath crystallized white in the air.

"Set up tents now! Are you trying to freeze to death?"

At someone's frantic cry, the hundred billionaires began unpacking the single-person survival tent kits distributed from the bunker. Any rational human being would have sought shelter from the cutting wind—a concave depression beneath a collapsed building, perhaps, or a low-lying area where massive interlocking tree roots formed a natural windbreak. But these were not rational human beings. These were the apex predators of capitalism, men who had spent their entire lives proving their worth through the "height" from which they looked down upon others.

David Sterling silently shouldered his tent bag and walked toward a crater ringed by collapsed building slabs that stood like a fortress wall. His line of sight was completely blocked. The floor was carpeted with rotting moss that gave off a musty odor, but the massive concrete masses on every side served as a perfect windbreak—an optimal position.

As he tamped down the crater floor, sharp voices erupted from a ledge roughly thirty meters above his head. It was a cliff formed where a former high-rise had been sheared in half—an exposed precipice commanding an unobstructed panoramic view.

"Back off, Cohen! My asset valuation inside the bunker was triple yours. I have every right to this spot!"

Preston Huntington III, the fourth-generation real estate scion, was red-faced and apoplectic.

"Give me a break, Preston! Those precious New York buildings your family owned were all underwater ten years ago. Your account has nothing but dust!"

Cohen, the elderly former owner of a major West Coast resort chain, lunged at Preston as if to seize him by the collar. The two men were snarling like animals over the so-called "penthouse zone"—the narrow strip at the cliff's edge with the highest elevation and the finest view. Acres of flat, open ground stretched in every direction, perfectly suitable for pitching tents. Yet they were fighting tooth and nail over that single cramped patch of high ground, asserting imaginary property rights over a ruined world.

"Gentlemen, please. Let's all calm down."

Nathaniel Pierce materialized between them with his characteristically smooth, unhurried gait. Standing on the dusty cliff edge, he adopted the air of a mediator brokering a deal at an upscale Wall Street restaurant.

"Even if the world has changed, order must be preserved. Preston, by your reasoning—if we go by the bunker's internal asset valuations—priority for this zone does fall to you."

Preston's lip curled in triumphant satisfaction. But Nathaniel immediately turned to Cohen.

"However, Cohen, I'm aware that among the personal effects you brought from the bunker, you have three bottles of antibiotics. What if Preston takes the penthouse here, and in exchange, you receive priority in any future 'dispute mediations'? I'll personally guarantee the arrangement."

On a barren ruin where no law applied and no force could be compelled, Nathaniel had conjured the concepts of "equity" and "concession" from thin air. Preston, intoxicated by the sense of victory, nodded eagerly. Cohen, satisfied at having secured a guarantee from Nathaniel—a proxy for power—cleared his throat and withdrew.

Nathaniel picked up a twig and drew a circle on the ground at the cliff's edge where Preston had staked his claim.

"Congratulations, Preston. This is now your territory."

Watching this absurd territorial dispute from the crater below, David let out a dry, hollow laugh.

In his years as a senior catastrophe actuary, David's desk had been buried each year under mountains of insurance claims from wealthy homeowners in Florida and the California coast. Despite living on terrain where hurricanes routinely carved away the shoreline, they had insisted on building glass-walled mansions at the very edge of cliffs—all for the "panoramic ocean view."

David had calculated with precision—to the second decimal point—exactly how vulnerable those mansions were to nature's fury, the exact percentage probability of structural collapse. But the wealthy had gladly paid exorbitant premium surcharges rather than abandon those precarious cliffs. For them, the "view" was never merely about scenery. It was vertical power—the ability to look down upon others—and a vanity altar proving they were exceptional beings.

One stormy night, those multi-billion-dollar mansions had been swept into the sea like sandcastles. As David processed the life insurance payouts for the wealthy who had drowned, he learned a bone-deep lesson: the most expensive invoice that human vanity ever pays is death itself.

And now, ten years later, on this primeval earth where humanity had perished, those foolish beasts were repeating the exact same mistake.

As night deepened, the wind turned savage. David's tent in the low-lying crater barely trembled, sheltered perfectly by the concrete ramparts. He crawled into his thick military sleeping bag and slowly chewed a protein cube from the bunker rations, methodically replenishing his energy. The actuary's instinct—ruthlessly calculating cost against utility—was keeping him alive.

By contrast, the penthouse zone thirty meters above had become a howling hell.

Perched at the cliff's edge with no windbreak whatsoever, it was a perfect target for the night's lashing gales. Preston's premium lightweight tent flapped and strained as if it might tear apart at any moment.

"Damn it! Why is it so cold?"

Preston's shrieking protest rode the wind down to David's crater as a faint echo. Beneath his thousands-of-dollars bespoke Italian hazmat suit, Preston had layered himself in a premium Mongolian cashmere blanket he had packed among his personal effects. But the savage cold of the wild penetrated luxury goods as if they were tissue paper, freezing him to the marrow.

Each time a tent peg was wrenched from the ground by the wind, Preston clung desperately to the poles with frozen hands. For ten years in the bunker's sterile environment, this fourth-generation heir had simply commanded his AI assistant to adjust the temperature. Now, on this savage night, he was rolling in the dirt with a runny nose, fighting to defend his precious "penthouse." The flawless view from his prime real estate showed him nothing but the gaping maw of pitch-black darkness.

The other billionaires fared little better. The higher their chosen ground, the more viciously the night wind and cold punished them, wringing groans and whimpers from their lips. Not one had the courage to swallow their pride and descend to the filthy craters below. To go down would mean surrendering their imaginary "title deeds" and admitting they belonged to a lower class.

As dawn finally broke with the pallid light of a dying star, David climbed from his crater, stretched, and surveyed the scene above.

Two of the billionaires who had staked their tents at the very pinnacle of the cliff—the so-called "royal floor"—had not emerged. Preston, face swollen and blue-black from the cold, was the one who discovered them. Their bodies lay curled in fetal positions inside tents shredded by the wind, frozen stiff. Rigor mortis had already set in, their faces locked in a final grimace of disbelief—as if they could not comprehend that death had come not from a predator's teeth, but from their own obsession with "height."

"They're dead. They froze to death."

Preston's trembling voice cracked as he spoke. For the first time, real fear—not the manufactured anxiety of a stock price dip—was carved into his face. But even through that fear, a darker emotion flickered behind his eyes. 'Two are gone. That makes more supplies for the rest of us.'

The actuary in David had already performed the same cold calculation, but at least he had the decency not to vocalize it.

Nathaniel Pierce stood a few paces away, arms folded, gazing down at the frozen corpses with an expression of serene contemplation.

"Regrettable. But this is precisely how the market self-corrects. Those who fail to adapt to new conditions are naturally eliminated. It's nothing more than that."

Nathaniel's words were delivered without a trace of mourning. They were the eulogy of an economist, not a human being.

David turned away and descended back into his crater. He opened his leather diary—the only personal possession he had brought from the bunker—and with a stub of pencil, he began to write.

Day One on the Surface: Two dead from hypothermia. The cause of death was not the cold, but vanity. The hierarchy of height that sustained their identities in the old world has now become a lethal trap. And yet, the survivors show no signs of learning. They are already counting the dead men's rations.

He closed the diary and looked up at the gray sky. Somewhere in the canopy above, the grotesquely swollen crows circled, waiting. They, at least, understood the value of patience.
