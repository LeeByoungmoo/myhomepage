23
Biological Cog-Wheels and the Single Point of Failure

The narrow gap in the exhaust duct—a tangle of rusted girders and moss—was the remnant of a bypass passage Chloe Bennett had left ten years ago when she designed the bunker's ventilation system.

David Sterling and Chloe crawled through the corroded pipe, their breathing thin and deliberate. Through cracks below, the red glow and acrid machine-oil stench of the compound's interior seeped upward. The two peered through a mesh grate at the heart of the compound—the so-called "power generation zone"—and froze where they lay.

What operated there was the most extreme, most barbaric form of real economy that capitalism had ever conceived.

Three massive hand-cranked capstans—drum-shaped winch mechanisms—squatted at the compound's center. And lashed to those heavy steel columns, circling endlessly like beasts of burden, were dozens of billionaires, stripped naked of their hazmat suits.

"My God…"
Chloe clamped both hands over her mouth.

Preston, the real-estate scion who had once graced the cover of Forbes; Arthur Lindley, the global corporation's HR chief; media moguls—all were naked, wooden yokes loaded across their shoulders, panting like draft animals. Their white skin was rubbed raw and bloody from the friction of the yokes, and their feet—caked in mud and filth—left crimson prints on the ground.

The instant anyone's pace slowed, a neo-human standing guard would drive an electrified club into their bare flesh.

"GAAAAAH!"
Arthur screamed at the shock, foam bubbling from his mouth, and nearly collapsed—but the chains connecting his neck and shoulders would not allow him to fall. Through the rending agony, he was forced to keep his legs moving, keep the gears turning. Their blood and sweat, the kinetic energy of grinding cartilage, drove the capstan-linked turbines, which shuddered as they fed power to the red bulbs illuminating the compound.

David looked down at the grotesque human conveyor belt with arid eyes.

In the age of civilization, these men had sat in plush leather boardroom chairs and earned millions of dollars per hour. They had wrapped workers' time and bodies in the polished term "human resources" and treated them as numbers on an Excel sheet.
But now, the neo-humans were measuring the billionaires' value in precisely physical units. The total electricity a single billionaire could generate by grinding his cartilage for an entire day amounted to a paltry few hundred watts. The blood and flesh of humanity's top 0.001 percent had been perfectly reduced to cheap bio-batteries powering the compound's light bulbs.

"So that's the 'thermodynamic ledger' you were talking about."
Chloe whispered, horrified.

"Exactly. And the man optimizing that ledger at a manic pace is right over there."

David's gaze moved past the capstans to what appeared to be a control room—a battered shipping container deeper inside the compound.

Inside, a bizarre scene was unfolding. Julian "Jules" Cross—who had been dragged in with a rope around his neck—was striding about the container's interior with a clean, uninjured face. The neo-human Alpha stood behind him, arms folded, while Jules had spread out the high-performance tablet and tools he had smuggled from the bunker and was tearing apart the neo-humans' crude switchboard at a frenzied pace.

"Hey, chief! You've been running all three turbines on completely independent batteries this whole time? No wonder your AC power keeps colliding and your voltage is all over the place!"

Cackling, Jules twisted the thick copper wires together and force-connected them to a massive main relay.

"Watch this! I'm going to consolidate this fragmented power grid into a single centralized grid. Surplus energy gets routed into that large accumulator over there, and we squeeze every last joule out of those bio-batteries—your billionaires—at zero percent loss. A perfect centralized control system!"

The instant Jules connected the final wire and threw the main switch—

WHIIIIIRR—POP!

The dim red bulbs that had flickered throughout the compound blazed in unison with blinding white light. The neo-humans' plasma-cutter weapons, which had been sputtering, erupted with stronger, more stable blue arcs. Every neo-human inside the compound roared in triumph, hoisting their spears.

The Alpha, too, let out a grating metallic laugh from behind his bone mask, apparently pleased. He clapped Jules on the shoulder and treated him as though he were a prized shaman.

"He's lost his mind…"
Chloe clutched her head in despair.

"Jules just upgraded their decrepit power grid to a bunker-grade high-efficiency microgrid. Now those neo-humans can run their plasma weapons around the clock without a single power shortage. We're finished. How do you bring down a fortress that's been made perfect?"

But a cold smile was spreading across David's lips.

"Perfect? No. That design is tantamount to suicide."
David pointed through the mesh grate at Jules's switchboard and explained in a low voice.

"The reason the neo-humans survived in this forest is that their system was fragmented. If one generator failed, another kept running. Efficiency was low, but that inefficiency meant slack existed. Jules has just bundled all of their power into a single centralized grid."

David's eyes glinted sharply.

"Do you know the word that actuaries on Wall Street fear most? Single Point of Failure—SPOF. When you concentrate every resource into one node for the sake of maximum efficiency, the destruction of that single central node cascades into the total collapse of the entire system."

The celebrated innovation of global logistics—just-in-time production. By reducing inventory—slack—to zero, it had achieved peak efficiency. But when a single cargo ship blocked a channel in the middle of the Pacific, factories around the world had shut down simultaneously.

Peak efficiency stripped of its buffer becomes a glass fortress that shatters at the slightest impact. Jules appeared to have given the neo-humans wings, but in truth he had implanted the most lethal vulnerability in the compound's heart.

"All we need to do is trigger an overload the system cannot absorb on that main switchboard they depend on. The centralized grid will fail to regulate its own voltage and burn the entire compound to the ground."

Chloe's eyes widened as she grasped David's intent.
"To trigger an overload, you'd need a massive burst of power generated instantaneously. But the billionaires turning those capstans are already so depleted they can barely walk. How do you extract surplus power from them?"

David's gaze fell on Preston and the other billionaires, walking beneath the gears with bloody tears streaking their faces.

"In the long history of capitalism, the most effective method of wringing workers to their absolute limit was never the whip."
David silently pried open an old ventilation-duct cover inside the pipe and lowered himself through.

"It's not despair you give them—it's a thin, deceitful sliver of hope. An incentive."

In the age of civilization, management had dangled the bait called "performance bonuses" to speed up the conveyor belt. Workers had voluntarily surrendered their rest breaks and ground their bones against the machine to snatch that pitiful lure.

The billionaires being whipped below had nothing left to lose. What they needed was not the whip but the mad hope that they could shatter the capstan and fight their way out. And to inject that hope, David had finished preparing himself to do something he had never done in his life—step out from behind the safety of a ledger and hurl his own body into the most dangerous real economy imaginable.

"Chloe, I need you to sever the control valve so the main switchboard's cooling system shuts down. I'm going to go motivate our poor bio-batteries."

Two shadows split apart in the darkness and began seeping soundlessly through the compound's deepest arteries. The countdown to a cruel, precision-engineered strike—one that would annihilate the neo-humans' perfectly optimized empire with a single overload—had begun to tick, measured in the metallic clank of turning gears.
