The Survival Contribution Score — Where Your Net Worth Equals Zero

By midmorning of the third day, the encampment had been transformed.

Under Nathaniel Pierce's direction, Arthur Lindley had worked through the night with methodical intensity, drafting what he called the "Survival Contribution System" on strips of bark peeled from birch trees, using a charcoal stick as his pen. The document was presented at dawn, nailed to a dead tree trunk at the center of camp with a sharpened bone.

"Attention, everyone. From this moment forward, the allocation of all resources—water, food rations, fire access, and medical supplies—will be determined by an individual's Survival Contribution Score."

Arthur read aloud from his bark manifesto, his thin voice carrying the same practiced authority he had once used to announce quarterly performance results.

"One point is awarded for each unit of firewood delivered. Two points for construction labor on the perimeter wall. Three points for one liter of potable water produced via the purification filter. Bonus points may be awarded at Nathaniel's discretion for exceptional service."

David Sterling stood at the edge of the gathering, observing the reactions.

The billionaires listened with the rapt attention of employees at a mandatory corporate town hall. They were accustomed to being evaluated—measured, ranked, compensated. The language Arthur used was deliberately familiar: "performance metrics," "contribution quotas," "resource allocation." It was corporate HR jargon transplanted into the wilderness, and it worked precisely because these people had never known any other framework for organizing human effort.

What David found most chilling was not the system itself—which was, in its crude way, rational—but the speed with which Nathaniel had seized control of its most critical variable.

The purification filter.

Among all the supplies they had dragged from the bunker, the portable water purification unit was the single most important asset. In a world where every stream and puddle was contaminated by a decade of unchecked radiation and biological mutation, that machine was the only reliable source of safe drinking water for a hundred people. And Nathaniel had, with quiet efficiency, moved the filter to a position directly adjacent to his personal tent. His subordinate—Preston, now reduced to a trembling guard dog after his public humiliation during the boar attack—stood watch over the machine with a makeshift club.

"The filter is a shared resource," Nathaniel had announced. "But operating it requires expertise. I will personally oversee its use."

No one challenged him. To challenge him would mean challenging access to water—and after three days of dehydration, with cracked lips and pounding headaches, no one was willing to risk that.

"Do you see what he's done?" David murmured, not to anyone in particular.

Chloe Bennett appeared beside him. The bunker's former chief structural engineer—the woman who had actually designed the underground facility that had kept them alive for a decade—she had been remarkably quiet since their emergence. While the billionaires had bickered over cliff-top real estate and campfire patents, Chloe had been methodically collecting soil samples, testing water sources, and cataloguing the mutated flora surrounding the camp.

"He who controls the water controls everything," Chloe said simply. "It's the oldest trick in human history. Every irrigation empire, every dam project, every water privatization scheme in the developing world—same playbook."

David nodded. "And Arthur's contribution system is the mechanism that converts that monopoly into total social control. You want water? Work. You want more water? Work harder. Can't work? Then you're worthless."

The system was activated that afternoon with immediate and devastating efficiency.

The first to be evaluated was, inevitably, the weakest.

Cohen—the elderly resort magnate with the fractured wrist—limped to the evaluation station (a flat stone where Arthur sat with his bark ledger). Arthur examined his injury and recorded it.

"Physical capacity assessment: severely impaired. Contribution forecast: minimal. I'm assigning you a provisional score of zero. You'll receive a quarter-ration of water until your condition improves."

"A quarter? I'll die of dehydration!"

"Then I suggest you find a way to contribute, Cohen. The system doesn't reward incapacity."

Cohen staggered away, his eyes wide with the bewilderment of a man who had never before been told he was worthless. In the old world, his net worth had been his identity, his shield, his proof of existence. Here, stripped of every abstract marker of value, he was just an old man with a broken wrist—a liability on a ledger.

David watched Cohen sit alone at the edge of camp, nursing his arm, and felt something he had not expected: a flicker of recognition. He had seen this before, hundreds of times, in the actuarial tables of workers' compensation claims. The laborer whose back gave out on the factory floor. The driver whose vision failed after decades of night shifts. The moment a human body could no longer generate profit, the system classified it as waste—to be discarded, written off, liquidated.

Cohen had spent his career on the profitable side of that equation. Now he sat on the other.

As the day wore on, the contribution scores were tabulated. A clear hierarchy emerged within hours. Those with physical strength and useful skills climbed the ranks; those without plummeted. Nathaniel sat at the apex—not because he produced anything, but because he controlled the means of production. Arthur, his loyal administrator, occupied the second tier, defining the rules by which everyone else was measured.

By nightfall, the camp was no longer a cluster of equals sheltering from the wilderness. It was a fully functional microeconomy, complete with a monopolist, a bureaucracy, a labor force, and—at the bottom, with a contribution score of zero—its first disposable human beings.

David wrote in his diary by the light of Dennis Chow's commodified campfire.

Day Three, Evening. Nathaniel has replicated the fundamental architecture of capitalism in seventy-two hours. He did not need money, laws, or institutions. He needed only three things: a scarce resource (water), a system of measurement (the contribution score), and fear (of thirst, of cold, of death). The billionaires comply because the alternative is unthinkable. They do not yet realize that the system is designed not to reward contribution, but to manufacture permanent debt. Tomorrow, the quotas will rise. The day after, they will rise again. And the gap between what is demanded and what a human body can deliver will widen until the only option left is total submission.

The formula has not changed in ten thousand years. Only the currency has.
