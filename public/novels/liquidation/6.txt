The Man Who Filed a Patent on Fire

The wreckage left by the boar stampede was catastrophic. Shredded tents and a smashed supply cart lay strewn across the mud, and the billionaires remained paralyzed with the visceral terror of the attack. But nature granted them no time to recover.

When the sun plunged below the horizon, the forest became a vast, lightless freezer. The same murderous chill they had endured on the penthouse cliff two nights before began slithering up their ankles like a serpent. Worse still, the collective dysentery and the boar attack had drained them to the dregs of their physical reserves. Everywhere, the sound of chattering teeth and wretched groaning filled the air.

"Fire… we need fire. Otherwise we'll all freeze to death tonight."

Everyone agreed with the trembling voice, yet no one stepped forward. Among the effects brought from the bunker were premium cigars and silver lighters, but ten years of storage had long since evaporated every drop of lighter fluid.

Then, from the pitch-black darkness, came the sharp screech of metal on metal.

Szzzt—FWASH!

All eyes snapped toward the sound. It was Dennis Chow—former Silicon Valley mobile device founder and cryptocurrency billionaire. He crouched amid the wreckage of a demolished tent, prying apart the battery from his shattered satellite smartphone. He tore a long strip of silver-coated thermal insulation fiber from the lining of his hazmat suit and forced it across the lithium-ion cell's positive and negative terminals.

FWASH—!

A violent spark leapt upward, catching the pile of bone-dry moss Dennis had gathered in advance. He leaned in and breathed gently on the ember. At last, a warm, red flame bloomed to life.

"Fire! Dear God, he's made fire!"

The billionaires, shivering violently, surged toward the flame like zealots rushing toward a prophet. Frostbitten hands stretched toward the warmth, lungs drew in sighs of relief—

"STOP. Right there. Don't cross the line."

Dennis rose, planted himself between the fire and the crowd, and spread his arms wide. In the firelight, his face bore not the selflessness of a rescuer, but the arrogance of a Silicon Valley founder taking the stage at an investor relations pitch.

"I hereby declare that the intellectual property rights to this combustion mechanism, utilizing the short-circuit principle of lithium-ion cells, belong exclusively to me, Dennis Chow. Any unauthorized use of this fire, produced by my patented technology, will be considered a serious infringement of rights."

A grotesque silence descended on the forest. In the bone-cutting cold, at the heart of a ruined world, the word "patent" echoed through the trees. In any sane universe, someone would have kicked Dennis in the sternum and seized the campfire.

But not one of the hundred billionaires laid a hand on him. They exchanged bewildered glances and shifted uncomfortably. For people who had spent their entire lives at the apex of capitalism, controlling others through law and contract, the phrase "intellectual property rights" was as sacrosanct as a religious commandment.

"Dennis, knock off the insanity! We're all going to freeze!"

Someone shouted, but Dennis snorted dismissively.

"Then invent your own fire. I refuse to tolerate the communist mentality of free-riding on the fruits of my intellectual labor. If you want warmth, pay a fair licensing fee."

David Sterling, watching this lunatic farce from behind, swallowed a bitter laugh. A memory surfaced from fifteen years ago: a multinational pharmaceutical company that had monopolized the patent for a breakthrough leukemia drug.

The drug's manufacturing cost had been a mere three dollars per pill. But armed with their patent, the company set the price at three thousand. In the developing world, tens of thousands of patients who could not afford the drug hemorrhaged and died. David's insurance firm had defended the pharmaceutical company, drafting a risk assessment report that weighed the rising death toll against the expected returns from maintaining the patent. "Patents are a sacred right that protects innovation. The dignity of life cannot be allowed to compromise the innovation value of capitalism." That had been the conclusion David had written.

That same refined savagery—which had legally killed tens of thousands—was now being reproduced with perfect fidelity before this tiny campfire.

"Dennis's claim is entirely valid—both legally and as a foundational rule of our new society."

Nathaniel Pierce parted the crowd and stepped forward. All eyes shifted. Nathaniel wore a gentle smile, but his gaze coiled toward Dennis's campfire like a serpent.

"If we seize Dennis's fire by force, that is plunder. The moment we permit barbaric plunder, tomorrow it will be your tents, and the day after, your water and rations that the strongest will strip away. Protecting intellectual property rights is, ultimately, the minimum safeguard for protecting your own assets."

Nathaniel's logic was airtight. By validating Dennis's patent, he was creating the legal precedent that would also shield his own monopoly over purified water. Nathaniel tore a page from the premium leather diary he had brought from the bunker and picked up a charcoal stub from the ground.

"Now then, Dennis. I recognize the value of your technology. Let us draft a licensing agreement: five Survival Contribution points for one hour of fire access. I'll notarize the contract."

Dennis nodded with a satisfied grin. On Nathaniel's crudely scrawled scrap of paper, the billionaires began to form a line.

Preston—his energy utterly spent from digging the latrine pit all day—stood trembling at the very end of the queue. He pressed his thumbprint onto an affidavit surrendering half his water ration credits before he was finally permitted to sit at the fire's outermost edge.

Deep in the darkened forest, a single campfire crackled amid enormous tree roots.

In its red glow, a hundred billionaires huddled like beasts, clutching charcoal-stained contracts to their chests. Not one complained. They felt, instead, a profound sense of reassurance—that they had obtained warmth through "contract," through civilized procedure, through legitimacy.

David Sterling observed this grotesque peace from a step removed, and opened his diary.

In this camp, there was no currency, no courthouse, no prison. And yet Nathaniel and Dennis had conjured "patent rights" and "credit notes" from the void with nothing more than a few words, and the people had willingly collared themselves, becoming voluntary slaves.

"Capitalism is not a system. It is a virus encoded in human DNA. Not even the apocalypse could sterilize it."

David wrote the first line with clinical detachment. Across the campfire, his eyes met Nathaniel's. Nathaniel, as if he had read every thought in David's mind, raised his hand in an elegant, silent toast. In the depths of David's pupils, the ledger of this mad world flickered crimson in the firelight.
