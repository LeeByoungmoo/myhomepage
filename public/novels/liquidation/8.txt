Memories of a Ten-Square-Meter Cell: Who Wrote the Rules

The cacophony of the grotesque venture capital feeding frenzy around the latrine pit faded to a distant hum in David Sterling's ears. He closed his eyes. Shutting out the reeking stench and the abrasive bite of the wind, a sealed chamber deep in his memory stirred to life like a ghost—sterile, inorganic, cold.

3.1 meters by 3.1 meters. Ceiling height: 3 meters.

A perfect white cube of precisely ten square meters.

That had been the entirety of the world permitted to David and the other ninety-nine billionaires for the past decade. These single-occupancy living quarters, designed by the bunker's chief engineer Chloe Bennett, had reduced every conceivable risk—contagion, radiation, physical conflict between residents—to an absolute zero through hermetic isolation. Three times daily, sterilized nutrient tubes were delivered through a hatch at precisely scheduled intervals. The AI analyzed each occupant's heart rate and body temperature, adjusting room illumination and climate to the decimal point.

For men and women who had inhabited multi-thousand-square-foot mansions and commanded legions of servants, this ten-square-meter equality was a suffocating torture. Inside the bunker, the man with ten billion dollars wore the same white sleep suit and swallowed the same flavorless liquid diet as the man with a mere hundred million. It was a space where the class distinctions manufactured by capitalism had been physically annihilated. In that vacuum of perfect bodily equality, they had begun to lose their minds.

The bunker system offered one—and only one—escape hatch to prevent their total psychological collapse: a virtual reality intranet accessed via brainwave-linked headsets, known as "Eden."

Each afternoon during free hours, all hundred residents donned their headsets simultaneously and logged into Eden. Inside that simulated space, they draped themselves in glamorous avatars of their former selves and gathered in artificially rendered bars in New York or banquet halls in Paris.

And it was in those virtual banquet halls that Nathaniel Pierce and Arthur Lindley had conducted their chilling program of conditioning—quietly, methodically, over the course of a decade.

David had watched it all unfold from a corner seat in Eden, observing the process with the detachment of a man viewing a brutal documentary.

In a world of perfectly equal physical resources, Nathaniel had manufactured power by creating artificial scarcity. He opened a clandestine private exchange within the Eden network. Old-world stocks rendered worthless by extinction, real estate titles, corporate ownership certificates—all were converted into virtual chips. With those chips, residents could gamble or purchase virtual luxuries: more intricately coded avatar garments, more realistic simulations of vintage wine.

At first, people treated it as a harmless diversion—a chess game to pass the tedium of confinement. But as time wore on, they became fanatically obsessed with these substanceless virtual transactions. In a space where bodily sensation had been neutered, the only way left to assert one's identity was to accumulate those virtual chips.

"David, look at them."

Five years ago, on the balcony of Eden's virtual banquet hall, Nathaniel had handed David a glass of champagne—nothing more than a rendered data image, of course—and whispered.

"Human beings cannot endure equality. If they cannot confirm their superiority over others, their sense of self collapses. They come to me clutching worthless certificates from a dead world, begging for virtual chips. And I, from nothing, conjure 'credit' and lend it to them."

"The virtual chips you issue are backed by no real-world value whatsoever. They are merely coded numbers."

At David's dry observation, Nathaniel had laughed aloud.

"Isn't that the very nature of money? The trillions of dollars in derivatives we traded on Wall Street were nothing but clusters of pixels on a screen. What matters is not substance. It is the collective hypnosis—making everyone believe those chips have value."

Nathaniel's words had been precisely correct. And the man who had hardened that hypnosis into a perfect system of control was Arthur Lindley, the former Chief Human Resources Officer.

Using Nathaniel's exchange as its foundation, Arthur had introduced the "Eden Reputation Index." Those who accumulated chips or contributed to Nathaniel's exchange saw their index rise, gaining speaking privileges and VIP treatment within the virtual world. Those who fell into debt and went bankrupt found their voices muted or their avatars rendered translucent—a ritual humiliation.

The reputation index operated as a perfect social death sentence. For a human being locked in a ten-square-meter white cell, being treated as invisible in the only available channel of social interaction was a terror worse than death itself.

Preston Huntington III had been the reputation system's most perfect victim. Even inside virtual reality, Preston could not shed the arrogance of a fourth-generation heir. He squandered virtual chips at the gambling tables like water. Eventually, buried in debt, his reputation index crashed to rock bottom, and the other residents systematically ignored and mocked him in virtual space. On the brink of madness from total isolation, Preston had fallen to his knees before Nathaniel and begged.

That was when Nathaniel, smiling, had placed a "pledge" before Preston:

[Upon the opening of the bunker doors and return to the physical world, the undersigned agrees that all debts incurred within Eden shall be repaid through real-world labor and resources.]

A devil's contract that transmuted imaginary debt into physical chains. Delirious with isolation, Preston had pressed his trembling digital thumbprint onto that virtual pledge. He was not alone. More than half the billionaires, unable to resist boredom and vanity, had taken on staggering virtual debts to Nathaniel.

David, the senior actuary, had mentally tallied that grotesque ledger and shuddered.

Over ten years, without a single act of physical violence, using nothing but human psychology and virtual data, Nathaniel had perfectly domesticated a hundred billionaires. They had lain in identical, equal rooms. But their minds and souls had already been filed away, layer upon layer, inside the vast pyramid of hierarchy that Nathaniel had engineered.

"Hey, David! Stop sitting there like an idiot and come look at this!"

A sudden shout snapped David's eyes open.

The phantom of the ten-square-meter cell dissolved, and the stinking, ruined forest flooded back into view. A media mogul was waving at David. Before him stood Preston digging at the latrine pit, and Arthur Lindley holding his bark ledger.

"Preston's infrastructure is on the verge of turning a profit already. At this rate, he might sweep up all of Nathaniel's water ration tonight. You're an actuary, aren't you? Give us a risk assessment on this infrastructure's future!"

The gleaming eyes of mud-caked billionaires babbling about investment returns. The insanity of earnestly trading imaginary currency—points—in front of Preston's pit was not some sudden aberration.

David rose slowly to his feet.

These people had spent ten years being conditioned—down to the marrow—in the art of blindly worshipping substanceless value and trampling one another to seize it. When the bunker doors opened, they had not gained freedom. The power game that had played out on Eden's virtual monitors had simply been copy-pasted—downloaded—into the physical world of mud, sewage, and blood.

And there, beyond the campfire, Nathaniel Pierce leaned casually against the communal water purification filter—the only one they had brought from the bunker—watching David with a thin smile. The same arrogant posture with which he had once raised a champagne glass on Eden's virtual balcony, five years ago.

Who wrote the rules.

David met Nathaniel's gaze with ice-cold eyes and turned to a new page in his diary.

The physical world had collapsed, but capitalism had not ended. Inside the incubator of the bunker, capitalism had fed on human neural pathways and evolved into a more grotesque, more vicious strain of virus. This absurd market economy, born from a latrine pit, would soon demand severed limbs and real blood. Only David's cold ledger warned of the brutal crash that was bearing down upon them all.
