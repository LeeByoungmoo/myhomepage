Drinking Water Rations Turned Derivative

Violence is the most primal form of power, but it suffers from a fatal flaw: it cannot sustain itself. Three days after the survival-of-the-fittest colosseum—where only the top ten received water—opened for business, the camp had transformed into a sprawling infirmary.

Daily brawls with axes and fists over logs had left more than half the hundred billionaires sprawled in the dirt with serious injuries. Broken ribs, split scalps, grotesquely swollen faces. Each morning they awoke to bodies weaker than the day before. For the elderly and physically frail—men like Cohen—daily combat in the forest against muscular young startup founders was a confirmed death sentence.

"I can't keep this up. By tomorrow I won't make the top ten—I won't even be able to drag a single branch back."

Cohen groaned, cradling his scab-crusted palms. Thirst was blurring his vision. He needed water to survive, but the "means of production"—his body—was already bankrupt.

Then, in Cohen's bleary vision, he spotted Preston Huntington III lounging comfortably against three massive oak posts in a corner of camp. Through intimidation and plunder, Preston had claimed first place three days running, monopolizing half the water rations. But Preston, too, was suffering from torn muscles. Watching him limp and smear mud into his wounded shoulder, a capitalist's instinct flared behind Cohen's eyes.

Cohen rummaged through the backpack he had brought from the bunker and produced a silver-foiled packet of high-concentration painkillers and antibiotic ointment. Then he crept cautiously toward the snarling Preston.

"Preston. Let's make a deal."

"Get lost, old man. I've got nothing to say to someone who can't even bring back a stick."

Preston raised his club, but Cohen dangled the antibiotic ointment before his eyes.

"By tomorrow, those wounds of yours will be infected and you won't be able to chop wood either. With these painkillers and ointment, you'll dominate first place again. I'm 'investing' this medicine in your body. In return, you pay me a dividend of three hundred milliliters from the liter of water you earn tomorrow."

Preston's club froze in midair.

It was the wilderness's first futures trade. A contract investing present goods—medicine—against future value—tomorrow's water that did not yet exist—and splitting the returns.

Capitalism invents financial engineering with uncanny genius when it hits the wall of physical limitation. The secret pact between Cohen and Preston spread through the camp like wildfire. Elderly and depleted billionaires stopped going into the forest altogether. Instead, they scraped together their last remaining real assets—tent fabric, shoes, food cubes—and attached themselves as sponsors to the young, physically capable "gladiators."

Nathaniel Pierce and Arthur Lindley did not merely tolerate this development. They elevated it into a formal financial product.

"Effective today, the fractional transfer and derivative trading of water ration rights is hereby legalized."

Arthur unfurled his bark ledger and made the announcement.

"Investors may draft 'futures contracts' securing a share of water contingent on their gladiator's ranking placement. The exchange commission is ten percent of the ration volume, remitted to Representative Nathaniel's purification operations fund."

The camp had become a fully functioning futures exchange overnight.

The billionaires now divided into two castes. The "underlying assets"—laborers who bled while hauling logs—and the "investors" who bet on their stamina and odds of success.

"I'll wager three days of my emergency rations that Preston finishes first tomorrow! Dividend payout: one hundred milliliters!"
"No—Steven secretly crafted a sharp stone axe yesterday! I'm selling a futures option on Steven placing top three—any buyers?"

They scrawled symbols and numbers in charcoal across the dirt, trading derivatives in a frenzy. Not a single drop of water had been purified yet, but on paper, dozens of liters had already been fractioned, sliced, and traded. When a rumor circulated that Preston had twisted his leg while felling a tree, the price of his "water bonds" cratered. When someone reported discovering a massive trunk, that person's equity surged.

David Sterling watched the gambling frenzy and was seized by a chilling sense of déjà vu.

Eighteen years ago, the landscape of Wall Street on the eve of the global financial crisis had looked exactly like this. Banks had taken the mortgages of impoverished homeowners—the underlying assets—sliced and bundled them into thousands of Byzantine "derivatives" known as CDOs. There was only one real house, but tens of thousands of paper bets on whether that house would collapse had been issued and traded. Everyone believed the numbers on their ledgers would rise forever, passing a ticking bomb from hand to hand.

How was what they were doing now any different?

The actual water the purifier produced was roughly ten liters per day. But the volume of "ration right derivatives" being bought and sold had already exceeded ten times that figure. The moment someone in the forest colosseum sustained a fatal injury—or died—and the underlying asset of labor collapsed, these derivatives would cascade into default, plunging the entire camp into an unprecedented inferno.

"Why don't you join in, David?"

A low, calm voice reached David's ear. It was Chloe Bennett, the bunker's chief engineer. The only woman among the hundred who appeared utterly indifferent to the spectacle. Days without bathing had left her hair a wreck, but her gaze was as keen as a forest predator's.

"An actuary like you must have already calculated how this ledger game ends."

At Chloe's question, David smiled bitterly.

"The underlying assets are rotting from the inside out. They treat human blood and muscle as an inexhaustible fuel source. Bankruptcy is a matter of time."

"Exactly."

Chloe glanced around once, then tugged David's sleeve.

"While those idiots gamble over imaginary droplets that don't exist, I found something real outside this camp. Come with me. I'll make you rewrite your entire risk ledger."

David rose without a word, compelled by the gravity of Chloe's gaze. While the billionaires howled and speculated over shares of Preston's lumber haul, the two slipped silently toward the deep boundary of the forest.

When the camp's noise had faded to a distant murmur, Chloe stopped before a massive fern colony. She carefully brushed aside the rotting leaves on the ground.

"Look at this."

David looked where she pointed. And in that instant, he felt his heart freeze solid.

A footprint, stamped clearly in the mud.

It did not belong to the polished sole of the fifty-thousand-dollar Italian bespoke boots the billionaires wore. It was a massive, crude track—something fashioned from rough animal hide lashed together with cord. A footprint closer to a beast's than a human's.

"Based on the stride length and depth, the individual is over six foot three and heavily built. And this print was made this morning. While we were rioting over the bank run, someone was watching us from behind these bushes."

Cold sweat trickled down David's spine.

The shock did not end there. From the undergrowth beside the footprint, Chloe picked up an exposed animal bone. A femur from a creature resembling the boar that had demolished their tents. But the bone had not been gnawed by animal teeth.

The cut surface was unnervingly smooth and precise—as if sliced by an industrial laser cutter.

"This wasn't cut with a stone axe or a rusted saw. This was severed in a single stroke by something extraordinarily sharp—a hunting blade, or something beyond that entirely."

Chloe handed the bone to David and whispered.

For ten years, they had believed the surface was an empty wasteland, devoid of all human life. That had been a catastrophic miscalculation. Beings who had survived the apocalypse in the open—enduring radiation and the savagery of the wild, evolving, or rather, arming themselves into entirely new forms—were ruling this forest.

"Those billionaire lordships in there, killing each other over latrine-pit equity and trading imaginary water shares."

Chloe ran a cold finger along the bone's smooth cross-section and murmured.

"They think they're the masters of this forest. But to whatever is out there, they're nothing but a hundred plump, cashmere-clad livestock that wandered into the slaughterhouse of their own accord."

David gripped the smooth bone tight.

Nathaniel's credit notes, Preston's violence, Arthur's flawless legal code—before the overwhelming force of this wilderness, all of it was less than garbage. Beyond the walls of this miniature capitalism built on imaginary power, the blade of true savagery was closing silently on their throats.
