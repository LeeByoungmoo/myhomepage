The World's Most Expensive Bonfire

The second night on the surface plunged the encampment into a crisis that transcended mere discomfort.

The frozen corpses of the two who had died on the "royal floor" were dragged down from the cliff and deposited at the edge of the camp. No one offered a eulogy; no one suggested a burial. The remaining ninety-eight billionaires merely glanced at the stiffened bodies, mentally tallied the dead men's share of rations, and scurried to secure their own positions.

But the cold was a problem that rations alone could not solve. Without fire, the coming night would claim more victims. And fire required fuel.

"Who knows how to start a fire? Anyone?"

The question hung in the air, met with blank stares and uneasy shifting. Among these hundred individuals—who had collectively controlled more wealth than the GDP of most nations—not a single one had ever struck a match outside of lighting a Cuban cigar. In the bunker, temperature had been regulated to the tenth of a degree by AI systems. Before the bunker, their homes had been heated by automated smart systems they had never bothered to understand. Fire was something that happened inside designer fireplaces for aesthetic ambiance, never out of necessity.

Dennis Chow, the Silicon Valley tech mogul who had once built the world's largest smart-home conglomerate, was the first to act. He gathered dried leaves and twigs with trembling hands, arranged them in a rough pile, and attempted to generate a spark by striking two rocks together—a technique he had apparently seen in a survival documentary during his years in VR.

Forty-five minutes of desperate scraping produced nothing but blistered palms and a small heap of dust. Dennis cursed and flung the rocks away.

"This is insane. I designed AI systems that controlled the climate of ten thousand homes simultaneously, and I can't light a goddamn pile of sticks."

"Perhaps I can help."

Jules Cross sauntered over with an infuriating grin. From the inner pocket of his hazmat suit, he produced a miniature survival lighter—military-grade, refillable, the kind of thing a man like Jules would have pocketed "just for fun" before the world ended. With a single flick, a steady flame danced to life.

"There she is. Prometheus, eat your heart out."

The reaction was immediate and Pavlovian. The billionaires surged toward the flame like moths, their hands outstretched, their eyes wide with primal relief. For a moment, the masks of privilege fell away, and they were simply cold, frightened animals drawn to warmth.

But the warmth lasted barely an hour. The small fire Jules had kindled consumed its fuel with alarming speed. The twigs and dead leaves in the immediate vicinity had been gathered quickly, but they burned even faster—dry tinder reduced to ash in minutes. The cold, patient and relentless, was already creeping back.

"We need real firewood. Logs. Something that will burn through the night."

Nathaniel Pierce's voice cut through the chattering of teeth. He stood by the fire's dying embers, his silhouette commanding, his expression calculating.

"But the trees in this forest are massive," someone protested. "We don't have axes. We don't have saws. We can barely break a branch."

"Then use what we do have."

Nathaniel's gaze swept across the camp, landing on the supply cart. Among the items the bunker had provided were several military-grade multi-tools and two collapsible hand saws—woefully inadequate for the towering mutant trees surrounding them, but theoretically capable of processing smaller deadwood.

What happened next was the first true economic transaction of the post-apocalyptic world.

Dennis Chow, who had managed to nurse the fire back to life with Jules's lighter, suddenly stood up and planted himself between the flames and the approaching crowd.

"Hold on. I built this fire. I maintained it. This fire is my intellectual property."

The camp fell silent.

"Are you serious?" Preston Huntington III stared at Dennis with open incredulity.

"Dead serious. In the old world, I held over three thousand patents. The principle hasn't changed. I created value; I own it. Anyone who wants to warm themselves at my fire needs to pay a usage fee."

"A usage fee? In what currency, you lunatic? Money doesn't exist anymore!"

"Sure it does." Dennis's eyes gleamed with the same fervor he'd once displayed pitching venture capitalists. "Labor. Each person who wants thirty minutes by this fire will go out and bring back an armful of burnable deadwood. My fire, my rules. I'm licensing access in exchange for fuel contributions."

David Sterling watched from the periphery, arms folded, as the camp erupted into outrage and then—with terrible inevitability—capitulation.

The fury was theatrical and short-lived. The cold was real, immediate, and lethal. One by one, the billionaires who had commanded armies of servants and manipulated global markets shuffled into the dark forest to scavenge for sticks and branches. They returned with pitiful bundles—their manicured hands scratched and bleeding from thorns, their designer boots caked in mud—and deposited their tribute at Dennis's feet.

Dennis inspected each offering with absurd gravity, occasionally rejecting a bundle as "insufficient BTU potential" and sending the humiliated contributor back into the darkness.

David recorded it in his diary: Day Two. Dennis Chow has invented the patent system in a world with no patent office. The campfire—humanity's most ancient technology—has been monetized. The fuel payments are, in essence, a primitive labor tax. The billionaires comply because the alternative is death by exposure. They do not recognize the irony: the men who spent their lives extracting labor from others at minimal cost are now the laborers, paying with their own blistered hands for the right to not freeze.

Nathaniel Pierce, notably, did not scavenge. He stood beside the fire with the easy confidence of a man who had already calculated a different angle. When Dennis demanded his contribution, Nathaniel merely smiled.

"Dennis, your fire is impressive. But fires die. What happens when the wind shifts, or the rain comes? You need someone to manage the long-term fuel supply chain—negotiating quotas, establishing shifts, optimizing burn rates. In short, you need a CFO."

Dennis hesitated. Nathaniel pressed his advantage.

"I'll organize a rotation system for fuel collection. I'll ensure your fire never goes out. In exchange, I sit by the fire for free—and I take a ten percent management fee from every fuel contribution."

Dennis, who understood the value of delegation better than anyone, extended his hand.

And just like that, beside the crackling, world's-most-expensive bonfire—fueled by the calloused labor of the ultra-rich and managed by a hedge fund manager extracting fees from thin air—the first crude architecture of post-apocalyptic capitalism was erected.

David glanced at Jules Cross, who sat apart from the group, tinkering with the remains of his destroyed drone. Jules caught David's eye and offered a crooked grin.

"Isn't it beautiful?" Jules whispered. "The world ended, and they rebuilt Wall Street in forty-eight hours."

David said nothing. He turned the collar of his jacket against the cold and retreated to his crater. Above him, the fire crackled—its light casting long, grotesque shadows of billionaires warming their hands over a flame they had paid for with their own suffering.

The commodification of survival had begun.
