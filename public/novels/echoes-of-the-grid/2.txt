# MEMORIES OF THE BLADE
## Episode 2: The Bronze Oath

---

### *Seoul, Present Day*

The lab results came back wrong.

Not incorrect — Su-hyun had triple-checked, had the technician run the analysis twice more, and finally stood over the mass spectrometer herself to watch the data render in real time. The results were consistent. They were reproducible. They were also, by every metric of modern materials science, impossible.

"Hemoglobin," she said.

Jin-woo sat across from her in the cramped faculty office she kept at K University, surrounded by towers of academic journals and the faint smell of old paper. His bandaged hand rested on his knee. The burn had not improved overnight; if anything, the edges had darkened, the blistered skin taking on a reddish hue that looked less like a thermal injury and more like a stain.

"Human hemoglobin," Su-hyun continued. She pushed her glasses up her nose — a nervous tic Jin-woo remembered from their university days. "Degraded, obviously. Trace amounts, embedded in the bronze alloy matrix at the molecular level. Not surface contamination. *Structural*. As if the blood was mixed into the molten metal during the original casting."

"How old?"

"Carbon dating on the bronze substrate puts the core material at approximately 2,300 years. Late Gojoseon period, consistent with the engraving styles I identified last night." She paused. "Jin-woo, hemoglobin proteins don't survive 2,300 years. They denature. They decompose. In an alloy matrix exposed to oxygen, temperature variation, moisture — they should be completely gone within a few decades, let alone millennia."

"And yet," Jin-woo said, echoing Mr. Kang without knowing it.

"And yet." Su-hyun folded her hands on the desk. Her nails were bitten short — another old habit. "The spiral engraving. I found a match. A bronze mirror excavated from a Gojoseon-era burial site near Pyongyang in 1978 — the published photographs show an identical spiral motif. It's catalogued as a 'cycle of life' symbol, associated with shamanic rebirth rituals."

"A rebirth ritual."

"Specifically, the infusion of living essence into crafted objects. The belief that a maker could bind a soul — or part of a soul — into something they created. Most scholars treat it as mythology." She met his eyes. "Most scholars haven't found two-thousand-year-old blood proteins in a bronze sword."

Jin-woo looked down at his bandaged palm. Through the gauze, he could feel the burn pulsing — a slow, steady rhythm, like a heartbeat that wasn't his.

That night, he dreamed.

---

### *Gojoseon, circa 300 BCE*

The mountain had no name either.

It rose from the forest like a fist of granite, its flanks scarred with ravines where spring meltwater carved channels in the rock. Near the summit, where the treeline surrendered to wind and stone, a cave mouth opened — dark, warm, breathing the mineral exhalation of the earth's interior.

Inside the cave, a man was building a fire.

His name was Dal-sol, and he was the finest blacksmith his people had ever produced, though he would never have used those words himself. In his language, the word for "blacksmith" was the same as the word for "shaper" — one who gave form to formless things. It was a sacred calling, inherited from his father and his father's father, stretching back to a time before memory when the first of their line had watched lightning strike a copper vein and understood that the gods were showing him what metal wanted to become.

Dal-sol's arms were landscapes of old burns. His hands were callused to leather. His back ached from twenty years of bending over crucibles, and his lungs carried the permanent rasp of a man who had breathed charcoal smoke since boyhood. He was thirty-two years old, which made him nearly old, and he was very much afraid.

The chieftain's command had been simple: forge a divine sword.

The northern horsemen were coming. Everyone knew it. The scouts had returned with reports of a mounted force ten times the size of anything the tribe had faced before — not a raiding party but an army, moving south through the forests with the systematic purpose of people who intended to stay. The chieftain, a grizzled warrior named Ureuk, had exhausted his military options. Alliances had failed. Fortifications were inadequate. What remained was the oldest recourse of desperate men: the supernatural.

"Make me a blade that cannot be broken," Ureuk had said, his scarred face lit by firelight. "A blade that kills what other blades cannot. A blade with a god inside it."

Dal-sol had not told the chieftain that he didn't know how to put a god inside a sword. He had not said that every technique he possessed — and he possessed many, learned from traders who came from the west with their iron-working secrets, and from the eastern coast where the fishermen smelted tools from meteorite fragments — was a technique for shaping *metal*, not divinity. He had simply bowed and climbed the mountain, because refusing the chieftain meant death, and failing the chieftain meant death, and the only narrow path that might lead to survival ran through the impossible.

He had been on the mountain for thirty-one days.

The forge he'd built was crude but effective: a stone-lined pit fed by leather bellows, the air channel angled to create a vortex that could push the temperature high enough to melt the copper-tin alloy he'd prepared. The copper came from a vein on the mountain's western face — the purest he'd ever seen, green-blue crystals gleaming in the rock like frozen water. The tin he'd traded for months ago, carried overland from the coast by a man who smelled of fish and asked no questions.

Thirty-one days. Fourteen attempts. Fourteen failures.

Each time, the alloy cooled into something flawed — a blade that shattered when struck, or bent like a reed, or held an edge for a single cut before going dull. Dal-sol had adjusted ratios, modified temperatures, experimented with quenching techniques he'd only heard described in fragments of conversation with traveling smiths. Nothing worked. The metal resisted him, as if it knew what he was asking and found the request inadequate.

On the thirty-first night, Dal-sol sat beside his forge and stared into the coals and considered the forbidden thing.

Every smith knew the stories. The old knowledge — older than bronze, older than fire, passed down in whispers that respectable craftsmen pretended not to hear. The ritual of infusion. The binding of life to metal. It required a living essence: blood, freely given, from someone the smith loved. Not animal blood. Not the smith's own blood. The blood of the beloved, offered voluntarily, because the binding only held if the essence carried love.

It was forbidden because it worked.

And it was forbidden because of what it cost.

---

*The fire was changing.*

*In Jin-woo's apartment, two thousand three hundred years in the future, the burn on his palm flared with heat. He shifted in his sleep, his left hand clenching against sheets that were damp with sweat. In his dream — or was it a memory? — he could feel the forge's heat on his face, smell the charcoal, hear the bellows breathing like a living lung.*

*He did not know yet that he was dreaming backward through time. That the blade on his kitchen table was a receiver, and he was tuning in.*

---

"You haven't slept."

Asa's voice came from the cave mouth. She stood silhouetted against a sky salted with more stars than any modern city-dweller would believe possible — a dome of light so dense it cast shadows. She was carrying a clay pot of barley porridge and a leather flask of water, and her face, as she stepped into the firelight, held the particular blend of love and exasperation that belongs exclusively to women watching the men they love destroy themselves.

"You shouldn't be here," Dal-sol said. "Your father —"

"My father is sharpening spears he knows won't work and making speeches about ancestral glory he doesn't believe." She set the food down and sat beside him. "What are you doing?"

"Failing."

"Ah." She leaned against his shoulder. She smelled of pine resin and the wild garlic that grew near the river, and her hair — unbound, black, falling past her waist — brushed against his arm with a softness that made the forge, the mountain, the war, all of it seem very far away.

"I can't do it," Dal-sol said. It was the first time he'd said it aloud. "I can't put a god in a sword. I'm a smith, not a shaman. I shape metal. That's all I know how to do."

"Then do what you know."

"What I know isn't enough."

Asa was quiet for a moment. Then she took his hand — his burned, calloused, trembling hand — and held it in both of hers. Her fingers were cool. His were hot. The temperature differential between them seemed, in that moment, to contain the entire problem of his existence: he was fire, she was water, and the thing the chieftain demanded was something neither element could produce alone.

"What would be enough?" she asked.

He told her. He hadn't meant to. The forbidden knowledge spilled out of him like water from a cracked vessel — the ritual, the binding, the requirement of blood freely given, the cost that the old stories hinted at but never fully described. He told her everything because she was the only person in the world he could not lie to, and when he was finished, the cave was very quiet, and the fire was low, and Asa's face was unreadable.

"No," he said, before she could speak. "Absolutely not."

"You didn't ask me anything."

"I don't need to. I can see you thinking it."

"Dal-sol —"

"*No.* I will not use your blood. I will not risk you. I will go to your father and tell him I failed and accept whatever —"

"You will die."

"Then I will die."

"And our people will die. And the children. And the old ones who can't run." She held his gaze. In the firelight, her eyes were the color of dark honey. "A drop of blood, Dal-sol. One drop. Against all those lives."

"The old stories say the cost —"

"The old stories were told by men who were afraid. I'm not afraid." She smiled, and the smile was so fierce, so *certain*, that it broke something inside him. "One drop."

---

The crucible was ready at dawn.

Dal-sol had spent the remaining hours of darkness preparing — selecting his finest copper, measuring the tin with obsessive precision, building the fire to a heat that made the cave walls weep with condensation. The alloy swirled in the stone crucible like liquid sun, orange-white and terrifying, and the bellows pumped and the cave roared with the voice of a furnace that knew what was coming.

Asa stood beside him. She wore a white linen shift — the ceremonial garment of their people, worn for births, deaths, and the moments between — and she held her right hand over the crucible with a steadiness that Dal-sol, who was shaking, could not match.

"Now," he said.

She pricked her finger on the tip of a bronze awl. A single drop of blood — impossibly small, impossibly red — swelled on her fingertip and fell.

The molten metal screamed.

There was no other word for it. The alloy convulsed, its surface cratering and then erupting upward in a fountain of white light that hit the cave ceiling and spread outward like a living thing. The temperature in the cave spiked so violently that Dal-sol staggered backward, pulling Asa with him. The crucible was vibrating — *singing* — a high, clear note that resonated in his teeth and his bones and in a place behind his breastbone that he had never been aware of before.

And then it stopped.

The cave was silent. The fire was out. The crucible sat in darkness, and in the darkness, the metal inside it glowed — not with residual heat but with something else, a light that was cold and steady and faintly blue, like moonlight trapped in amber.

Dal-sol approached the crucible. His hands were shaking so badly he could barely grip the tongs. He tilted the molten metal into the blade mold he'd prepared — a simple, elegant shape, a straight-edged short sword with a tang for a wooden grip — and the metal flowed like water and filled the mold as if it had been waiting its entire existence for this exact shape.

He quenched it. He polished it. He fitted the handle.

The blade was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen.

It was beautiful in the way that a storm is beautiful, or a predator at full sprint — beauty that carries inside it the absolute certainty of lethality. The edge was sharper than anything he had ever achieved, sharp enough to split a hair held against it. The balance was supernatural: the blade seemed to weigh nothing and everything simultaneously, as if gravity had been renegotiated in its favor.

He held it up. The blue-white glow had faded, but the metal retained a sheen that no bronze should possess — a liquid quality, like the surface of deep water.

"It's perfect," Asa whispered.

She was leaning against the cave wall. Her face was pale. Paler than it should have been.

---

*Somewhere outside of time, the blade remembered.*

*The first hands. The hot ones, scarred and sure. The first blood. Sweet, unwilling, given in love. The first moment of awareness — not thought, exactly, but something adjacent to thought: a *knowing*. A sense of self that had no name and no precedent.*

*I was born.*

*And immediately, I was hungry.*

---

The fever came that night.

Asa collapsed at the foot of the mountain, halfway back to the village. Her skin was burning. Her pulse was rapid and thready, like a bird's heart beating against the inside of a closed fist. The healers came — the old women with their herb bundles and their chanting, the shaman with his drum and his animal masks — and none of them could find a cause.

"Her spirit is draining," the shaman said, and looked at Dal-sol with eyes that held too much understanding.

Dal-sol carried the blade to the edge of the forest and tried to destroy it.

He struck it against granite. The granite split. He threw it from a cliff. It landed point-first in the riverbed below, undamaged, and when he climbed down to retrieve it, he found it *warm*, as if it had been waiting for him. He heated the forge to its maximum temperature and tried to melt it back down. The blade sat in the fire like a stone in water — unchanged, untouched, almost *amused*.

It could not be destroyed.

The chieftain took the sword to war. The battle lasted less than an hour. The northern horsemen — hardened steppe warriors who had never lost an engagement — broke and fled before Ureuk's charge, not because the tribe's fighters were superior but because the blade in Ureuk's hand moved with a speed and violence that transcended human capability. It cut through bronze armor like paper. It seemed to anticipate the enemy's movements. Warriors who came within its reach died before they understood what was killing them.

The tribe was saved.

Asa was dying.

Each day she grew thinner, more translucent, as if she were being slowly erased. She could no longer walk. She could no longer eat. She lay on a pallet of furs in her father's longhouse, and the only thing that seemed to bring her comfort was the sound of Dal-sol's voice, which he gave her without interruption, talking until his throat was raw — telling her stories of the forge, of the metals he'd shaped, of the life he'd imagined for them in the years they would now never share.

On the seventh night, Ureuk returned in triumph. The celebration lasted until dawn. The entire village drank and danced and sang songs about the divine sword and the great chieftain who wielded it. In the longhouse, Dal-sol held Asa's hand and listened to the singing and hated every note.

"I did this to you," he said.

"I chose," she whispered. Her voice was barely audible. "Don't take that from me."

"I'll find a way to reverse it. I'll break the binding —"

"You can't break it. You know that." She squeezed his hand. Her grip was terrifyingly weak. "Promise me something."

"Anything."

"Don't let the sword be the last thing you make. Make something beautiful after this. Something that doesn't cost anything."

He couldn't speak. He just held her hand and pressed his forehead against her knuckles and breathed.

When her breathing finally stopped, just before dawn, the silence in the longhouse was absolute. Outside, the celebration continued. Inside, Dal-sol sat with the woman he loved, who had given a drop of blood to save a nation and been consumed by the very thing she helped create.

He took the blade from Ureuk's tent. The chieftain was too drunk to notice.

In the gray light of early morning, Dal-sol sat cross-legged on the forest floor and held the blade across his knees. It was warm in his hands. He could feel it *pulsing* — a slow, rhythmic throb, like a heartbeat. Like *her* heartbeat, the one that had stopped an hour ago.

He took his finest engraving tool and carved a single mark into the flat of the blade, just above the tang. A spiral. Small, precise, trembling with the grief of the man who made it.

In the language of his people, the spiral meant many things. The turn of seasons. The path of stars. The journey of the soul after death.

But the meaning Dal-sol chose was simpler.

*Come back.*

---

In his apartment in Mapo-gu, Jin-woo woke with tears on his face and the taste of charcoal in his mouth.

The burn on his palm had deepened. Its edges were darker now, the skin around it faintly discolored, as if something beneath the surface was spreading. He stared at it in the bathroom mirror, turning his hand under the fluorescent light, and saw — or imagined he saw — a pattern in the burn that resembled nothing so much as a spiral.

On the kitchen table, the blade waited.

Quiet. Patient. Full of things it had not yet shown him.

---

*Come back, he wrote. The first words carved into me. The first wish pressed into my metal by a human hand.*

*I have carried those words for twenty-three centuries.*

*And every soul I have touched since has added their own.*

*But his — Dal-sol's — remain the deepest. Because they were the first. Because they were written in love. And because, of all the wishes carved into me across the long millennia of my existence, his is the only one I have ever wanted to grant.*

---

*End of Episode 2*
