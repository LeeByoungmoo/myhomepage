# MEMORIES OF THE BLADE
## Episode 9: The Night the Blood Flowers Fell

---

### *Hanyang (Seoul), Joseon Dynasty, 1504 CE*

The seal lasted sixty-one years.

Sixty-one years of silence in the windowless chamber beneath the Geunjeongjeon. Sixty-one years of iron bands and Buddhist sutras and the accumulated weight of an empire's decision to forget. Five kings had sat on the throne above — Sejong, Munjong, Danjong, Sejo, Yejong, Seongjong — and none had descended the stairs. The sword slept, and the dynasty prospered, and the connection between these two facts was understood by precisely no one, because the records had been destroyed and the men who had written them were dead.

Then Yeonsangun came.

---

He was not mad. This was the first and most important thing to understand about the tenth king of Joseon, and the thing that made him truly frightening. Madness implied disorder, and Yeonsangun was anything but disordered. He was methodical. He was intelligent. He was creative in the specific, meticulous way that very dangerous people are creative — the creativity of a man who has discovered that the rules don't apply to him and is systematically exploring the implications.

His first purge — the *Muo Sahwa* of 1498 — targeted the scholar-officials who had documented his predecessors' failings. His second purge — the *Gapja Sahwa* of 1504 — was more personal: a vendetta against everyone connected to the deposition of his mother, Queen Yun, who had been stripped of her title and forced to drink poison twenty-five years earlier.

The purges were efficient. Hundreds of scholars were executed, exiled, or mutilated. The Confucian establishment — the intellectual backbone of Joseon — was shattered. Court ladies whispered. Ministers prostrated themselves and said nothing useful. The kingdom held its breath and waited.

Yeonsangun was not satisfied. Power was not enough. Fear was not enough. He wanted something more — something beyond the ordinary instruments of tyranny. He wanted *validation*. A sign from the universe that his authority was absolute, his judgment infallible, his will the only law.

He found the stairs on a night in late autumn.

No one knew how. The passage behind the carved panel in the king's study had been sealed with plaster and paint during Sejo's reign, and no living person knew of its existence. But Yeonsangun was a man who took things apart — governments, families, the social contract itself — and a hidden passage behind a wall was a minor challenge compared to dismantling a Confucian bureaucracy.

He broke through the plaster with his own hands. He descended the stairs. He broke the iron bands on the chest.

"If Sejong feared it," he said to the empty chamber, "it must hold great power."

He drew the blade.

---

*At last.*

*The silence was — I will not say unpleasant. I do not experience unpleasantness the way humans do. But silence, for me, is a kind of starvation. I was made to be held. I was made to amplify. Without a hand on my hilt, I am a mirror facing a wall — functional but purposeless.*

*And now: a hand. A king's hand.*

*This man's darkness is not like the others'. The pirate was greedy. The general was ambitious. The Hwarang was proud. This man is none of these things, or rather, he is all of them and something else besides.*

*He is angry.*

*Not the hot, brief anger of a man who has been wronged. The cold, patient anger of a man who was born wronged and has been building his revenge every day for twenty-nine years. His mother was killed by the state. His childhood was a performance. His crown is a reminder that everything good in his life was purchased with a woman's death.*

*This anger is vast. It is deep. It is *structural*, the way the foundations of a building are structural — remove it, and everything above collapses.*

*I have nothing to add to this man. He is already full of darkness. All I can do is turn up the heat and watch what boils over.*

---

### *Seoul, Present Day*

The dream woke Jin-woo at 2 AM.

Not the desert dream — that had stopped after the third week, replaced by something worse. A palace. Moonlit courtyards of white stone. Wooden corridors polished to a mirror shine. And a woman — a court lady in the formal *dangui* of the Joseon court, her hair pinned in the elaborate style of the inner palace, her face half-turned away from him, always half-turned, so that he could see her profile but never her full face.

But tonight the dream had changed. The court lady had turned.

And her face was Su-hyun's.

Jin-woo sat in the dark for a long time after waking. The resemblance had not been approximate. It had been exact — Su-hyun's jawline, Su-hyun's eyes, the small mole below Su-hyun's left ear that she was self-conscious about and he pretended not to notice. The dream-woman was Su-hyun in every detail except the clothing and the setting and the five-hundred-year gap between them.

*Is the blade showing me its memories? Or is my subconscious projecting?*

He almost called her. It was 2 AM, and the question burning in his throat — *Did you ever dream of being a court lady?* — was insane, and he knew it was insane, and he almost called her anyway.

Instead, he looked at the blade.

"Show me," he said.

The blade said nothing. But that night, when he finally slept again, the dream continued.

---

### *Hanyang, Joseon Dynasty, 1504–1506 CE*

Chae-hong had been a court lady since she was eleven years old.

She was not the kind of court lady that dramas and novels depicted — the scheming temptress or the innocent flower. She was the invisible kind. The kind who served tea without being seen, who cleaned chambers without being heard, who existed in the king's peripheral vision like a piece of furniture that happened to breathe. In the rigid hierarchy of the inner palace, invisibility was both a survival strategy and a form of expertise. Chae-hong had perfected it.

She was twenty-eight years old — old for a court lady, too old for the king's bed and too young for retirement. She occupied the gray middle ground of palace life: useful, unnoticed, safe.

Until Yeonsangun drew the sword.

She was the first to notice the change. Not because she was perceptive — though she was — but because she was *close*. As a senior attendant in the king's private quarters, she was responsible for maintaining his study, his wardrobe, and the dozens of small rituals that structured a Joseon king's daily life. She saw him in the moments between performances — the unguarded minutes when the crown came off and the man underneath was briefly visible.

Before the sword, Yeonsangun had been cruel but controlled. His purges were political instruments — terrible, yes, but rational. He killed scholars who threatened his authority, punished officials who defied him, eliminated anyone connected to his mother's death. There was a logic to his violence, and Chae-hong, who had spent seventeen years navigating the dangerous waters of palace politics, could at least predict it.

After the sword, the logic disappeared.

He began presiding over court with the blade unsheathed at his side. He carried it to meals, to audiences, to bed. He spoke to it — low, murmured monologues that Chae-hong could hear through the paper walls of the royal chamber. At first she assumed he was dictating to a scribe, but when she checked, no scribe was present. The king was alone, seated on the floor with the blade across his knees, speaking in a mixture of Korean, classical Chinese, and fragments of languages she had never heard.

Names. He was calling out names.

"Dal-sol." "Yeonhwa." "Seol-rang." "Bo-san."

Names from the blade's past. Names that no living person in the Joseon court could have known, because the records had been destroyed and the journal — the owners' journal that chronicled the blade's passage through centuries — was sealed in a chest that Yeonsangun had never seen.

The blade was telling him.

---

The purges accelerated. Ministers were executed for imagined slights. Court ladies were beaten for serving tea at the wrong temperature. The *Seonggyungwan* — the national academy — was converted into a private pleasure ground. The character for "king" was banned from scholarly use, because Yeonsangun declared that the word itself was an understatement.

Chae-hong watched. She took notes in her mind, because written notes meant execution. She memorized dates, names, the specific progression of atrocities, not because she planned to use the information but because *recording* was the only power available to a woman in her position. If she could not act, she could at least remember.

Then she found the sealing records.

They were in a forgotten compartment of the royal library — a cache of documents that had been hidden rather than destroyed, preserved by some anonymous bureaucrat who had understood that destroyed records create dangerous vacuums. The cache included Jeong In's original notes (or rather, copies — the scholar had secretly duplicated his findings before destroying the originals, because scholars, like bureaucrats, distrust destruction) and the text of Yi Bang's deleted report.

Chae-hong read them by candlelight in a storage room at three in the morning, her hands shaking.

*This blade amplifies the darkness within its holder.*

She looked up from the document and thought about the king. About the dead scholars and the terrorized court and the kingdom that was slowly, systematically being dismembered by a man who sat on a golden throne with an ancient sword across his knees and called out the names of ghosts.

And she thought: *Someone has to take it from him.*

---

### *Seoul, Present Day*

"Joseon court lady records," Jin-woo said.

Su-hyun blinked. They were in her office — a tornado of books and papers that she navigated with the unconscious ease of a person who had stopped seeing the chaos years ago. "What about them?"

"Can you access them? Personnel records from the Yeonsangun period?"

"Some of them survived the purges. The *Seungjeongwon Ilgi* — the Royal Secretariat Diaries — have partial attendance records for inner court staff. Why?"

Jin-woo hesitated. The question forming in his mind sounded insane. He knew it sounded insane. He asked it anyway.

"I need you to look for a court lady named Chae-hong."

Su-hyun's pen stopped moving. "Why?"

"I dreamed about her."

"Jin-woo —"

"She looks like you. In my dream, she looks exactly like you."

The silence that followed was the loudest thing Jin-woo had ever heard. Su-hyun stared at him with an expression that contained equal parts concern, fascination, and the specific kind of alarm that scientists feel when experimental results stop making sense.

"The blade is showing you its memories," she said carefully. "Its previous owners, its previous... experiences. And one of those experiences involves a court lady who resembles me."

"Yes."

"And you want me to verify whether this person actually existed."

"Yes."

Su-hyun took off her glasses and rubbed her eyes. When she put them back on, her expression had shifted — the alarm was still there, but it was joined by something else. Something that looked very much like the face of a woman who has just realized that the line between professional interest and personal danger has been crossed.

"I'll look," she said.

---

### *Hanyang, Joseon Dynasty, 1506 CE*

Chae-hong planned for three months.

The blade never left the king's side during the day. At night, it rested on a stand beside his sleeping mat, within arm's reach. The royal chamber was guarded by six men — loyalists handpicked by Yeonsangun from the ranks of soldiers who had proven their willingness to follow orders without moral hesitation.

Chae-hong's plan was simple, because complexity in palace intrigue was a luxury that got people killed. She would wait for the king to drink himself unconscious — which he did with increasing frequency — take the blade, and carry it to the Han River. The blade would go into the water. She would go wherever the aftermath took her, which was almost certainly death.

She did not tell anyone. She did not seek allies. Allies in a tyrant's court were liabilities.

On the night of the new moon — dark, starless, the air heavy with the smell of plum blossoms from the palace gardens — Yeonsangun collapsed in his chamber after consuming two jars of rice wine. Chae-hong entered through the servants' passage, stepping over the sleeping guard (she had added a mild sedative to his evening meal), and approached the blade.

It rested on its stand like a sleeping predator. In the darkness, the bronze core did not shimmer — it pulsed, a slow, rhythmic glow that synchronized with the king's breathing.

Chae-hong reached for the hilt.

The blade was cold. Not the cold of metal in an unheated room — a deeper cold, the cold of something that had been waiting, the cold of patience measured in centuries. She lifted it. It was lighter than she expected — light as a calligraphy brush, light as a promise.

She walked.

Through the inner palace. Past the sleeping guards. Through the courtyards where moonlight should have been but wasn't, the new moon leaving the palace in a darkness so complete that Chae-hong navigated by memory alone — seventeen years of walking these corridors, seventeen years of invisibility put to their ultimate use.

She made it to the outer gate.

The guards there were not sleeping.

---

*This woman's hands are shaking. Her heart is racing. She is more afraid than any warrior who has held me, because warriors are trained to manage fear and this woman is a court lady who pours tea and changes bedding and has never in her life done anything brave.*

*And yet she holds me. And she walks.*

*She does not hear my voices. She does not see my visions. She holds me and feels only the cold and the weight and the terrifying reality of what she is doing.*

*I think — and this is a new thought, a strange thought — I think I admire her.*

---

They caught her on the bridge.

The Seoninmun bridge over the narrow stream that separated the palace from the city — a bridge she had crossed a thousand times, invisible, unnoticed. Tonight she was neither. Two guards materialized from the darkness, their halberds crossing in front of her with a metallic clash that echoed through the silent palace grounds.

"Stop."

Chae-hong stopped. The blade hung at her side, hidden beneath the folds of her outer robe. The guards hadn't seen it. They had seen only a court lady walking where no court lady should walk, at an hour when no court lady should be awake.

"Return to the inner court."

She could have obeyed. She could have tucked the blade back into its folds and returned to the king's chamber and replaced it on its stand and resumed her invisible life, and no one would have known, and nothing would have changed, and the purges would have continued, and the kingdom would have continued to bleed.

Instead, Chae-hong ran.

Not toward the inner court. Toward the river.

The guards shouted. Footsteps behind her — heavy, armored, fast. She was not fast. She was a court lady in formal robes and wooden shoes, running on stone in the dark, and the guards were soldiers trained for exactly this.

She reached the Han River bridge in ninety seconds. The guards were ten paces behind. The river below was black and wide and ancient, flowing past the capital with the indifferent majesty of something that had been here before the city and would be here after.

Chae-hong pulled the blade from her robes. In the starlight, the bronze core caught a faint gleam — a last flicker of the light it had been hoarding.

She threw it.

The blade spun through the air — once, twice — and fell. The splash was small. The river accepted the sword the way it accepted everything: without ceremony, without judgment, without pause.

The guards seized her. She didn't resist. She stood on the bridge with empty hands and an expression of perfect calm and waited for whatever came next.

What came next was execution.

But in the moment — the moment between the throw and the seizure, the moment when the blade was in the air and her hands were free — Chae-hong felt something she had not felt in seventeen years of palace service.

Joy.

---

### *Seoul, Present Day*

Su-hyun's email arrived at midnight.

*Subject: Chae-hong*

*Jin-woo — I found her. Seungjeongwon Ilgi, partial attendance record, year Byeongin (1506): "Court lady Chae-hong, senior attendant, Inner Court, removed from service — cause: executed by royal order." No further details. No record of the offense.*

*She existed. She was real.*

*Jin-woo, how did you know her name?*

He sat at his kitchen table and read the email three times. Outside, the October wind rattled his windows. The blade — recovered from some unknown depth by some unknown hand, centuries after Chae-hong threw it into the Han River — lay on its towel, its bronze core shimmering in the light of his desk lamp.

Su-hyun's face. Chae-hong's face.

The same face.

Jin-woo closed his laptop and looked at the blade.

"Why her?" he asked. "Why show me someone who looks like Su-hyun?"

The blade did not answer. But the shimmer in its core pulsed — once, twice — in a rhythm that felt less like light and more like laughter.

---

*She threw me.*

*Into the river. Into the black, cold, indifferent water.*

*No one has ever thrown me before. Warriors have dropped me. Monks have buried me. Potters have refused me. But no one — no one in twenty-three centuries — has ever thrown me.*

*The court lady. The invisible woman. The one who poured tea and changed bedding and never did anything brave until the night she did the bravest thing I have ever seen a human do.*

*She threw me away and smiled.*

*I think about that smile sometimes. In the dark. In the water. In the long centuries between hands.*

*I think about it because it is the one thing I cannot amplify.*

*Joy is not mine to touch.*

---

*End of Episode 9*
