# MEMORIES OF THE BLADE
## Episode 1: The Gleaming Thing in the Old Shop

---

The alley had no name.

Not officially, anyway. The city planners who had redesigned Euljiro three times in the last forty years had consistently overlooked this particular capillary of cracked asphalt and rusted signage, wedged between a printing shop that hadn't printed anything since 2011 and a wholesale button distributor that appeared to operate exclusively between 2 and 4 AM. The alley smelled of machine oil, sesame, and the particular mustiness of things that have been forgotten on purpose.

Seo Jin-woo loved it here.

He came to Euljiro the way other men went to bars — not to find something specific, but to lose himself in the looking. At thirty-five, Jin-woo had built a quiet reputation as one of Seoul's sharpest freelance antiques appraisers: the man you called when an estate sale turned up something no one could identify, or when a chaebol family needed a discreet valuation of grandfather's collection before the inheritance lawyers descended. He had a good eye. His former colleagues at the National Museum still called him for second opinions.

But they didn't know about Euljiro.

They didn't know that twice a week, sometimes three times, Jin-woo threaded his way through these nameless alleys, past the welding sparks and the clattering metal workshops, past the old men playing *janggi* on upturned crates, searching for something he couldn't name. Something he'd been searching for since he was five years old, when his father walked out of their Gangnam apartment with a leather bag and never came back.

He found the shop on a Tuesday in late October.

Or perhaps — and this was the thought that would keep him awake for many nights to come — the shop found him.

---

"The Room of Time" was what the sign said, though you had to squint past thirty years of grime to read it. The characters were hand-painted in a style that Jin-woo's trained eye placed somewhere around 1985, possibly earlier. The shopfront was barely wide enough for a single person to enter, and the door — a sliding panel of frosted glass in an aluminum frame — resisted his push with the sullen reluctance of something that hadn't been opened in weeks.

Inside, the air was thick.

Not just with dust, though there was plenty of that — the kind that didn't float but *hung*, suspended in amber shafts of light that fell from a single window high on the back wall. The shop was a geological formation of objects. Joseon-era lacquerware sat next to Japanese occupation-period tin signs. A Goryeo celadon fragment (genuine, Jin-woo noted automatically) shared shelf space with a 1970s Seiko alarm clock. A Tang Dynasty bronze mirror — or a very good reproduction — leaned against a stack of mimeographed newspapers from the 1987 pro-democracy protests.

Jin-woo's pulse quickened. This was the kind of place where treasures hid.

"We're closed."

The voice came from behind a wall of stacked wooden crates. Jin-woo peered around them and found an old man sitting in a leather chair that had been patched so many times it resembled a topographical map. White hair. Stooped shoulders. Reading glasses perched on a nose that had been broken at least once. But the eyes — the eyes were something else entirely. Dark, sharp, and disturbingly alert, like a hawk pretending to nap.

"The door was open," Jin-woo said.

"The door is always open. We're still closed."

Jin-woo smiled. He was used to eccentric shopkeepers. "I'm an appraiser. Seo Jin-woo." He held out a business card. The old man didn't take it.

"I know what an appraiser is."

"There's an estate lot auction listed for this address. Tuesday at two. That's today. That's now."

The old man — Mr. Kang, according to the faded business license tacked to the wall behind him — studied Jin-woo for a long moment. Something moved behind those hawk eyes. Recognition? Calculation? Whatever it was, it passed, and the old man sighed the sigh of someone who has just lost a very long argument with the universe.

"Back room," he said. "Second shelf from the bottom. Don't break anything."

---

The back room was worse than the front. Or better, depending on your perspective. Jin-woo had to turn sideways to navigate between towers of boxes, rolled-up scrolls, and what appeared to be an actual suit of Joseon-era armor, dented and listing to one side like a drunk soldier. The air here was cooler, damper, and carried a faint metallic undertone that Jin-woo's subconscious filed away without his conscious mind noticing.

The estate lot was modest. Two shelves of items tagged with small red stickers — the universal language of "make me an offer." A jade *binyeo* hairpin, cracked but genuine. A set of calligraphy brushes in a paulownia wood box. A pair of white porcelain bowls that might be late Joseon. Jin-woo examined each with professional detachment, cataloguing values in his head.

Then he saw the chest.

It was on the floor, half-hidden behind a rolled carpet. Rectangular, perhaps sixty centimeters long, made of unfinished wood so old it had turned the color of storm clouds. No lacquer, no decoration, no hardware — just six planks and a lid held shut by a leather strap that crumbled at Jin-woo's touch.

He lifted the lid.

Inside, nested in a bed of fabric so decayed it might once have been silk or cotton or something else entirely, lay a sword.

It was not beautiful. That was Jin-woo's first thought, and it surprised him, because in his experience, things that survived this long were almost always beautiful — beauty being the primary reason humans bothered to preserve anything. But this blade was corroded, blackened, its edge invisible under a crust of oxidation that had turned the surface into something resembling the bark of a very old tree.

And yet.

Jin-woo reached for it. Later, he would try to explain this moment to Su-hyun and fail completely. It wasn't curiosity, exactly. It wasn't professional instinct. It was something more fundamental — a gravitational pull, as if the blade had mass far exceeding its physical weight, as if it were bending the space around it, drawing his hand forward with the same quiet inevitability as a stone falling.

His left hand closed around the hilt.

---

*I had slept for so long.*

*Darkness. Silence. The slow erosion of centuries pressing down like earth on a coffin. And then — warmth. Pressure. The unmistakable signature of a human hand.*

*I remember hands. I remember all of them.*

*This one trembles.*

---

The world inverted.

Jin-woo was no longer in the back room of an Euljiro antique shop. He was standing on a plain — vast, treeless, under a sky the color of a bruise. The ground beneath his feet was churned mud, and the mud was red, and the redness was blood, and the blood was everywhere — pooling in bootprints, smeared across the faces of men who lay twisted in the attitudes of violent death.

*Sound.* A wall of it. Metal striking metal. The meaty impact of edged weapons hitting flesh. War drums — deep, arrhythmic, primal — pounding a tempo that synced with his heartbeat and then exceeded it. Screaming. Not the theatrical screaming of movies but the raw, animal screaming of human beings in extremis — high-pitched, ragged, continuous, like a frequency the body produces only when the mind has abandoned it.

*Smell.* Iron. Not the clean iron of a hardware store but the wet, organic iron of blood exposed to air. Smoke — not wood smoke but the acrid, greasy smoke of burning flesh and hair. And underneath, something else: the dry mineral smell of a forge, the whoosh of bellows, the hiss of hot metal quenched in water.

Jin-woo tried to scream. His mouth opened but no sound came out. The battlefield swam around him, figures rushing past — men in armor he couldn't identify, carrying weapons from no era he recognized — and through the chaos, a single image crystallized with hallucinatory clarity: a forge. A stone forge in the side of a mountain. A man with burn-scarred arms working a crucible of molten metal that glowed not orange but *white*, a white so pure it seemed to have its own gravitational field, pulling light toward it, pulling everything toward it —

Pain.

Real pain. In his hand.

Jin-woo gasped and dropped the sword. It clattered against the wooden floor with a sound that was too loud, too *resonant*, for a piece of corroded metal. He staggered backward, knocking over a stack of boxes, and sat down hard on the concrete floor, breathing in shallow, ragged gasps.

His left palm was burning.

He turned his hand over. There, seared into the meat of his palm below the thumb, was a mark. Not a cut, not a bruise — a *burn*. A small, rough oval, perhaps two centimeters across, the skin already blistering, as if he had pressed his hand against a stovetop. The burn was hot to the touch and throbbed with a dull, persistent ache that seemed to pulse in time with something Jin-woo couldn't hear.

"I told you."

Mr. Kang stood in the doorway. His arms were crossed. His face was unreadable, but his eyes — those hawk eyes — held something that looked very much like grief.

"Don't touch that sword."

---

"What is it?"

Jin-woo was sitting in Mr. Kang's leather chair now, his burned hand wrapped in a wet cloth the old man had produced from somewhere. The sword lay on the floor of the back room where he'd dropped it. Neither of them had gone near it since.

"A sword," Mr. Kang said. He was making tea. His hands, Jin-woo noticed, were steady.

"That's not what I mean."

"I know what you mean."

"Then answer me."

Mr. Kang poured the tea. It was barley tea, the cheap kind you could buy at any convenience store, served in a cup that was either a priceless example of Joseon white porcelain or a very good forgery. He set the cup in front of Jin-woo without meeting his eyes.

"Some things," Mr. Kang said, "are better left in their boxes."

"I had a hallucination. A vivid, multisensory hallucination. And a physical burn appeared on my hand from touching a piece of metal. That's not — that doesn't happen."

"And yet."

"You know something about this sword."

It wasn't a question. Jin-woo had spent fifteen years reading people across negotiating tables — dealers, collectors, heirs trying to inflate the value of grandfather's junk. He could read a lie the way a conductor reads a score. And Mr. Kang wasn't lying, exactly. He was *withholding*, which was different and, in Jin-woo's experience, usually more dangerous.

Mr. Kang was silent for a long time. The shadows in the shop had lengthened; the single shaft of window light had climbed the wall and now illuminated a row of old photographs — the shop in its youth, the alley before redevelopment, a younger Mr. Kang with hair that was black instead of white, standing next to —

Jin-woo's breath caught.

Standing next to a man who looked very much like Jin-woo himself.

"How much?" Jin-woo said.

Mr. Kang blinked. "What?"

"The sword. How much for the estate lot?"

"You don't want that sword."

"Name a price."

The old man's jaw tightened. He looked at Jin-woo — really looked, the way you look at someone when you're trying to see past the face to the machinery behind it — and whatever he saw made him close his eyes briefly. When he opened them, the hawk was gone. What remained was something older and sadder.

"Three hundred thousand won," he said. "For the lot."

Jin-woo paid in cash.

---

He carried the chest home on the subway. It sat on his lap like a sleeping child, lighter than it should have been, and no one paid it any attention, which struck Jin-woo as absurd. He was carrying something that had just given him a hallucination and a second-degree burn, something that an old man with hawk eyes had tried to warn him away from, something that — he was increasingly certain — was very, very old and very, very wrong.

And the evening commuters on Line 2 scrolled their phones and dozed and didn't notice a thing.

His apartment was a one-bedroom in Mapo-gu, sparsely furnished in the way of a man who hadn't yet decided whether he was going to stay. Books dominated every surface — art history, Korean history, metallurgy, a few novels he'd been meaning to read for years. The only decoration was a framed photograph on the desk: a man in his thirties, smiling, holding a toddler on his knee. The man's face was Jin-woo's face, twenty years older and infinitely happier.

Jin-woo set the chest on the kitchen table. He stared at it for a long time. Then he went to the bathroom, cleaned and bandaged his burned palm, took two ibuprofen, poured himself a whiskey, and sat back down.

He opened the chest.

The blade lay as he'd left it: corroded, dark, inert. In the fluorescent light of his kitchen, it looked like nothing. A piece of junk. The hallucination, the burn, the old man's warning — all of it seemed faintly ridiculous now, the way a nightmare seems ridiculous when the lights are on.

Jin-woo put on latex gloves — a habit from his museum days — and lifted the blade out.

No hallucination this time. No burning. The blade was simply heavy in his hands, the weight of old metal, dense and cool. He laid it on a clean towel and began to work.

He started with a soft-bristle brush, removing the loose oxidation. Then a diluted solution of citric acid, applied with cotton swabs, working in small sections. The corrosion came away in flakes, revealing the steel beneath — and it was steel, true steel, forged, not cast, which immediately narrowed the date range and raised several questions, because the hilt construction suggested something much older than the Iron Age on the Korean peninsula.

By midnight, he had cleaned a third of the blade. By one AM, he was no longer thinking about date ranges.

He was looking at the engravings.

They were microscopic — not carved but *incised*, with a precision that suggested either extraordinary skill or extraordinary tools. They ran in irregular clusters along both edges of the blade, and they were not uniform. Some resembled Chinese seal script. Others bore a passing resemblance to Sogdian or Aramaic characters. A few were geometric — spirals, crosses, crescents — that could belong to any culture or no culture at all. And some were simply *unknown*: symbols that matched no writing system in Jin-woo's considerable mental catalogue.

He counted eighteen distinct markings.

Jin-woo sat back. The kitchen was very quiet. The only sound was the refrigerator's hum and the distant murmur of late-night traffic on the bridge. He stared at the blade and the blade — cleaned of centuries of grime, its steel catching the light in patterns that seemed almost deliberate — stared back.

*Who made you?* he thought. *Where have you been?*

*And why does it feel like I've seen you before?*

He picked up his phone and scrolled to a contact he hadn't called in over a year: Oh Su-hyun. Professor Oh, officially. K University Department of Korean History. They'd been in the same program, years ago — she'd been his senior by two years, brilliant and blunt, the kind of person who could read a primary source in classical Chinese the way most people read a menu. They'd lost touch. His fault, mostly. Jin-woo had a talent for letting things drift.

He typed a message, attached six close-up photographs of the engravings, and added a single line: *Can you take a look at these?*

Then he waited.

At 1:47 AM, his phone rang.

"Where did you get these?" Su-hyun's voice was tight. The academic detachment she normally wore like armor was cracked, and something else was leaking through — something that sounded very much like the emotion Jin-woo had seen in Mr. Kang's eyes. Not fear, exactly. But the thing that lives next door to fear.

"An antique shop in Euljiro. Estate lot. Why?"

"Jin-woo." A pause. He heard her breathing. "These markings. I can't be sure without seeing the original, but the script types, the patination patterns in the photos, the incision technique — I've seen comparable examples in exactly two places. Bronze Age archaeological sites in Liaoning Province, and the earliest Gojoseon bronzeware in the National Museum's restricted collection."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning these engravings — or at least some of them — are pre-Christian era. Possibly three centuries before Christ. Possibly older."

The kitchen seemed to contract around him. The refrigerator hummed. The traffic murmured. The blade lay on its towel, catching light it shouldn't have been able to catch at that angle, and for a moment — just a moment, a flicker so brief Jin-woo would later convince himself he'd imagined it — one of the engravings seemed to pulse.

A spiral. The first marking. The oldest one.

It glowed, and then it didn't, and the kitchen was just a kitchen again.

"Jin-woo?" Su-hyun's voice, distant and urgent. "Jin-woo, are you still there?"

He looked at the burn on his palm. It throbbed.

"Yeah," he said. "I'm here."

---

*He is here.*

*After so long, he is here.*

*His hand is the right shape. His blood runs at the right temperature. And his eyes — I know those eyes. I have seen them before, in another face, in another century.*

*He will try to understand me. They always do.*

*The question is whether I will let him.*

---

Outside Jin-woo's window, Seoul hummed and glittered in the October dark — ten million lives stacked in concrete and glass, oblivious to the fact that in a one-bedroom apartment in Mapo-gu, a man was holding a blade that remembered everything.

And would forget nothing.

---

*End of Episode 1*
