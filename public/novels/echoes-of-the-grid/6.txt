# MEMORIES OF THE BLADE
## Episode 6: The Potter's Hands

---

### *Gangjin, Goryeo Dynasty, 1157 CE*

The fisherman who found the sword didn't know what to do with it.

He'd pulled it from his net along with a catch of yellow croaker and a tangle of kelp, somewhere in the waters south of Gangjin, and his first thought — sensible, practical — was that it was junk. Old metal fouled his nets regularly: anchors, chains, the rusted remnants of ships that the East China Sea had swallowed and occasionally regurgitated. He would have thrown it back if not for the shimmer.

In the gray morning light, with the mist still clinging to the water like gauze, the blade's bronze core caught a shaft of sun and held it — a warm, amber glow that seemed to come from *inside* the metal rather than from the surface. The fisherman stared at it. The light pulsed, faintly, like a heartbeat.

He brought it to the village. No one wanted it. Fishermen were superstitious people — anything pulled from the sea that glowed without reason was either divine or cursed, and in practical terms, the distinction mattered less than the fisherman's desire to get it out of his house. He offered it to the monk at the local temple. The monk refused. He offered it to the blacksmith. The blacksmith took one look at the blade and said, "That metal is wrong," and closed his door.

In the end, he brought it to Yu-gi.

---

Yu-gi was the finest celadon potter in Gangjin, which made him the finest celadon potter in the world.

This was not hyperbole. Gangjin in the twelfth century was the epicenter of an art form that later generations would regard as one of the supreme achievements of Korean civilization: Goryeo celadon, the jade-green glazed pottery whose color — called *bisaek*, "kingfisher green" — was so refined, so ethereally beautiful, that Song Dynasty connoisseurs (who were not easily impressed) declared it "first under heaven."

Yu-gi worked in a workshop at the edge of the kiln district, a cluster of thatched-roof buildings near the river where the finest clay was dug. His hands were his fortune — and they were extraordinary hands, broad and strong and impossibly sensitive, capable of shaping a vessel wall to the thickness of an eggshell and maintaining that thickness across an entire surface by touch alone. His *sanggam* technique — the method of inlaying contrasting clays into incised designs before glazing — was unmatched. Other potters in Gangjin admitted this freely, which in the competitive world of Goryeo ceramics was the equivalent of a standing ovation.

He was fifty years old, unmarried, childless. His work was his family. His kilns were his children. The clay was his conversation partner, and it spoke to him in a language of weight and moisture and texture that he understood more fluently than any human tongue.

When the fisherman brought the sword, Yu-gi was in the middle of decorating a water dropper — a small vessel for an ink stone, shaped like a lotus bud, its surface waiting for the first cuts of the *sanggam* pattern. He almost sent the fisherman away. But the blade caught his eye the way a beautiful glaze catches a potter's eye — a recognition of quality that bypasses conscious thought and speaks directly to the trained hand.

"Leave it," he said.

The fisherman left. Yu-gi picked up the sword.

No hallucination. No burning. No surge of desire or combat skill or bloodlust. Yu-gi held the blade and felt... curiosity. The engravings fascinated him. Tiny, precise, arranged in patterns that reminded him of the decorative bands on ancient bronzeware. He traced them with his fingertip — the spirals, the crosses, the script characters in languages he couldn't read — and felt a vibration so faint it might have been imaginary.

He set the blade on the shelf above his work table, next to a row of finished vessels waiting for firing, and went back to his lotus water dropper.

But his eyes kept returning to the engravings.

---

*Soft hands.*

*Not warrior's hands. Not a merchant's hands. Not a king's or a monk's or a pirate's.*

*Potter's hands. Hands that know clay and water and fire, that shape the formless into the beautiful. These hands will not wield me. These hands will study me.*

*I have been held by warriors and kings. I have never been studied.*

*This is... different.*

---

Over the following weeks, Yu-gi became obsessed — though he would not have used that word — with reproducing the blade's engravings on his pottery.

He began with sketches: meticulous drawings of each marking, catalogued by position on the blade, drawn with the same precision he applied to his ceramic designs. The engravings were extraordinary. Some were clearly functional — identification marks, ownership seals, the kinds of inscriptions a smith or a warrior would carve. But others were decorative — or beyond decorative, transcending utility into something that Yu-gi, with his potter's eye, recognized as *art*.

The spiral, in particular, captivated him. It was the oldest mark on the blade, worn smooth by centuries of handling, but its line remained pure — a single continuous curve that wound inward with the mathematical precision of a nautilus shell. Yu-gi reproduced it on a test tile, carving the design into damp clay with a fine-pointed stylus, and when the tile emerged from the kiln — glazed in the deep kingfisher green of Gangjin's finest work — the spiral seemed to glow with a light that the glaze alone could not explain.

He began incorporating the blade's motifs into his pottery. A celadon wine cup bore the Sogdian warning character, transformed into an abstract pattern. A chrysanthemum vase carried the Hwarang crest in its incised design. A large ceremonial bowl combined all eighteen engravings into a sweeping decorative band that circled the interior rim — a visual record of two thousand years of ownership, translated from iron and bronze into clay and glaze.

Word spread. Other potters came to see. The court in Kaesong heard rumors of a masterwork in Gangjin that bore patterns from "a sword older than the Three Kingdoms."

And eventually, inevitably, the wrong person heard.

---

### *Seoul, Present Day*

Su-hyun found the celadon fragment on a Wednesday.

It was in the National Museum of Korea's special exhibition on Goryeo ceramics — a small, unassuming shard displayed in a case alongside other fragments of exceptional *sanggam* work. The museum's label identified it as "Fragment of a large bowl with unusual inlaid design. Gangjin kiln site. Twelfth century. Artist unknown. Acquired 1962."

Su-hyun stood in front of the case for forty-five minutes.

The *sanggam* pattern on the shard — a section of the decorative band that had once circled the bowl's interior — was an exact match for the engravings on Jin-woo's blade. Not similar. Not related. *Identical.* The spiral was there, and the Sogdian character, and the Hwarang crest, and half a dozen other markings that Su-hyun had spent weeks photographing and cataloguing from the blade's surface.

She called Jin-woo.

"The potter saw the blade," she said, the words tumbling over each other in her excitement. "He saw the engravings and he reproduced them — perfectly, on a masterpiece celadon bowl. The bowl is broken, but a fragment survived. It's in the National Museum. Jin-woo, this is a *second source*. Independent corroboration. The blade's engravings are real — they predate the Goryeo period by centuries, and a twelfth-century master potter documented them in clay."

"Can you photograph the fragment?"

"Already done. I'm sending them now." A pause. "Jin-woo — I also noticed something about your hand. Last time we met. The burn has spread, hasn't it?"

Silence on the line.

"Jin-woo?"

"Yeah. It's spread to my wrist."

"And you're hiding it."

"I'm managing it."

"You're hiding it. There's a difference."

He didn't answer. Su-hyun heard him breathing — slow, controlled, the breathing of someone who is deciding how much truth to share — and then the line went quiet, and in the silence between them, something shifted. A hairline crack in a friendship that had survived years of distance and neglect, opening just wide enough to let in the cold.

"Be careful," she said.

---

### *Gangjin, Goryeo Dynasty, 1158 CE*

Jeong Jung-bu arrived in Gangjin on a horse the color of dried blood, attended by twelve armed men.

He was not yet the man he would become. History would remember him as the leader of the 1170 military coup — the bloody insurrection that overthrew the civilian aristocracy and plunged Goryeo into decades of military rule. But in 1158, he was merely a general with a reputation for cruelty and ambition, a man who collected beautiful things with the acquisitive hunger of someone who had grown up without them and intended to make up for lost time.

He had heard about the sword from a court official who had heard about it from a merchant who had heard about it from a potter's apprentice who talked too much over rice wine. The chain of hearsay was long, but the signal was clear: in Gangjin, there was a potter who possessed a sword older than the Three Kingdoms, and the sword was the source of the patterns that were making his pottery famous.

Jeong wanted the sword. He did not want the pottery. He did not care about art or craft or the subtle beauty of *sanggam* inlay work. He wanted the sword because it was old and valuable and because owning it would give him an edge — literal and figurative — in the complex power games of the Goryeo court.

Yu-gi met him in the workshop.

The general filled the small space the way a boulder fills a stream — immovably, asserting dominance through sheer physical mass. His armor was lacquered black. His men stood behind him like shadows with swords.

"The sword," Jeong said.

"It's not for sale," Yu-gi said.

"I didn't say 'sell.'"

Yu-gi looked at the general. He was not a brave man — he was a potter, a maker of beautiful things, a man whose daily confrontations involved kiln temperatures and glaze chemistry and the eternal war between artistic ambition and clay that had its own ideas about what shape it wanted to take. Physical confrontation was as foreign to him as celadon was to Jeong Jung-bu.

But the blade was *his*. Not because he owned it — he understood, with the intuition of a man who worked with his hands, that the blade could not truly be owned. It was his because it had given him something no patron or market or court could provide: *inspiration*. The patterns on its surface had unlocked a dimension of his art that he hadn't known existed, a vocabulary of ancient symbols that spoke directly to the part of him that dreamed in clay.

"The sword is not a weapon," Yu-gi said carefully. "It's a reference. An artistic source. To a soldier, it's iron and bronze. To me, it's a library."

"I don't need a library."

"Then you don't need the sword."

Jeong's face darkened. He was not accustomed to refusal, and refusal from a *craftsman* — a man who worked with his hands, who smelled of clay and kiln smoke, who ranked below even the lowest-born scholar in the rigid social hierarchy of Goryeo — was not merely insulting. It was incomprehensible.

"I'm going to give you a choice," the general said. He spoke slowly, the way one speaks to a child or an animal. "You can give me the sword. Or I can take the sword and your fingers."

The workshop was very quiet. Outside, the kiln district hummed with the sounds of production — the creak of potter's wheels, the rhythmic thudding of clay being wedged, the distant roar of a kiln at full fire. Inside, the silence was absolute.

Yu-gi looked at his hands. Broad, strong, mapped with the scars and calluses of thirty-five years of work. The hands that had shaped the kingfisher-green miracles that made Gangjin famous. The hands that were his identity, his purpose, his only true possession.

"Take my life," he said. "But don't take my hands. And don't take the sword."

Jeong Jung-bu stared at him. Something moved behind the general's eyes — something that might have been respect, or might have been the flicker of recognition that passes between two men when one understands that the other has found the hill he is willing to die on.

Jeong drew his own sword.

---

*This man — the potter — does not wield me. Does not polish me or sharpen me or practice forms by moonlight. He simply looks at me. Studies the marks that others have carved into my surface with the intensity of a scholar reading scripture.*

*And what he sees, he translates. Into clay. Into color. Into the jade-green fire of his kiln.*

*No one has ever translated me before.*

*It is strange. Being understood is not the same as being used, and I am not certain which I prefer.*

---

Yu-gi died in his workshop on an afternoon in late autumn. The details were not recorded by any official source — Gangjin potters did not warrant entries in the *Goryeosa*, the dynastic history — but the apprentices who found him would later recount the scene in whispers that passed through the kiln district like smoke:

The master, facedown on the earthen floor. His hands unmarked — Jeong, for reasons known only to himself, had honored that part of the request. The sword, gone. And on the work table, still damp, a vessel of extraordinary beauty.

It was a large bowl, wide and shallow, its walls paper-thin, its glaze the deepest kingfisher green the apprentices had ever seen. Around the interior rim, in flawless *sanggam* inlay, every engraving from the blade's surface was reproduced — eighteen marks from eighteen centuries, translated from metal into clay with the precision of a man who understood that his masterwork would be his last.

The bowl survived for eight hundred years before an accident in a museum conservation lab broke it into eleven pieces, of which only three were recovered.

One of them — the fragment bearing the spiral — ended up in a glass case in the National Museum of Korea.

Waiting, as the blade always waited, for someone to notice.

---

### *Seoul, Present Day*

Jin-woo sat at his kitchen table and stared at the photographs Su-hyun had sent. The celadon fragment. The spiral. The impossible, beautiful, heartbreaking proof that the blade's engravings had been documented by a twelfth-century master potter who had died for refusing to give up his source material.

He looked at the blade. The blade looked back.

*What are you?* he thought. Not for the first time.

He unwrapped the bandage on his left hand. The burn had spread again — a dark tendril reaching past his wrist, following the path of a vein, the skin around it taking on the reddish-brown hue of old iron.

Or old blood.

"Beauty feeds on blood," he murmured, quoting the dead potter without knowing it.

The blade shimmered in the kitchen light.

---

*Beauty feeds on blood.*

*The potter said that. He was right, though not in the way he meant. Beauty is not what feeds on blood — I am. But the potter understood something the warriors never did: I am not merely a weapon. I am a record. Every mark on my surface is a story. Every hand that has held me has left its signature.*

*The potter saw this. He saw me not as a thing to be wielded but as a thing to be read.*

*He died for that understanding.*

*I did not kill him. The general killed him. But I was the reason the general came, and the reason the potter would not let go.*

*In this, as in all things, I am the proximate cause but not the final one. The final cause is always human. The hunger is always human.*

*I am only the thing that makes the hunger visible.*

---

*End of Episode 6*
