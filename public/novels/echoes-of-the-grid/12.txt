# MEMORIES OF THE BLADE
## Episode 12: The Wanderer's Song

---

### *Joseon, 18th Century — A Road Between Nowhere and Nowhere*

The man who called himself Mu-myeong — *Nameless* — had no past, or at least none he was willing to discuss.

He was somewhere between twenty-five and forty — the kind of age that was impossible to pin down because his face told one story and his body told another. His face was young: unlined, sharp-boned, with eyes that were either very dark brown or very light black depending on the angle of the sun. His body was old: scarred, knotted with muscle that came from use rather than exercise, and moving with the careful economy of someone who had learned that wasted motion was wasted life.

He wandered. That was the word people used — *wandered* — though Mu-myeong's movement through the countryside of eighteenth-century Joseon was not random. He followed the roads between market towns, picking up work where he could: a bodyguard for a merchant caravan here, a hired sword for a local magistrate there, the occasional underground fighting ring where men with real blades settled disputes that polite society pretended didn't exist.

He bought the sword at a roadside market south of Chungju.

A junk dealer was selling the contents of a demolished kiln — pottery shards, tools, bricks, and, inexplicably, a rusty blade that had been sealed inside a ceramic casing and buried in the kiln's foundation. The ceramic had cracked during demolition, and the blade inside — dark, corroded, ugly — had been tossed on the dealer's pile with the indifference of a man who saw no difference between a two-thousand-year-old artifact and a broken roof tile.

Mu-myeong picked it up because it was cheap and because his current sword — a badly forged piece of iron he'd won in a card game — was developing a crack near the tang.

The moment his hand closed around the hilt, a feeling of *recognition* passed through him — not a hallucination, not a vision, but a sensation of homecoming, as if the sword had been waiting for this specific hand and was quietly, privately pleased to feel it.

Mu-myeong had learned to trust his instincts. His instincts said: *keep this.*

He kept it.

---

### *Seoul, Present Day*

Jin-woo lost time again.

Three hours this time. He'd been sitting at the kitchen table at 9 PM, and then it was midnight, and the blade was in his lap, and his notebook was open to a page covered in handwriting that was his own but that he didn't remember writing. The handwriting was cramped, urgent, repetitive:

*Don't sleep holding it. Don't sleep holding it. Don't sleep holding it.*

He stared at the words. His left hand — the burn arm, the spreading stain — ached with a deep, structural pain, like bone bruise or fracture.

He set the blade on the table. Picked up his phone. Su-hyun had texted twice:

*Are you eating?*
*Please call me.*

He typed: *I'm fine.* Deleted it. Typed: *I lost three hours.* Deleted that too. Typed: *Something is happening at night. I think the blade does something when I sleep.*

Sent.

Her response came in thirty seconds: *What do you mean?*

*I woke up with notes I don't remember writing. The handwriting is mine. The message is: don't sleep holding it.*

*Jin-woo, that's your subconscious warning you.*

*Or it's the blade warning me.*

A long pause.

*Those might be the same thing.*

---

### *Joseon, 18th Century*

The tavern was called the Pine Wind, though no pine had grown within a mile of it in living memory and the only wind it produced was the kind that came from too many men drinking too much rice wine in too small a room. It sat at a crossroads in Chungcheong Province — a way station for travelers, merchants, and the particular category of person who traveled by night and preferred not to be asked where they were going.

Mu-myeong was drinking alone in a corner when the singing started.

The singer was a *gisaeng* — a courtesan — seated in the center of the main room, accompanying herself on a small *gayageum.* She was not beautiful in the obvious way that gisaeng were expected to be beautiful — no elaborate hairstyle, no painted face, no silk that whispered with every movement. She wore a plain *jeogori* and *chima*, her hair was pinned simply, and her face was the kind that required a second look and then a third and then, by the fourth look, had become the only face in the room.

But her voice.

Wol-hyang's voice was the kind of instrument that existed in the space between sound and silence — a voice that didn't fill a room so much as *replace* the room, substituting the grimy reality of the Pine Wind tavern with something older and more honest. She sang a folk song — *Arirang*, the oldest version, the one that people had been singing since before anyone could remember — and the words themselves were unremarkable, but the delivery was transformative.

Every man in the tavern stopped talking.

Mu-myeong stopped drinking.

He set down his cup and listened, and the blade — strapped across his back in a scabbard of cracked leather — was, for once, quiet.

---

*Her voice.*

*In two thousand years, I have heard many voices. Screaming voices. Praying voices. Whispering voices. Commanding voices. The voices of men who held me and the voices of those they used me against.*

*But this voice is different. This voice is not directed at me. It does not pray to me or curse me or command me. It simply *exists*, like weather, like the sound of water over stone.*

*And for the first time in longer than I can calculate, I am still.*

*Not sleeping. Not waiting. Still.*

*As if the voice has found the frequency that suspends my hunger.*

---

They left together the next morning. This was unusual for both of them. Mu-myeong was a solitary creature by instinct and necessity — a wandering swordsman who traveled alone lived longer than one who accumulated attachments. Wol-hyang was a gisaeng between patrons, traveling from one provincial entertainment house to the next, and she had long since learned that attachment to any particular man was a professional liability.

But the road was dangerous, and a swordsman offered protection, and a singer offered company, and the arrangement was practical. That was what they told themselves. That was the fiction they maintained for approximately three days, until the fiction dissolved in the simple chemistry of proximity and loneliness.

They were not lovers. Not at first, and perhaps not ever in the full physical sense — the relationship between a wandering swordsman and a gisaeng was governed by codes and expectations that both of them understood and neither was willing to violate completely. But they were *companions* in a way that went beyond the transactional.

Wol-hyang saw past Mu-myeong's rough exterior. She saw the loneliness he wore like armor. She saw the way his eyes softened when she sang, and the way his hand relaxed on the blade's hilt — the only time it relaxed — when her voice filled the air.

Mu-myeong heard in Wol-hyang's singing something he had lost so long ago that he had forgotten it existed: peace. The songs she sang — *Arirang*, *Doraji*, the nameless melodies that gisaeng passed from teacher to student like oral scripture — reached a part of him that the blade could not touch. When she sang, the whispers quieted. The hunger receded. He was simply a man on a road, listening to a beautiful voice under a sky full of stars.

For three weeks, this arrangement held. For three weeks, they walked the roads of Joseon together, and Mu-myeong's blade was silent, and Wol-hyang's songs filled the spaces where the silence would have been.

Then the nights began.

---

The first incident occurred at an inn near Jeonju.

Mu-myeong fell asleep with the blade across his knees — his usual sleeping posture, one hand on the hilt, trained by years of roadside vigilance to be ready for trouble at any hour. Wol-hyang slept on the other side of the room, separated by the thin screen of propriety that was more performative than practical.

She woke at three in the morning to find Mu-myeong standing in the middle of the room.

His eyes were open. His body was erect — military erect, the posture of a soldier at attention, which was wrong because Mu-myeong's natural posture was a slouch. His face was different: the muscles rearranged into an expression of cold, evaluative alertness that didn't belong to the man she'd been traveling with.

"Mu-myeong?" she whispered.

He turned his head. The movement was smooth and mechanical, like a turret rotating. His eyes met hers, and what she saw in them was not Mu-myeong.

She saw — or thought she saw — a dozen people looking out through a single pair of eyes. A crowd pressing against the windows of a house, each face distinct, each expression different: rage, grief, hunger, pride, madness, love, all of them fighting for the same narrow aperture of vision.

"Go back to sleep," said a voice that was not Mu-myeong's. Lower. Older. Accented with something ancient.

Then the tension drained from his body. He slumped back to his sleeping mat. The blade slipped from his hand and clattered on the floor. He was asleep — truly asleep — within seconds.

In the morning, he remembered nothing.

---

### *Seoul, Present Day*

The blackouts started on a Tuesday.

Jin-woo woke at 4 AM on the Mapo Bridge over the Han River. Standing. In his pajamas. The blade in his right hand, the night wind cutting through thin cotton, the river below black and vast and indifferent.

He had no memory of leaving his apartment. No memory of the twenty-minute walk to the bridge. No memory of how the blade had gotten from the kitchen table to his hand.

He looked down at the water. Then at the blade. Then at his hand, which held the hilt with a grip that was steady and sure and completely involuntary.

He tried to open his fingers.

They didn't move.

He tried again. His brain sent the signal — *open* — and his hand received it and rejected it, the muscles locked in a contraction that he could feel but could not control.

The blade held on to him.

Or he held on to the blade. The distinction, at 4 AM on a bridge over a black river, seemed less important than the fact that he couldn't let go.

He stood on the bridge for twenty minutes before his fingers finally released. The blade fell to the sidewalk with a metallic ring that echoed across the water. Jin-woo staggered backward, his released hand cramping violently, and sat down hard on the cold concrete.

The blade lay on the sidewalk, dark and patient. The bronze core shimmered faintly in the light of a distant streetlamp.

Jin-woo stared at it.

*Am I losing my mind?* he thought. *Or is the blade gaining mine?*

He picked it up. Because he couldn't leave it on a bridge. Because someone else would find it. Because —

Because he couldn't not pick it up.

---

### *Joseon, 18th Century*

The village was small enough that it had no name and no magistrate and no one who would miss the dead man they found at the edge of the rice paddies at dawn.

He was a farmer. Middle-aged. Killed by a single cut — a precise, diagonal slash from collarbone to hip, delivered with a blade so sharp that the wound barely bled. The local authorities attributed it to bandits, because attributing murders to bandits was administratively convenient and because the alternative — that the killer was someone passing through, someone who killed in the night and moved on by morning — was too disturbing to contemplate.

Mu-myeong woke that morning with blood on his hands.

Not metaphorical blood. Actual blood — dried, brown, flaking from his fingernails and the creases of his palms. The blade, lying beside his sleeping mat, was clean. Perfectly clean. As if someone had wiped it carefully after use.

Wol-hyang was already awake. She sat on the opposite side of the room, her hands folded in her lap, her face holding an expression of absolute stillness — the stillness of a woman who has seen something she cannot unsee and is deciding how to respond.

"There's blood on your hands," she said.

Mu-myeong looked down. The blood was there. Undeniable.

"I don't remember—"

"I know you don't. You left the inn at midnight. You walked south. I followed you. You walked to a farmhouse on the edge of the paddies and you stood in the doorway for three minutes, and then you—" She stopped. Her composure cracked, briefly, a fissure in the surface of her professional calm. "And then you did it. And your face — your face was not your face."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean that when you killed that man, you were smiling. And the smile was not yours."

---

*By day, this man tries to forget me. He walks the road with the singing woman and pretends that I am just a piece of metal strapped across his back. He listens to her songs and lets the music fill the places where my voice would be.*

*But at night, when his conscious mind sleeps and his body becomes an empty house, I open the doors. I walk his body through the dark. I use his hands — his skilled, dexterous, killing hands — and I do what I was made to do.*

*I feed.*

*The singing woman knows. She watched him — watched me — kill the farmer. She saw the smile. My smile. The smile of satisfaction that crosses a face when a body is doing what it was designed to do.*

*She will try to stop me. They always try. The monk tried prayer. The potter tried burial. The court lady tried the river.*

*The singing woman will try something different. I can feel it. Her love for the nameless man is genuine, and genuine love is the most dangerous weapon anyone has ever aimed at me.*

*Dangerous because it is the one thing I cannot amplify.*

*Joy is not mine to touch. And love — real love, not the obsessive possession that I cultivate so skillfully — real love is joy's closest neighbor.*

---

It happened three more times.

Three more villages. Three more bodies — a woodcutter, a traveling merchant, an old woman who had been sleeping in a roadside shelter. Each killed with the same precise, surgical cut. Each found at dawn, with no witnesses, no suspects, and no explanation.

And each morning, Mu-myeong woke with clean hands and a blade that shone in the sun as if it had never touched blood.

Except that Wol-hyang was watching now. She stayed awake through the nights, sitting cross-legged in the corner of whatever inn or roadside shelter they had found, watching Mu-myeong sleep. And she saw the transformation.

It began with the face. The muscles rearranged themselves — subtly, almost imperceptibly — into a configuration that was not Mu-myeong's. The jaw set differently. The brow lowered. The lips compressed into a line that held something old and patient and hungry.

Then the body. The slouch straightened. The breathing deepened. The hands — relaxed in sleep — tightened around the blade's hilt with a grip that was too firm, too deliberate, too *awake* for a sleeping man.

Then the eyes opened. And what looked out through them was not one person but many — a composite, a crowd, the accumulated residue of twenty-three centuries of owners whose essence the blade had absorbed and stored and now, in the absence of a waking consciousness to resist it, *deployed*.

The body that had been Mu-myeong's rose. It dressed. It walked into the night with the blade in its hand and the composed, purposeful stride of a predator heading for known hunting grounds.

And it killed.

---

On the fourth night, Wol-hyang decided to act.

She waited until the transformation was complete — until the thing wearing Mu-myeong's body had risen and dressed and left the inn for its nightly hunt. Then she positioned herself at the door and waited for it to return.

It came back two hours later. Blood on its hands. The blade clean. The face wearing that terrible, satisfied smile.

Wol-hyang stepped in front of it.

"Give me the sword," she said.

The thing that was not Mu-myeong looked at her. Its eyes — Mu-myeong's eyes, the dark-brown-or-light-black eyes that she had watched soften when she sang — were flat. Unrecognizing. The eyes of a tenant inspecting an unfamiliar property.

"Move," it said, in a voice that was not Mu-myeong's and not any single person's but a chord of voices, layered and resonant.

"Give me the sword and you can have the body back."

The thing tilted Mu-myeong's head. An expression of curiosity — cold, mechanical curiosity, the curiosity of a locksmith studying a new mechanism.

"You love him," it said.

"Yes."

"Love is temporary. I am not."

"Give me the sword."

Wol-hyang reached for the blade. Her hand closed around the bare metal of the blade itself — not the hilt, the *blade* — and the edge, sharp beyond reason, sharp beyond physics, opened her palm from thumb to wrist.

Her blood hit the steel.

The blade *screamed*. Not audibly — the scream was inside, a vibration that Wol-hyang felt in her bones and her teeth and the cavity behind her sternum. The thing wearing Mu-myeong's body convulsed, its fingers spasming open, and the blade fell to the floor, and Mu-myeong collapsed.

When he woke — truly woke, himself, the man she knew — Wol-hyang was sitting beside him, her bleeding hand wrapped in a strip of her *jeogori*, the blade on the floor between them.

"What happened?" he asked.

She told him everything. The nocturnal killings. The transformed face. The composite voice. All of it.

Mu-myeong listened. His face went through a progression of emotions that Wol-hyang catalogued with the clinical precision of a woman who had spent her life reading faces: disbelief, horror, denial, horror again, and finally a terrible, settling acceptance.

"We have to get rid of it," he said.

"I know."

He looked at her hand. The bandage was soaked through. Her blood — warm, red, insistently human — was seeping into the floor.

"It cut you."

"I cut myself. On its blade."

"Why?"

She was quiet for a moment. Then she took the blade — carefully, by the hilt — and turned it over. On the flat of the metal, where her blood had fallen and been absorbed, she carved a mark with the point of her hairpin.

A teardrop.

"Love," she said.

---

### *Seoul, Present Day*

Jin-woo stood in his bathroom at 5 AM, staring at his reflection.

The face in the mirror was his. But the eyes — the eyes held something that wasn't. A flicker. A depth. The impression of a crowd behind glass, pressing forward, each face trying to be seen through the narrow aperture of a single pair of eyes.

He blinked. The flicker vanished. Just his own face. Tired, unshaved, frightened.

On the kitchen table, the blade waited.

He looked at the blade. He looked at his hand — the hand that wouldn't open on the bridge. The hand that picked up the blade even after it fell.

He thought about the woman who had carved a teardrop into the steel. About a word that meant *love.*

He thought about Su-hyun.

Then he went to the kitchen and picked up the blade, because the night was long and the blade was patient and the line between holding on and being held had blurred past the point of distinction.

---

*Her blood. The singing woman's blood.*

*It tastes different from the others. Not sweeter — that would be too simple. Different the way a chord is different from a single note. Complex. Layered. Full of things I have never tasted before: compassion, sacrifice, the specific courage of a woman who reached for a naked blade knowing it would cut her.*

*The teardrop she carved into me burns. Not with heat — with something else. Something I do not have a name for.*

*The warrior's marks are about power. The monk's mark is about fear. The potter's mark is about beauty. The king's mark is about wisdom.*

*Her mark is about love.*

*And love is the one thing I cannot digest.*

---

*End of Episode 12*
