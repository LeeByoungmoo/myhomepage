# MEMORIES OF THE BLADE
## Episode 7: Under the Black Flag

---

### *The Southern Coast of Goryeo, 1380 CE*

The pirates called themselves *waegu* — "Japanese marauders" — though the reality was more complicated than the name suggested. Some were Japanese. Others were Goryeo renegades who had found more profit in raiding their own coast than defending it. A few were Chinese, or Jurchen, or simply men of no fixed nationality who had discovered that the sea had fewer laws than the land and that the boundary between a pirate and an entrepreneur was largely a matter of who was writing the history.

Their lord — the one they followed with the absolute loyalty of men who have chosen their master and would die before choosing again — was a man called Kuro.

Kuro was Japanese. This much was certain, though little else about him was. He was tall for a man of his era — nearly six feet — with a lean, predatory build and a face that would have been handsome if it hadn't been ruined by a deep scar that ran from his left temple to the corner of his mouth, the souvenir of an encounter with a Goryeo coastal garrison that had left six of Kuro's men dead and Kuro himself with a permanent expression of sardonic amusement, as if the universe had told him a joke he wasn't sure he appreciated.

He found the blade in the ruins of a temple on Geoje Island.

Jeong Jung-bu had taken the sword from the potter Yu-gi two centuries earlier, and what happened to it in the intervening years was recorded in fragments — the owners' journal that Jin-woo had found in Mr. Kang's basement listed seven names between Yu-gi and Kuro, each entry briefer and more desperate than the last. The blade had passed from general to courtier to monk to thief, each owner holding it for a shorter period, as if the sword's appetite were accelerating. The last owner before Kuro — a disgraced Goryeo nobleman — had sealed the blade in a temple on Geoje and ordered the monks to pray over it continuously.

The monks had stopped praying when the pirates burned the temple.

Kuro pulled the blade from the rubble of the altar, and the moment iron touched flesh, he felt what every previous owner had felt: the surge. The power. The whispering certainty that the world was his to cut, and that the sword in his hand was the instrument of a destiny he hadn't known he possessed.

Within a month, he was the most feared pirate lord on the southern coast. Within a year, no ship sailed between Goryeo, Japan, and Song without calculating the risk of encountering Kuro's black-flagged fleet.

But Kuro was not Bo-san. Where the Goryeo pirate king had been bombastic and visible, Kuro was surgical and quiet. He didn't display his power — he *deployed* it, with the calculated precision of a strategist who understood that fear was a more efficient weapon than violence.

The blade was his secret. He refitted it with a Japanese-style hilt — wrapped sharkskin and silk cord, the *tsuka* of a proper katana — and wore it on his left hip, concealed beneath a cloak. No one outside his inner circle knew that Kuro's invincibility stemmed from an ancient blade rather than natural talent.

No one except the man hunting him.

---

### *Seoul, Present Day*

Jin-woo was cleaning the blade when he found the metalwork.

He had been examining the hilt assembly — the point where the blade's tang met its handle — when a microscopic detail caught his eye. The handle wrapping was modern: leather, machine-stitched, probably twentieth century. Beneath it, the tang bore tool marks consistent with at least two previous handle replacements. But the oldest marks — nearly invisible, buried under layers of patina — were distinctly Japanese.

"Someone replaced the handle in a Japanese style," he told Su-hyun over the phone. "The tool marks are consistent with traditional *tsukamaki* — katana handle-wrapping. Probably fourteenth or fifteenth century."

"That matches the journal entries. There's a sequence of owners between Goryeo and early Joseon that includes—" Paper rustling. "—a pirate referred to only as 'the Foreign Lord.' The entry is in classical Chinese, and it's unusually detailed. Whoever wrote it was clearly terrified."

"What does it say?"

"Roughly: 'The Foreign Lord took the blade from the temple ruins. He fitted it in the manner of his people. With it, he terrorized the coast for three years, until the naval officer Yi Bang reclaimed it in the Battle of Jindo Strait. Yi Bang submitted a report on the blade to the new dynasty's founders. The report was erased from the records.'"

"Erased."

"Deliberately. Whoever founded the Joseon Dynasty — and we're talking about people who kept *obsessively* detailed records of everything — made a conscious decision to remove all mention of this blade from official history."

Jin-woo was silent for a moment. Through the window of his apartment, the lights of Mapo-gu glittered in the autumn dark.

"Su-hyun. Someone called me last night."

"Called you?"

"An unknown number. Male voice. Educated, calm. Very calm. He said one thing: 'Return the blade.'"

"What did you say?"

"Nothing. The line went dead."

"Did you trace it?"

"I called the police. They ran the number. It doesn't exist. No carrier, no registration. The call left no record on my phone's log — I only know it happened because I was awake when it came in."

The silence on the line had a different quality now. Heavier.

"Jin-woo," Su-hyun said carefully, "how would anyone know you have the blade?"

"I don't know."

"Mr. Kang?"

"Mr. Kang is gone."

"Then who?"

The blade lay on his kitchen table, its bronze core catching the light. Jin-woo stared at it.

"I think," he said slowly, "there are people who've been tracking this blade for a very long time. Longer than us. And they want it back."

---

### *The Southern Coast of Goryeo, 1383 CE*

Yi Bang was not an impressive man.

This was his greatest asset. Goryeo naval officers in the late fourteenth century were, as a rule, either aristocratic dilettantes who owed their commissions to family connections, or grizzled veterans who had survived long enough to become cynical. Yi Bang was neither. He was a quiet, methodical man from a minor military family in Chungcheong Province, and his rise through the ranks owed nothing to brilliance or connections and everything to the unglamorous virtue of doing his job well.

He organized convoys. He maintained ships. He drilled his crews until their maneuvers were reflexive. He studied the coast's tidal patterns, weather cycles, and the behavioral tendencies of specific pirate captains with the patience of a man cataloguing insects. He was, in short, boring — and in a profession where boring men survived and exciting men got their ships burned, this was no small advantage.

His assignment to the anti-pirate task force came with minimal ceremony and maximal expectations. The court in Kaesong was collapsing — the Goryeo dynasty was in its final years, consumed by factional warfare and Mongol interference — and the coastal raids were bleeding what remained of the kingdom's commerce. "Find Kuro. Stop him. You have six ships and four months."

Yi Bang spent three of those months doing nothing visible. He anchored his fleet in Jindo Strait and waited. His subordinates grew restless. The court sent terse dispatches demanding action. Yi Bang read them, filed them, and continued waiting.

He was not idle. He was *listening*. Fishermen who passed through the strait were questioned — gently, conversationally, the way Yi Bang did everything — about Kuro's patterns. Where did the black-flagged fleet resupply? Which islands did they use for shelter? What time of day did they attack? How did Kuro fight?

The last question produced the most interesting answers. Every fisherman, every captured pirate, every coastal villager who had survived a raid described Kuro's combat in the same bewildered terms: "He fights like no one I've ever seen." "His sword moves before the enemy moves." "It's as if he knows what you're going to do before you do it."

Yi Bang, who did not believe in supernatural swords, noted these reports with professional interest. A warrior who appeared to anticipate his opponents might simply be reading body language at a subconscious level — a skill that could be developed through years of combat experience. Or he might have superior intelligence, allowing him to predict tactical movements in advance.

Or — and this was the possibility Yi Bang circled back to at three in the morning, alone in his cabin with maps spread across the table — or the reports were literal, and Kuro possessed something that gave him an advantage that skill alone could not explain.

---

*A new one watches from the strait. A quiet man. A patient man.*

*The quiet ones are the most dangerous. The loud ones — the pirates, the generals, the kings — they burn bright and fast and leave nothing behind but ash and legend. The quiet ones observe. They calculate. They wait.*

*I do not yet know if this quiet man will hold me. But I know he is coming.*

*They always come.*

---

The Battle of Jindo Strait lasted four hours.

Yi Bang chose his moment with the mathematical precision of a man who had spent three months calculating exactly this: a narrow stretch of water between the mainland and Jindo Island, where the tidal current ran so fast that ships caught in it at the wrong phase could not maneuver. He positioned his fleet at the northern end of the strait and waited for Kuro's ships to enter during the ebb tide.

Kuro realized the trap too late. His fleet — eleven ships, carrying perhaps three hundred men — entered the strait in pursuit of a decoy merchant vessel and found Yi Bang's six warships blocking the exit. The tidal current was already pulling them south, into the narrowest part of the channel, where there was no room to turn and no wind to sail against.

The battle was brutal. Yi Bang's ships closed to boarding distance and his marines — well-drilled, well-armed, and fighting with the cold proficiency of men who had been told exactly what to do and in what order — swarmed the pirate vessels. Kuro's men fought with the desperate fury of cornered animals, but desperation is no substitute for discipline, and one by one, the pirate ships were overwhelmed.

Kuro's flagship was the last to fall. Yi Bang boarded it personally — not out of bravado but because his intelligence suggested that Kuro would not surrender to a subordinate, and a dead pirate lord was less useful than a captured one.

He found Kuro on the foredeck, the ancient blade in his hand.

The pirate lord was wounded — a deep cut across his left thigh that had soaked his leggings with blood — but he fought with an intensity that made Yi Bang's marines hesitate. His swordsmanship was unlike anything they had trained for: fluid, unpredictable, drawing from fighting styles that spanned centuries and continents. Two marines went down before Yi Bang ordered the rest back and stepped forward himself.

They fought for seven minutes.

It was the longest seven minutes of Yi Bang's life. Kuro's blade — the ancient blade, the blade of Dal-sol and Yeonhwa and Seol-rang and Jang Bo-san — moved with a speed and intelligence that transcended the man wielding it. Yi Bang parried, dodged, retreated, analyzed. He could feel the rhythm of Kuro's technique — no, the *blade's* technique — and beneath the rhythm, a pattern. The blade favored certain angles, certain timings. It drew from a library of combat experience, but the library had gaps: styles that had been forgotten, counters that had evolved.

Yi Bang found one.

A simple feint — a false opening on the right side, exactly the kind of invitation that the blade's pre-programmed combat instincts would punish with a lateral cut. The cut came. Yi Bang was already moving left, below the blade's arc, and his own sword — standard Goryeo military issue, nothing supernatural about it — connected with Kuro's wrist.

The pirate's fingers opened. The blade clattered to the deck.

Kuro looked at the fallen weapon. Then at Yi Bang. His expression was not defeat — it was something stranger, something almost like relief.

"Take it," Kuro said. His voice was hoarse. "And do something I couldn't. Put it down."

---

Yi Bang did not put it down. Not immediately.

He picked up the blade from the bloodied deck and felt — *something*. A tremor. A warmth. A whisper at the edge of hearing that said, in a voice that had no language, *I know you. I've been waiting for you.*

He sheathed the blade and carried it to his cabin and did not touch it again for three days. On the third day, he carved a mark into the flat of the blade — crossed swords, the emblem of Goryeo naval service — and wrote a detailed report.

The report described the blade's apparent antiquity. Its unusual metallurgy. The testimony of captured pirates regarding its combat-enhancing properties. And Yi Bang's own assessment, delivered in the measured, understated prose of a career military officer who understood that credibility was more important than drama:

*This weapon is unlike any sword in the Goryeo arsenal or any foreign arsenal known to this officer. Its influence on its holder appears to transcend physical properties. I recommend that it be sealed by the state and kept under permanent guard.*

He submitted the report to the newly established Joseon court — the Goryeo dynasty had finally collapsed, and the new kingdom was still sorting its records — and waited for a response.

None came.

When Yi Bang inquired, he was told the report had been received, reviewed, and — by order of a court official whose name was not disclosed — destroyed.

The blade was placed in the Royal Armory.

The record was erased.

As if neither had ever existed.

---

### *Seoul, Present Day*

Jin-woo played the phone call back in his memory for the hundredth time.

*Return the blade.*

Four syllables. A male voice — Korean, educated, the kind of voice that suggested expensive schooling and a life spent in rooms where decisions were made. No threat in the tone. No urgency. Just a statement of expectation, delivered with the quiet confidence of someone who was accustomed to being obeyed.

He hadn't slept properly since the call. Every sound in his apartment — the creak of settling wood, the hum of the refrigerator, the distant rumble of traffic — registered as a potential threat. He'd installed a new deadbolt on his front door. He'd started carrying the blade with him, wrapped in cloth, in a duffel bag that went everywhere he went.

The irony was not lost on him. He was becoming like the owners in the journal — possessive, paranoid, unable to separate his identity from the blade's presence.

Su-hyun had noticed. Of course she had. She noticed everything, with the relentless observational precision of a historian trained to read truth between the lines of official records.

"You're carrying it with you now," she said. It wasn't a question.

They were in the university cafeteria again. The blade — in its duffel bag — sat under the table between Jin-woo's feet, the way a parent keeps a child close in a crowd.

"Someone is looking for it. I can't leave it unguarded."

"Or you can't leave it."

The distinction hung in the air between them.

---

*They fight over me. It doesn't matter who wins. In the end, only one hand holds me.*

*The naval officer carved crossed swords into my surface and wrote a report that was erased from history. He understood what I was — or came close to understanding. He recommended I be sealed.*

*But sealed things are unsealed. Buried things are unearthed. Erased records leave ghosts in the margins.*

*I am very good at leaving ghosts.*

---

*End of Episode 7*
