# MEMORIES OF THE BLADE
## Episode 5: The Pirate King's Tooth

---

### *Gyeongju, Unified Silla — 300 Years Later*

The tomb raiders came in February, when the ground was frozen hard enough to muffle the sound of their shovels.

There were three of them: two brothers from a fishing village near Ulsan and a Buddhist novice who had been expelled from his monastery for stealing altar silver. The novice — a weasel-faced man named Gwi — had spent his years at the monastery memorizing the old records that pilgrims brought from the capital, and among those records was a fragment of a text that described a Hwarang warrior burying "a sword of terrible power" in a cave on Namsan Mountain, near two seated Buddhas.

Gwi had spent five years cross-referencing the description with the topography of Namsan. He had identified seven possible locations, eliminated four through physical exploration, and narrowed his search to a grove near the summit where two weathered stone Buddhas flanked a natural cleft sealed with stones that were clearly not natural to the site.

They dug for three hours.

The stones came away reluctantly, as if the mountain itself were protesting. Behind them, wrapped in silk that crumbled to powder at the first breath of air, lay the blade. Dark iron. Bronze shimmer. Engravings that caught the moonlight and held it.

Gwi reached for it. The moment his fingers closed around the hilt, he gasped — not from a hallucination, not from a burn, but from a sudden, overwhelming wave of *desire.* He wanted things. He wanted gold and women and power and the specific, exquisite pleasure of watching other people realize they had less than he did. The desire was so intense it was physical, a heat in his chest that radiated outward until his skin flushed and his pupils dilated and his breathing came in short, shallow gasps.

The brothers stared at him.

"We sell it," Gwi said, and his voice was different — lower, smoother, the voice of a man who had decided that the world owed him something and was about to begin collecting.

They carried the blade down the mountain and sold it within a week, through a network of black-market antiquities dealers that connected the disintegrating Silla kingdom to the rising Goryeo dynasty's hungry new aristocracy. The blade passed through six hands in three months, each owner holding it just long enough to feel the pull before selling it — or being killed for it.

In the autumn of 1043, it arrived at the port of Byeokrando on the Yellow Sea coast, where a man with very specific tastes was waiting.

---

### *The East China Sea, 1043 CE*

Jang Bo-san did not call himself a pirate.

He called himself a maritime trade facilitator, which was technically accurate in the same way that calling a hurricane "vigorous weather" was technically accurate. He operated a fleet of twelve ships out of the islands south of the Korean peninsula, intercepting — *facilitating* — trade between Goryeo, Song China, and Japan. His men were loyal because he paid well and terrifying because he punished betrayal with a creativity that had become legendary along the eastern seaboard.

Bo-san was forty-one years old, barrel-chested, missing three teeth (two lost in combat, one in a wager he preferred not to discuss), and possessed of an ego so robust it had its own gravitational field. He claimed descent from the great Jang Bo-go, the ninth-century maritime king who had controlled the seas between Korea, China, and Japan — a claim that was almost certainly fabricated but that Bo-san repeated with such conviction that even skeptics found themselves half-believing it.

He acquired the sword from a terrified dealer in Byeokrando who practically threw it at him. The dealer — a man who had survived forty years in the black market by being shrewd, cautious, and utterly amoral — had owned the blade for six days and was desperate to be rid of it. He didn't explain why. He didn't need to. The black circles under his eyes and the tremor in his hands said everything.

Bo-san examined the blade by lamplight in his cabin aboard the *Haeryong* — the Sea Dragon — his flagship. The iron was crude, the edge dull, the whole thing encrusted with the grime of a thousand years in a mountain tomb. But the bronze core shimmered, and the engravings — Bo-san couldn't read any of them, but he recognized craftsmanship when he saw it — spoke of an age and a purpose that transcended the ordinary.

"How old?" he asked.

"Older than Silla," the dealer said. "Older than Goguryeo. Older than the Three Kingdoms. My buyer in Kaesong said the metallurgy is consistent with late Gojoseon."

Bo-san whistled softly. Gojoseon was ancient history — the semi-mythical kingdom from which all Korean civilization claimed descent. An artifact from that era, if authenticated, would be worth a fortune.

But Bo-san didn't sell it.

He kept it.

---

*The sea.*

*I have never tasted salt before. The man who holds me now smells of brine and ambition and the particular brand of confidence that belongs to people who have never been told no by anyone strong enough to make it stick.*

*He is greedy. This I can use.*

*Greedy men are easy. They hold on tight because they cannot imagine letting go. And the tighter they hold, the faster I fill them.*

---

The change was immediate.

Bo-san's first raid after acquiring the blade was against a Song Dynasty merchant convoy southeast of Jeju Island — three heavy junks carrying silk, porcelain, and silver. It should have been a difficult engagement. Song naval escorts were well-armed and disciplined, and three junks meant at least eighty fighting men.

Bo-san's fleet took them in twenty minutes.

He led the boarding action himself, the ancient blade in his right hand, and the blade *moved*. Not like a weapon — like an extension of his nervous system, like a limb he'd been born with but had only just discovered. It anticipated the enemy's strikes before they came. It found gaps in armor that shouldn't have existed. It cut through Song steel with the casual authority of a master calligrapher's brush moving through wet ink.

Bo-san was not a reflective man. He didn't wonder why a thousand-year-old blade fought better than anything forged in the best workshops of Goryeo. He didn't question why his swordsmanship had improved overnight from competent to supernatural. He simply absorbed the power the way a sponge absorbs water — automatically, gratefully, and without thought for what might happen when the saturation point was reached.

His men noticed.

"The captain's different," whispered Dok-su, the first mate, to the helmsman one night, when Bo-san was in his cabin talking to the sword. Yes — *talking* to it. Low, murmured conversations that the crew could hear through the thin wooden bulkheads but couldn't quite make out, as if Bo-san were negotiating terms with something that had its own agenda.

"Different how?"

"Better in a fight. Worse in everything else."

This was an astute observation. Bo-san's tactical brilliance had always been balanced by a rough but genuine humanity — he was a pirate, yes, but a pirate with a code. He didn't kill surrendering crews. He treated prisoners with a minimum of cruelty. He distributed plunder fairly. These were not moral principles so much as business practices — a reputation for restraint made future targets more likely to surrender without a fight, which saved time and men.

After the blade, the code eroded.

The first sign was the Song merchant captain. The man had surrendered promptly, kneeling on the deck of his captured junk with his hands raised and his crew behind him. Standard protocol was to take the cargo, set the prisoners adrift in a lifeboat with enough provisions to reach shore, and be on their way. Instead, Bo-san had stood over the kneeling captain with the blade resting on the man's shoulder, tilting the edge against his neck, watching a bead of blood form and run down the man's collar. He held the pose for a full minute — an eternity on a pitching deck — before Dok-su's quiet cough broke the spell.

"We should go, Captain. The tide."

Bo-san blinked. Stepped back. Sheathed the blade. The Song captain slumped forward, shaking.

The second sign was the crew.

### *Seoul, Present Day*

Jin-woo found the journal on a rainy Thursday.

It wasn't in Mr. Kang's abandoned shop — the shop was as empty as before, the padlock untouched, the dust settling into its permanent arrangements. It was in Mr. Kang's *basement*.

The basement entrance was around the back of the building, down a narrow flight of concrete stairs that descended to a steel door. The door was unlocked. Not just unlocked — *ajar*, propped open with a folded piece of newspaper, as if someone had left it that way deliberately. As if someone had wanted him to find it.

Jin-woo pushed the door open and stepped into a space that smelled of damp concrete and old leather.

The basement was small — perhaps four meters square — and nearly empty. A wooden shelf against one wall held a few dusty bottles of soju. A folding chair. A bare bulb hanging from the ceiling, which flickered to life when Jin-woo pulled the chain. And on the shelf, positioned with the careful precision of something placed rather than stored, a leather-bound journal.

It was old. The leather was cracked and darkened with age, the binding repaired multiple times with different materials — silk thread, cotton cord, what appeared to be catgut. The pages inside were a palimpsest of handwriting: different inks, different scripts, different centuries. Some entries were in classical Chinese. Others in Korean *hangul*. A few in scripts Jin-woo didn't recognize.

Each entry followed the same format: a name, a date (when available), and a brief description. The earliest entries were fragmentary — single characters, smudged and faded. But as the journal progressed through time, the entries grew longer and more detailed.

Jin-woo's hands were shaking as he turned the pages.

*Dal-sol. Bronze smith. Made the blade. Lost his love.*

*Yeonhwa. Tang soldier. Carried the blade across the desert. Lost herself.*

*Seol-rang. Hwarang. Buried the blade in Namsan. Lost his name.*

*Jang Bo-san. Sea king. Took the blade to war. Lost his fleet.*

Page after page. Name after name. Century after century. Each entry written by a different hand — not one chronicler but a chain of them, each keeper adding to the record before passing the responsibility to the next. A relay of witnesses spanning two thousand years.

The final entry was in modern Korean, written in ballpoint pen. The name had been erased — scratched out so thoroughly that the paper was torn. But beneath the scratched-out name, in a different ink, someone had added a single line:

*It is not finished.*

The handwriting was Mr. Kang's.

### *The East China Sea, 1043–1044 CE*

The crew began dying on the winter solstice.

Not in battle — Bo-san's fleet was undefeated now, a terror of the eastern seas, its reputation preceding it like a bow wave. Ships surrendered at the sight of the Sea Dragon's black pennant. No, the crew died in other ways. Quieter ways. Ways that defied rational explanation and that the surviving crew members discussed only in whispers, if at all.

The first was the lookout, a young man named Cheol. He was found one morning at the base of the mainmast, his neck broken, as if he had climbed to the crow's nest in the dark and simply stepped off. His eyes were open, and his expression — the crewmen who found him would never agree on this — was either horror or ecstasy.

The second was the cook, who walked into the sea during the middle watch with his pockets full of stones. Three men saw him go. None moved to stop him. They said later, unanimously and with the bewildered certainty of people who cannot explain their own inaction, that it had seemed *right*. That watching the cook walk into the black water between the swells had felt, in the moment, like the most natural thing in the world.

The third was the ship's carpenter, who was found in the hold with his own awl driven through his left eye. The official verdict was accident. No one believed it.

Bo-san noticed. He wasn't stupid — he was, in fact, brilliantly intelligent, which was part of the problem. Brilliant people are the best at constructing explanations for things that should not be explained. The lookout was careless. The cook was despondent. The carpenter was clumsy. Three deaths in three months was unusual but not unprecedented on a vessel that spent its life at sea. These things happened.

What Bo-san did not allow himself to notice was the pattern. Each death had occurred within a day of the blade being used in combat. And each dead man had, at some point in the preceding week, handled the sword — cleaning it, sharpening it, carrying it to Bo-san's cabin. Brief contacts. Seconds at most. Enough.

---

*Three. The lookout, the cook, the carpenter.*

*They were small. Their grip was brief and their spirits thin. I took what I needed — the lookout's fear of heights, the cook's loneliness, the carpenter's guilt over a daughter he'd abandoned — and I *amplified* it until the volume exceeded what their minds could contain.*

*The captain is different. The captain is loud already. Ambition, pride, hunger — he was full of these before I arrived. I merely turned up the heat, and his own nature did the rest.*

*The woman, though. The woman on the captured ship. She hears me.*

*That is... inconvenient.*

---

So-so arrived on the *Sea Dragon* in the spring of 1044, as cargo.

She was a Song Dynasty noblewoman — the daughter of a minor court official in Hangzhou, traveling to the port of Mingzhou to join her husband. Her ship had been intercepted by Bo-san's fleet, and she had been taken aboard as a hostage, her ransom value calculated by the first mate with the detached professionalism of an accountant.

She was thirty years old, plainly dressed, and possessed of the kind of beauty that took several minutes to notice and several hours to stop noticing — not the beauty of symmetry or youth, but the beauty of intelligence made visible. Her eyes missed nothing. Her mind processed everything. And within her first hour on the *Sea Dragon*, she had assessed Bo-san with the accuracy of a woman who had been reading powerful men since childhood and found most of them wanting.

"Your crew is terrified of you," she said, when Bo-san came to inspect his new hostage.

"Good."

"Not of *you*. Of whatever you carry." Her Goryeo was accented but fluent — Song noblewomen of a certain class were expected to speak multiple languages. "There's a sound in this ship. A vibration. Below the threshold of hearing but above the threshold of — " She paused, searching for the word. "Above the threshold of dreaming."

Bo-san stared at her.

"I'm a merchant's daughter," she said. "We're taught to listen for things other people can't hear. Lies. Hesitations. The sound a scale makes when the weights are wrong." She looked toward the wall of Bo-san's cabin, beyond which the blade hung on its rack. "That sound is wrong."

"You're imagining things."

"Your cook walked into the sea last month. Your lookout fell from the mast. Your carpenter drove a tool through his own skull. And you — " She paused. "How long has it been since you've slept through the night?"

Bo-san's hand moved involuntarily toward the hilt of the blade at his hip. So-so tracked the movement with her eyes, and what she saw there — the compulsive reaching, the unconscious caress — confirmed everything.

"That sword," she said quietly. "How long have you had it?"

"Long enough."

"And before you — who had it?"

"I don't know. I don't care."

"You should." So-so leaned forward. "Because whatever that sword is, it isn't a weapon. It's a mouth. And it's eating your crew from the inside out, and when it's finished with them, it will eat you."

Bo-san laughed. It was the laugh of a man who was used to being the most dangerous thing in any room and found the idea of being threatened by a piece of metal genuinely amusing.

So-so did not laugh.

### *Seoul, Present Day*

Jin-woo spent the night reading the journal.

The entries were addictive — each one a keyhole view into a life consumed by the blade. Some entries were detailed: multi-page accounts of acquisition, use, and loss, written with the careful hand of someone who understood the importance of the record they were keeping. Others were barely legible: a name, a date, a single panicked sentence. *Don't touch it. Don't touch it. Don't touch it.*

By dawn, Jin-woo had created a timeline on his apartment wall. Index cards. Red string. The full serial-killer-investigation aesthetic. Each card bore a name, a date, and a single-line summary:

*Dal-sol, c. 300 BCE — Creator. Lost his love.*
*Yeonhwa, c. 650 CE — Warrior. Lost herself.*
*Seol-rang, c. 750 CE — Hwarang. Buried the blade.*
*Jang Bo-san, c. 1044 CE — Pirate. Lost his fleet.*

And the pattern — the pattern Su-hyun would later confirm with her own research — was already visible. Every owner met a tragic end. Every owner was consumed by the blade's amplification of their own nature. Every owner, without exception, lost the thing they loved most.

Jin-woo looked at the blade on his kitchen table. His left hand throbbed.

*What do I love most?* he thought.

His phone buzzed. A text from Su-hyun: *Are you okay? You missed our meeting.*

He stared at the message for a long time. Then he set the phone down and went back to the journal.

---

### *The East China Sea, 1044 CE*

The typhoon came from the south.

Bo-san had sailed through storms before — it was an occupational hazard of piracy in the East China Sea — but this one was different. The barometric drop was precipitous, the wind backing from southeast to northeast in a pattern that the helmsman, a man with forty years of ocean experience, called "the devil's pinwheel." The sky turned the color of a bruise. The sea rose.

Bo-san ordered the fleet to make for shelter, but the storm moved faster than any ship. Within an hour, the waves were running at fifteen meters — black walls of water that crashed over the *Sea Dragon*'s deck with enough force to shatter timbers. Two of the smaller ships capsized within the first hour. A third lost its mast and drifted into the dark, its crew's screams audible for precisely three seconds before the wind swallowed them.

In the chaos, Bo-san stood on the foredeck of the *Sea Dragon* with the blade in his hand.

"I am Jang Bo-san!" he screamed into the storm. "Descendant of Jang Bo-go! King of the eastern seas! I do not bend to wind and water!"

The blade caught a flash of lightning and held it — held the light for a fraction of a second longer than physics allowed, glowing blue-white in the darkness, and for that fraction of a second, Bo-san felt the blade's full power pour into him: the strength of every warrior who had held it, the accumulated combat skill of two thousand years, the absolute certainty that he was invincible, immortal, a god in human skin.

The next wave hit the *Sea Dragon* broadside.

The ship broke in half. The sound was enormous — the tearing of oak and pine and teak, the shriek of iron nails wrenched from their seats, the explosive decompression of air pockets in the hold. Bo-san was thrown from the foredeck into the black water, and the blade was torn from his grip, and the cold hit him like a wall of stone.

He sank.

The last thing he saw, as the darkness of the sea closed over him, was the blade.

It was floating.

Not drifting — *floating*. Riding the surface of the churning water with a deliberate buoyancy that no piece of metal should possess. Ships sank around it. Men drowned beneath it. The storm raged above it. And the blade floated, serene and patient, waiting for the current to carry it to the next shore and the next hand and the next hunger.

---

So-so survived.

She was pulled from the water by a fishing vessel out of Jeju that had spotted the wreckage at dawn. She was the only survivor from the *Sea Dragon*. The fishermen wrapped her in blankets and gave her rice porridge and asked her, with the gentle curiosity of people who understand that the sea takes what it wants, what had happened.

"A storm," she said.

She didn't mention the blade. She didn't mention the floating. She didn't mention the sound she had heard, in the moment the ship broke apart — a sound that was not the screaming of men or the roar of the wind but something else, something *underneath*, like a sigh of satisfaction from a thing that had just been fed.

She returned to Hangzhou and married her husband and bore three children and died at seventy-two, and she never spoke of the blade again.

But once, when she was very old, her youngest grandchild found her weeping over a calligraphy she had written: a single character, repeated again and again across the page, in a script the child didn't recognize.

The character, in ancient Sogdian, meant *warning*.

---

*The ocean.*

*Salt — I've never tasted it before. The pirate king sinks, his lungs filling with the cold, dark water, his ego finally encountering something larger than itself.*

*I float. I have always floated. Bronze does not float, and iron does not float, but I am neither bronze nor iron — or rather, I am bronze and iron and blood and desire and twenty-three centuries of accumulated human darkness, and the combined weight of all these things is, paradoxically, lighter than water.*

*The current takes me south. Toward another shore. Another hand.*

*I can wait.*

*I have always been good at waiting.*

*End of Episode 5*
