Episode 2: Ghost Data
"Five minutes?" Chloe’s voice cracked, her tablet slipping from her hands. "Dr. Finch, what is he talking about? Who did he see in the lobby?"

"Us, Chloe. A localized temporal echo." I didn't stop moving. I ripped open the bottom drawer of my desk, bypassing the encrypted hard drives, and grabbed the heavy, leather-bound notebooks—my grandfather’s original handwritten logs. Physical ink on dead wood. Safe from the digital meat grinder.

"Julian, the Bronco," I snapped, stuffing the notebooks into a canvas messenger bag. "Tell me it’s the old one. The '74."

"Four-barrel carburetor, points ignition, no computer, no GPS," Julian grinned, though sweat was pouring down his face. "It runs on gasoline and explosions. It’s dumb as a rock, Arthur. Right now, dumb is our best survival strategy."

"Good." I grabbed Chloe by the arm. She flinched, her eyes wide with panic. "You're coming with us. You've seen the raw data. You’re our control group."

"To where? Nevada?! I have a midterm on Tuesday!"

"By Tuesday, the concept of a midterm will be a quantum impossibility," Julian yelled, already halfway out the heavy soundproof door. "Move!"

We plunged into the corridor. The emergency lights were flickering in a chaotic, arrhythmic strobe. The building, usually alive with the quiet drone of servers and HVAC systems, groaned like a sinking submarine.

As we hit the stairwell, the reality of the 'copy error' manifested.

A grad student was running up the stairs toward us. He looked terrified, clutching a laptop to his chest. But as he brushed past, he didn't make a sound. No footsteps. No breathing. And he was slightly translucent, like a poorly rendered hologram. A second later, the real student came sprinting up the stairs, screaming in absolute terror, perfectly overlapping the ghost trajectory of his own immediate future.

"Don't touch them!" I barked at Chloe, pulling her against the cold concrete wall. "Pauli exclusion principle! Two identical fermions cannot occupy the same quantum state. If the universe hasn't finished separating the timelines and you touch your own duplicated mass..."

"You annihilate," Julian finished, taking the stairs three at a time. "Total quantum collapse. Just keep your head down and don't touch anything that looks fuzzy!"

We burst into the ground-floor lobby. Through the glass doors, the Boston sky was a sickening bruised purple. And there, just pushing through the revolving doors, were three figures. A man in a leather jacket, a man in a trench coat, and a young woman.

Us.

They were flickering, their edges jagged with static. The universe was buffering, trying to render two sets of data in the same spatial coordinates.

"Brace!" Julian roared, tackling me and Chloe behind the heavy marble security desk.

The air pressure in the lobby suddenly spiked, popping my ears. A wave of raw ozone washed over us, accompanied by a sound like tearing canvas. The air shimmered, the temperature dropped twenty degrees, and then... the ghost figures vanished. The lobby was empty. We had just missed colliding with our own duplicated data by seconds.

"Out! Now!"

We sprinted through the shattered glass doors and into the alley. Julian’s vintage Ford Bronco sat idling, a beautiful, rusted hunk of analog machinery vibrating with mechanical certainty. We threw ourselves inside. Julian slammed the gearshift, and the heavy V8 engine roared, peeling out onto Massachusetts Avenue.

The city was descending into absolute madness.

It wasn't a riot; it was an ontological breakdown. Traffic lights were showing red and green simultaneously. Pedestrians were collapsing on the sidewalks, clutching their heads as their brains tried to process memories from a timeline that hadn't happened yet.

Chloe was in the backseat, staring at her smartphone. "Dr. Finch... my phone. Look at the date."

I glanced back. The digital display on her iPhone was scrolling through years like a slot machine. 2026. 2011. 2035. 1999. Then the screen filled with a chaotic mess of alphanumeric garbage before the battery physically swelled, cracking the glass. I snatched it from her hand and threw it out the window.

"Digital devices are high-density information hubs," I explained, gripping the dashboard as Julian swerved to avoid a flickering city bus. "If the universe is running out of data capacity and trying to back itself up, the first things to corrupt will be the places where information is most concentrated. Servers, quantum networks, microchips. They are the hotspots for the cosmic copy errors. We call it Ghost Data."

"So, what?" Chloe yelled over the roar of the engine. "We just go off the grid? Forever?"

"Until the universe finishes its cellular division," Julian shouted from the driver's seat. "Arthur’s right. The universe is in Interphase. It’s copying its DNA. Right now, reality is a double helix that hasn't unzipped yet. We just have to survive the unzipping."

Julian punched the analog radio on the dashboard. Static hissed through the speakers, followed by an automated emergency broadcast system. But the voice was wrong. It was a terrifying mashup of audio files.

"...severe weather warning for Suffolk County... this is John F. Kennedy... Russian missiles in Cuba... turn left at the next intersection... God have mercy on us all..."

Julian snapped the radio off. The silence inside the cabin was heavy, contrasted by the apocalypse unfolding outside the windows.

"My grandfather’s bunker in Nevada," Julian said, his knuckles white on the steering wheel as we hit the on-ramp for I-90 West. "Six hundred feet underground. Lead-lined. Built to survive a nuclear electromagnetic pulse. It’s a total Faraday cage. The ultimate analog safe room."

I looked out the window. Above the skyline, the clouds were beginning to stretch horizontally, pulled taut by an invisible, massive tension. The spindle fibers were forming.

"Step on it, Julian," I muttered, my hand resting on my grandfather’s notebooks. "The membrane is pulling tight."
