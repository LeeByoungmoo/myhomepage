Episode 15: How to Walk in a Blind Spot
The Stryker was bleeding out. Not oil or diesel, but mass.

We were tearing through the chimerical landscape at forty miles per hour, alternating violently between the humid redwood forest of the parallel Earth and the freezing, cracked hardpan of the Nevada desert. The dimensional overlap was flickering so fast it was creating a strobe effect, turning the world outside the viewing slit into a nauseating blur of green and grey.

"The corridor is shrinking!" Julian yelled, gripping the edge of my drafting board. The needle on his analog magnetometer was violently oscillating. "The node is less than twenty feet wide, Arthur! We are driving down a hallway!"

Before I could answer, a horrific, high-pitched screech tore through the cabin. It wasn't the engine. It was the sound of heavy composite armor being erased from existence.

"Left side! Left side!" Captain Miller roared, jerking the heavy steering wheel to the right.

I looked through the left viewing slit. The exterior armor of the Stryker was brushing against the invisible wall of the spatial tear. The metal wasn't bending or sparking. It was simply dissolving into blinding white static. The vehicle was too wide for the safe zone.

"Correct your heading!" Thorne barked, bracing herself against the roof.

"I can't!" Miller screamed, fighting the wheel. "The right side is clipping too!"

We were a cork being forced through the neck of a shrinking bottle.

With a deafening crunch, the rear axle caught the edge of the dimensional fault line. The spatial physics sheared the heavy military tires perfectly in half. The Stryker violently spun out of control, rolling twice before slamming on its side into the muddy roots of a massive redwood tree.

The impact knocked the breath out of my lungs. For a moment, the world was nothing but the taste of blood, ringing ears, and the suffocating smell of ruptured battery acid.

"Out!" Thorne’s voice cut through the chaos. She was already kicking the jammed rear hatch open. "The vehicle is bridging the tear! It's going to be deleted!"

I unclipped my harness and fell into the mud coating the side wall. I grabbed Julian, hauling him up, and we spilled out of the back hatch into the freezing night air.

Fifty yards away, Silas’s Stryker had suffered the same fate. Its front end was lodged into a shimmering wall of static, the engine block completely severed from reality. Silas was dragging himself out of the wreckage alone. The two soldiers who had ridden with him didn't come out at all.

We scrambled away from our downed vehicle just as the spatial furrow tightened.

It was utterly silent. The thirty-ton armored Stryker didn't explode. The space it occupied simply folded inward, overlapping itself. The heavy steel chassis crumpled into a dense, impossible geometric knot before vanishing entirely in a flash of white noise, leaving a perfectly smooth, circular crater in the dirt.

"Well," Silas grunted, limping over to us with his shotgun slung over his shoulder. "So much for the armor."

We were standing in the middle of a clearing, surrounded by a swirling vortex of ghost data. The safe zone—the zero-tension node we were using to survive—was no longer a straight line. It was a chaotic, shifting labyrinth of stable space, meandering through a forest of absolute destruction.

"We can't drive," Thorne said, wiping blood from a gash on her forehead. She looked at me, her predator eyes demanding the next step. "How do we walk it, Finch?"

I pulled my grandfather’s notebook and a mechanical stopwatch from my canvas bag. The glass on the stopwatch was cracked, but it was still ticking.

"The contractile ring in a dividing cell isn't a solid blade," I said, my voice trembling as I looked at the terrifying walls of static closing in around us. "It's made of millions of actin and myosin filaments ratcheting past each other. It’s a net. The tension nodes aren't a highway; they are a spiderweb. They open and close based on a specific rhythmic frequency."

"You want us to hopscotch through a dimensional meat grinder?" Miller asked, his face pale.

"I want us to step exactly where the tension cancels out," I said, holding up the ticking stopwatch. "I know the frequency of the biological contraction. I can time the shifts in the spatial geometry. But we have to move in single file. And you have to step exactly in my footprints. If you deviate by a single inch, the tear will take you."

Thorne didn't hesitate. "Lead the way, Doctor."

I stepped to the front of the group. The air in front of me was shimmering, thick with the pressure of two universes trying to occupy the same space. I clicked the stopwatch.

Tick. Tick. Tick.

"Three seconds," I muttered, watching the static wall ahead of me ripple. "Two. One. Now."

I stepped forward into the haze.

The physical sensation was horrifying. It felt like walking through chest-deep, freezing water. The air pressure was so immense my ears popped with every step. To my left, a redwood tree was flickering between existence and non-existence. To my right, the cracked desert floor was raining upwards into the sky.

"Step on the downbeat!" I yelled back to the group. "The nodes only stabilize for two seconds before the filaments shift! Move!"

Julian was right behind me, his eyes locked on my boots. Chloe followed him, then Silas, Miller, and Thorne bringing up the rear. We formed a desperate, crawling human chain, weaving through a minefield of broken physics.

We walked for what felt like hours, though my stopwatch said it was only twenty minutes. The biological timing required absolute focus. Step. Pause. Shift right. Step. Pause. Shift left.

We were walking the ultimate tightrope. Below us was solid ground, but on either side was a drop into infinite, deleting oblivion.

"Arthur, the frequency is speeding up," Julian panted, staying perfectly in my tracks.

"I know," I ground out, my eyes burning with sweat. "The furrow is reaching its maximum constriction. The final cut is imminent."

Suddenly, the static wall to our right violently lurched inward.

The rhythm had stuttered. The biological machinery of the universe had glitched.

Chloe, who was mid-step, lost her balance. She stumbled, her right boot slipping exactly two inches outside of my designated footprint.

The spatial tear grabbed her leg.

It wasn't a physical snare. It was a gravitational absolute. Her foot crossed the event horizon of the cleavage furrow, and the universe instantly assigned her mass to the separating timeline.

Chloe screamed, a sound of pure, unadulterated terror, as she was pulled violently to the right, toward the blinding wall of white noise.

"Chloe!" Julian roared.

He didn't think. He abandoned the safety of my footprint and lunged, grabbing her outstretched hand just as her shoulder hit the static wall.

"Julian, no!" I screamed, reaching back. "The overlap!"

Julian dug his boots into the mud, his muscles straining as he played a game of tug-of-war with a dimensional rift. The friction of the overlapping realities was catastrophic. Where Julian’s hands gripped Chloe's, the air sparked with raw plasma.

"Pull!" Silas bellowed, dropping his shotgun and wrapping his massive arms around Julian’s waist.

With a sickening, tearing sound, Julian and Silas yanked Chloe backward, ripping her out of the dimensional fault line. The three of them collapsed into the mud inside the safe zone, panting and shaking.

Chloe was sobbing hysterically, clutching her leg. The rubber sole of her right boot was gone, sheared off with surgical precision.

But Julian wasn't making a sound.

He was staring at his right hand.

I fell to my knees beside him, my stomach turning to ice.

Julian’s hand was... wrong. When he reached into the tear to pull Chloe back, his hand had occupied two universes simultaneously. The spatial overlap had scrambled his atomic structure. His fingers were partially fused together, the flesh shimmering and flickering like a bad television signal. It looked like the deformed, chimerical stag we had seen earlier.

He had localized Ghost Data grafted onto his own body.

"Arthur," Julian whispered, his voice trembling as he stared at his glitching, translucent fingers. "I... I can't feel it."

Thorne walked up, her face a mask of cold calculation. She looked at Julian’s hand, then at the narrowing corridor of stable space ahead of us.

"Wrap it," Thorne ordered, pulling a roll of gauze from her vest and tossing it to me. "We don't have time to bleed. The node is collapsing."

I quickly bandaged Julian’s flickering hand, binding the terrifying visual glitch beneath layers of white cotton. He gritted his teeth, his eyes meeting mine. The fearless astrophysicist was gone; in his place was a man who had just touched the edge of the abyss.

"We have to keep moving," I said, pulling him to his feet. I looked at the stopwatch. The ticks were accelerating. "The final stage is starting. The membrane is closing."
