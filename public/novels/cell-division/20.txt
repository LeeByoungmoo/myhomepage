Episode 20: Witness to Evolution (Finale)
The second morning in the new universe began with the sound of metal on metal.

It was Dr. Evelyn Thorne, leaning against a rusted steel doorframe, cleaning her revolver. The morning after the apocalypse, the first thing she did was count her remaining ammunition and wipe the dust from the barrel. Thorough, ruthless pragmatism. It was how she had survived on the razor's edge of evolution.

"Four nine-millimeter magazines. One hundred and twenty rounds of 5.56. Fourteen rounds for my magnum," Thorne reported to no one in particular, snapping the cylinder shut. "No food. Half a liter of water left in Miller's canteen. We move inland at exactly 1000 hours to find fresh water and something to eat. Any objections?"

No one answered.

Captain Miller sat in the shade of the observatory's shattered dome, numbly re-knotting the bootlace that had snapped when he'd faced his doppelgänger. Chloe was curled up in a corner, dead asleep. Tear streaks had dried on her face; only the slow rise and fall of her chest said she was still with us.

And Silas Mercer.

The old prepper hadn't moved from the spot where he'd collapsed the night before. The battered, hand-cranked military radio sat in his lap. The spring had run down hours ago and the radio had gone silent, but Silas kept staring into nothing, turning the antenna back and forth. His grief was too big for words. None of us dared go near it.

I opened my grandfather's leather-bound notebook—the Darwin family heirloom—and set it on my knees.

The hand holding the pencil was trembling. I turned to the last blank page. I had to record it: the cosmic algorithm of survival that had been running for 13.8 billion years. Interphase. Prophase. Metaphase. Anaphase. Telophase. And finally, abscission. The mechanism was complete.

I wet the tip of the pencil and began scratching words across the rough paper.

*Observation Log. 14 hours post-division.*
*The cell has split in two. We are now inside the new daughter cell (B-side). The local inertial frame collapsed when half the mass disappeared, but the human brain is adapting to the new physics through neuroplasticity. The phantom limb is a physical phenomenon: our bodies keep reaching for the resistance of a missing half of the universe. This wound will not heal.*

I stopped writing and looked up. The massive brass telescope at the center of the observatory gleamed coldly in the morning light.

*Time is a luxury for those who don't have our blood, Arthur.*

My father's voice, arrogant and cold, echoed in my head. The man who had locked me inside the micro-world simply because I was a descendant of Darwin. He had trusted the mechanism of life blindly and mocked the sky as a dumping ground for dreamers.

But my father was wrong.

The perfect biological mechanism of cell division was not salvation. It was violence. A ruthless survival strategy that could only finish its work by tearing flesh, crushing bone, and grinding my oldest friend into a dimensional rift.

I closed the notebook. And I let that heavy leather heirloom—my father's entire creed—drop onto the moss-covered concrete floor.

Something heavy snapped inside my chest. The invisible thread that had been choking me my whole life finally broke. I was free of my father's microscope.

I stood up and walked toward the telescope.

"Dr. Finch," Thorne called after me. "I don't think we have time for stargazing."

"Some things you can see even in daylight, Dr. Thorne."

I turned the heavy gears. With a screech of metal, the great optical tube tilted toward the blue morning sky. I put my eye to the eyepiece and found the coordinates of the massive, geometric Cut—the erased half of the universe—that I had seen the night before.

The sunlight washed out most of the detail, but the air was so pristine that the black voids of deep space still showed their faint outlines.

It should have been empty. An absolute vacuum. No light. No mass. Nothing.

But there, in the dead center of that void, I caught a tiny distortion.

I held my breath and fine-tuned the brass dials, pushing the magnification as far as it would go.

It wasn't light. It was gravitational lensing. In a region of absolute nothing, space itself was bending ever so slightly, warping the light of the stars behind it. That cannot happen where there is no mass.

"Silas!" My voice boomed through the empty observatory. "The radio! Turn it on! Now!"

Silas looked up with hollow eyes. "Spring's run down. And there's nothing out there but dead air anyway."

"Wind it! Just once, please!" I shouted, keeping my eye locked to the telescope.

Startled, Captain Miller scrambled over and started cranking the radio's handle himself. The spring ratcheted tight, and the radio crackled to life.

The same soft white noise from the night before poured out. The cosmic microwave background. Ancient. Empty.

"There's nothing there, Doctor," Miller panted.

"Take it down to the lowest band it has," I said, double-checking the coordinates. "Whatever is bending that light is enormous. If it's sending anything, it'll be at the long end of the spectrum. Slow waves."

As if finally waking up, Silas took the tuning dial in his rough hand and began to turn it, slowly, very slowly. The pitch of the static sank lower and lower.

And then.

*Tick.* ... *Tick.* ... *Tick.*

Through the white noise came a faint, rhythmic pulse.

Everyone in the observatory froze. Even Chloe's eyes snapped open, and she sat bolt upright.

It wasn't a pulsar. It wasn't storm static. It was a mathematically perfect signal, repeating at exactly one-and-a-half-second intervals.

"What is that?" Thorne dropped her cleaning rag and shot to her feet. "Are you telling me there are other survivors on this planet?"

"No." I slowly pulled my eye away from the eyepiece. The hair on my arms was standing on end, and my heart was hammering. "It isn't coming from Earth. It's coming from deep space."

"From the void?" Silas said, clutching the radio. "From the hole where half the stars got cut away?"

"When a cell finishes dividing, the two daughter cells don't become strangers," I said quietly, staring into the empty air.

The last piece of the cosmic puzzle was clicking into place: the lensing in the eyepiece, the pulse on the radio. One phenomenon.

"They talk to each other. They send chemical signals. They sense each other's presence, regulate their density, sometimes even trade information to survive. It's called cell signaling."

I turned to face the survivors. What I felt wasn't terror. It was awe—pure and total, unlike anything I had felt in my life.

"That void isn't empty. It's the other side of the wall. That signal is an echo from the universe we came from. The A-side."

*Tick.* ... *Tick.* ... *Tick.*

It sounded like someone knocking on a wall from the far side of the void. *We made it. We're still here.* Two universes that had survived being torn apart, confirming each other's existence in the most primal language there is: gravity itself.

Tears ran down Silas's weathered face. Not tears of loss. Tears of relief. Here was physical proof that the original universe had survived—that his grandson, Julian Mercer, who had held the door open until the very end, had not died for nothing.

"Major Tom..." Silas choked out, clutching the radio to his chest like a wounded animal. "Major Tom wrote back."

I laid my hand on the cold metal barrel of the telescope.

The phantom limb will torment us forever. Every time we look up at the erased half of the universe, we will feel that bone-deep loss. No one knows whether we will make it out of this new West Coast wilderness and find some trace of civilization, or whether we are the last humans on this planet, destined to quietly go extinct.

But I was no longer afraid.

We were the DNA that had survived the purge—the data the system tried to format and couldn't. The mechanism was violent. But at the end of all that violence, we had finally looked the vast, empty universe in the eye.

Leaving my family's legacy lying on the floor, I looked up at the telescope's lens one last time. The universe Julian had so desperately wanted to show me was right there.

"Let's move out, Dr. Thorne."

I stepped out beyond the shattered dome, into the brilliant, blinding morning sun.

"Let's go see what evolution does next."

(The End)
