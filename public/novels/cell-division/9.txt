Episode 9: Dr. Thorne’s Hunt
The sky over the Nevada desert didn't just break; it unzipped.

Dr. Evelyn Thorne stood up through the open top hatch of the lead Stryker armored vehicle, gripping the heavy .50 caliber machine gun mount to steady herself. The lateral gravity that had practically thrown her against the steel hull a few minutes ago had vanished as abruptly as it began. But what replaced it was infinitely worse.

She pulled her tactical goggles down over her eyes, shielding them from the harsh, biting wind and the impossible geometry unfolding above.

The bruised magenta sky was now bisected by a jagged, blindingly white fault line that stretched from the northern horizon to the southern edge of the Earth. It didn't look like a cloud formation or an atmospheric anomaly. It looked like a crack in a pane of thick glass, behind which lay a terrifyingly empty, absolute void.

Anaphase, she thought, remembering the frantic, half-mad debriefing from the biologists at DARPA before the digital networks completely vaporized. The universe is tearing its chromosomes apart.

"Doctor Thorne! Get back inside!"

Captain Miller, the commander of the small, heavily modified convoy, grabbed her tactical vest and yanked her down into the troop compartment. He slammed the heavy steel hatch shut just as the air outside popped with a deafening, concussive boom.

Thorne stumbled into the narrow aisle of the Stryker. The interior was lit by the dim red glow of analog emergency lights. All the digital screens—the GPS, the thermal imaging, the digitized comms—were dead, their LCD panels shattered by expanding batteries or fried by logic paradoxes. The driver was navigating through the desert using a mounted brass compass and a periscope.

"We just lost Stryker Three," Miller yelled over the roar of the diesel engine, his face pale beneath his helmet. "It drove straight through a visual distortion. One second it was there, the next... it was just gone. Half a bumper was left cleanly sheared off, like it was cut by a laser."

"It wasn't cut," Thorne said, her voice icy, devoid of the panic that was consuming the soldiers around her. "It was sorted. The spatial fabric is splitting into two distinct multiverses. Whatever crosses that fault line gets assigned to the new reality. We have to stay parallel to the tear."

Evelyn Thorne was a pragmatist to her core. Before the universe decided to undergo cellular division, she was the Director of Advanced Threat Logistics at DARPA. Her job was to figure out how to keep the United States government functioning through nuclear wars, biological plagues, and asteroid strikes.

But no contingency plan had accounted for the universe running out of hard drive space.

Yesterday, Washington D.C. had simply ceased to exist, swallowed by a massive, overlapping cluster of ghost data. An hour ago, Cheyenne Mountain—the impenetrable, nuclear-proof heart of the U.S. military—was cleanly bisected by a dimensional tear because it was built on an active cosmic fault line. The most secure bunker on Earth was currently existing in two different dimensions simultaneously, crushing everyone inside.

Steel and concrete couldn't stop a dimensional tear. The only thing that could was geometry.

Thorne reached into her tactical rig and pulled out a manila folder. Inside were two analog photographs. One was of Julian Mercer, a brilliant but reckless astrophysicist from the VLA. The other was of Dr. Arthur Finch, an MIT quantum biologist.

When the digital apocalypse began, DARPA’s last functioning analog surveillance hub had intercepted a bizarre terrestrial radio transmission. Julian Mercer had downloaded the raw, uncorrupted telemetry of the 'Dark Flow' onto physical plotter paper and fled. And he had taken a biologist with him.

Thorne had put the pieces together. If the universe was undergoing a biological division, then the spatial tears wouldn't be random. They would follow a fractal, structural pattern. There would be 'safe zones'—microscopic nodes where the opposing tension perfectly canceled out.

Arthur Finch knew where those nodes were. And Julian Mercer had the map to find them.

"How far to the coordinates?" Thorne asked, leaning over the driver’s shoulder to look at the physical paper map taped to the dashboard.

"Two miles," the driver grunted, wrestling with the heavy, unassisted steering wheel. "But Doctor, there's nothing out there. It's just a dead-end mesa in the Black Rock Desert."

"It’s an old NORAD signal relay site," Thorne corrected. "Decommissioned in the nineties, bought by an eccentric prepper named Silas Mercer. It's a six-hundred-foot-deep Faraday cage built on top of a zero-tension node. It's the only safe place left on this continent."

The Stryker lurched violently as it hit a dry riverbed, the heavy suspension groaning.

"Doctor!" the gunner shouted from the periscope. "I see a structure. Base of the mesa. Looks like a collapsed mine entrance, but there are fresh tire tracks leading right up to it."

Thorne felt a cold, predatory satisfaction settle in her chest. They had survived the tear.

"Surround the entrance," Thorne ordered Captain Miller. "Mount the analog breaching charges on that door. But do not blow it unless I give the word. I need the people inside alive. Their brains are the only things keeping humanity from being deleted."

Six hundred feet below the surface, the silence of the bunker was shattered by a sound that didn't belong to the cosmos.

A harsh, mechanical buzzer cut through the silence. Silas Mercer, who was still organizing the spilled cans of soup, froze. Julian and I stopped breathing.

Silas slowly walked over to a heavy, red alarm bell mounted on the wall. A small analog counter next to it was clicking furiously.

"Perimeter tripwires," Silas whispered, his blue eyes narrowing into dangerous slits. "The physical ones. Not motion sensors. Someone just drove heavy armor over the pressure plates at the mesa base."

"Could it be scavengers?" Chloe asked, backing away from the heavy steel elevator doors as if they might burst open at any second.

"Scavengers don't drive thirty-ton vehicles in a coordinated tactical formation," Silas grunted. He reached under the workbench and pulled out a heavy, military-grade detonator wired to a thick bundle of red cables. "I wired the entrance tunnel with C4 ten years ago. One twist of this handle, and the entire shaft collapses. Nobody gets in, nobody gets out."

"Wait!" I yelled, grabbing his arm. "If you collapse the shaft, the concussion wave in this enclosed space could rupture our eardrums, or worse, compromise the Faraday shielding!"

Before Silas could argue, a loud, distorted screech echoed through the bunker. It was coming from a dusty, wall-mounted intercom box near the elevator—a direct, hardwired analog line to the surface door.

Crack... hiss... "Dr. Finch. Dr. Mercer. This is Dr. Evelyn Thorne with the Department of Defense."

The voice was female, crisp, and entirely devoid of fear. It cut through the underground silence like a scalpel.

Julian’s face went white. "Thorne. She’s the head of DARPA’s black-sky contingency division. She's the one who wanted to weaponize the Dark Flow data before the network collapsed."

Hiss... "I know you can hear me. We have a visual on your tire tracks. We also have enough thermite to melt that steel blast door into slag. But I'd rather we handle this like scientists."

I stepped toward the intercom, pressing the heavy metal button. "Dr. Thorne. The DOD doesn't exist anymore. The government doesn't exist. There is no authority left to give orders."

Crack... "Authority is a construct, Dr. Finch. Survival is physics. You have the overlapping map. You know where the nodes are. The cleavage furrow just bisected Cheyenne Mountain. We are losing the structural integrity of the planet, and you are sitting on the only topological safe zone in Nevada."

"Go to hell!" Julian shouted into the speaker. "If we open that door, we compromise the Faraday cage! The Ghost Data will flood in!"

Hiss... "The data overwrite is the least of our problems right now, Julian. Look at your seismographs. Look at the tectonic tension. The tearing has started. The universe is unzipping. I have a convoy of analog-modified armor and fifty men. If you give me the map of the nodes, we can evacuate what's left of our command structure to the safe zones."

She paused, and the intercom hissed with dead air before her cold voice returned.

"If you refuse, I will blow this door, compromise your cage, and let the dimensional tear sort you out. You have sixty seconds to manually open the shaft."

Silas didn't say a word. He just calmly began to untwist the safety wire on the C4 detonator.

We were trapped. The universe was tearing itself apart above us, and the wolves were at the door.
