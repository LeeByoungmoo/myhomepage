Episode 16: Crossed Orbits
The air was no longer a gas. It had the viscous, suffocating drag of deep water.

Every step required a monumental exertion of physical strength. The safe zone—the final biological node—was shrinking down to a microscopic thread. In cellular division, this final, fragile bridge connecting the two dividing halves is called the midbody. We were walking across the cosmic midbody, and it was violently vibrating, preparing for abscission. The final cut.

Behind us, the chimerical forest and the cracked desert were gone, swallowed by a blinding, towering wall of white noise that trailed our footsteps by mere inches.

"Keep the pace!" Thorne yelled, though her voice sounded warped, as if she were speaking through a thick pane of glass. "The corridor is collapsing!"

I was at the front, my eyes locked on the cracked face of the stopwatch. Tick. Tick. But my focus kept slipping. I couldn't stop looking back at Julian.

He was stumbling. The brilliant astrophysicist, the man who had effortlessly navigated the cosmos through his radio telescopes, was losing his balance on solid ground. His right arm was tucked tightly against his chest. The white gauze Thorne had used to wrap his glitched hand was no longer white. It was glowing from the inside with a sickly, pixelated light, humming with the static of two overlapping realities fighting for dominance within his cellular structure.

He was a walking quantum paradox. And the universe hates a paradox.

Cambridge. 2014.

The trunk of the rusted '74 Ford Bronco slammed shut with a metallic clang that echoed through the empty MIT parking lot. It was raining—a cold, miserable New England drizzle.

Julian stood in the rain, tossing his keys in the air and catching them. He was wearing a faded denim jacket, grinning like a man who had just broken out of maximum security.

"Are you sure about this, Julian?" I asked, standing under the awning of the biology building, holding a steaming cup of awful cafeteria coffee. "The VLA in New Mexico? You’re abandoning the doctoral program here for a glorified listening post in the middle of a desert?"

"I'm not abandoning anything, Arthur. I'm escaping," Julian laughed, leaning against the wet hood of his truck. "They want me to spend the next five years running computer simulations of dead galaxies. I want to hear them. Out there, in the desert, the sky is so clear you feel like you can reach out and touch the Milky Way. It's the big picture."

He walked over to me, the smile fading from his face, replaced by a rare, earnest gravity. He looked up at the towering, brutalist concrete architecture of my lab building.

"Come with me, Arthur."

I froze, the coffee burning my fingers. "What?"

"Leave the microscope. Leave Richard Finch and the ghost of Charles Darwin," Julian said, his voice urgent. "Pack a bag. Get in the Bronco. We drive west. You have a brilliant mind, Artie. Stop using it to look at the bars of your cage. Come out to the desert and look at the stars with me."

For three agonizing seconds, I wanted to say yes. I wanted to drop the coffee, run to my apartment, and leave the suffocating pressure of the micro-world behind forever.

But the invisible threads of my family's legacy were wrapped too tightly around my throat. I looked down at my shoes.

"I can't," I whispered. "The mechanism is here. My work is here."

Julian looked at me, a profound sadness settling in his eyes. He slowly nodded, stepping back into the rain.

"You're burying yourself alive, Arthur," he said softly. "I hope, someday, you find a way to look up."

He got into the Bronco. The V8 engine roared to life, and he drove out of the parking lot, heading west. I stood under the awning for a long time, watching his taillights disappear into the grey rain, feeling like a single cell that had just divided, leaving half of itself behind.

"Arthur! The path!"

Silas’s roar yanked me violently back to the present.

I looked forward and my blood ran cold. The invisible corridor of stable space wasn't just shrinking; it was twisting. The ground beneath our feet began to spiral, corkscrewing like a strand of DNA.

"Adjust your footing!" I screamed, instinctively throwing my weight to the left to compensate for the shifting gravity. "The midbody is twisting! It’s trying to snap the connection!"

We scrambled along the twisting spatial thread. The noise was unbearable—a deafening, harmonic screech of reality being stretched beyond its tensile limit.

Suddenly, Julian collapsed.

He didn't just trip. His legs simply gave out as a violent spasm racked his body. He fell onto the twisted dirt path, screaming in agony as he clutched his right arm.

"Julian!" I dropped the stopwatch and scrambled back to him.

The gauze wrapped around his hand had disintegrated into static. The localized ghost data wasn't just grafted to his hand anymore. The horrific, flickering pixelation had crept up his forearm, mapping itself over his veins. His arm was phasing in and out of our dimension.

"Get away from him, Finch!" Thorne barked, raising her assault rifle and aiming it directly at Julian.

"Put the gun down!" Silas roared, racking his shotgun and aiming it at Thorne’s head.

Captain Miller instantly raised his M4, aiming at Silas. It was a three-way standoff inside a collapsing dimension.

"Look at him, Doctor," Thorne said, her voice like ice, ignoring Silas's shotgun. "He touched the tear. His atomic structure is entangled with the parallel dimension. He is a corrupted file."

"He's not a file, he's a human being!" I yelled, shielding Julian with my body.

"He's an anchor!" Thorne shouted, pointing at the wall of static that was suddenly bowing inward, drawn directly toward Julian’s glowing, glitching arm. "The universe is trying to delete the overlap! His quantum signature is dragging the dimensional tear right toward us! If we don't leave him behind, or neutralize the corruption, the safe zone will collapse on all of us!"

I looked at the wall of white noise. She was right. The sheer wall of the cleavage furrow was bending, curving like a gravitational lens, pulled inexorably toward the ghost data spreading up Julian's arm. He was a magnet for the apocalypse.

"Arthur," Julian gasped, his face slick with sweat, his eyes wide with pain. He looked at his own phasing arm, then up at me. "She's right. The math checks out. I'm... I'm bleeding into the other side."

"I'm not leaving you," I said through gritted teeth, grabbing his good arm and trying to haul him up. "I stayed in the lab once. I watched you drive away. I'm not doing it again."

"Artie, you have to let go," Julian choked out, trying to pull away. "I'm pulling the tear toward you. You have to finish the observation. You have to survive the cut."

"Nobody is dying today!" Silas bellowed, his massive frame stepping between us and the collapsing wall of static.

Then something broke.

It was a single, sharp crack, like a piano wire snapping inside my own skull.

The ground beneath us violently shuddered. The intense, viscous air pressure instantly vanished. The twisting spatial corridor straightened out with a violent whiplash.

I looked ahead.

The path didn't continue. The thread had broken.

Fifty yards in front of us, the midbody had severed. The safe zone ended in a sheer, absolute wall of nothingness. We were standing on a severed umbilical cord, floating in the void.

"Abscission," I whispered, the biological term falling from my lips like a death sentence. "The final cut. The cell membrane has sealed."

The division was over.

We were standing in the aftermath. But as I looked around our tiny island of stable reality, floating in a sea of absolute static, I realized the terrifying truth.

We hadn't made it to the other side. And we hadn't stayed on Earth.

We were trapped inside the cut.
