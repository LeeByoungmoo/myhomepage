Episode 18: Goodbye, Major Tom
The fall was short, and the ground was unbelievably soft.

I tumbled across wet dirt and thick moss. The moment my shoulder hit the ground, a sharp pop sounded behind me, like the seal breaking on an enormous vacuum-packed jar. I rolled over and reached for the empty air I had just fallen through.

Nothing.

The blinding white noise, the ear-splitting spatial friction, the collapsing island of dirt—all were gone. Only the cool, damp forest air touched my fingertips. The dimensional door had closed forever.

"Julian!"

Silas’s primal roar tore through the quiet forest. The old prepper stumbled forward, frantically feeling the empty air.

"Julian! Answer me, kid!"

He punched the air and clawed at the dirt, searching for the doorway where his grandson had stood seconds ago. All he found was a dense, peaceful thicket of giant ferns.

Silas grabbed me by the collar and hauled me up from the mud. His blue eyes burned with rage and terror.

"Where is he?! Where is my grandson, Arthur!"

"He couldn't come..." I gasped, his face blurring behind my tears. "Julian couldn't cross, Silas. Half of him was already entangled with this universe. If he'd come through, Pauli exclusion... he would have collided with his other self and detonated."

"I should have dragged him through! I should have blasted that goddamn wall with my shotgun!" Silas screamed.

"If Julian hadn't held the door, we all would have dissolved into the void with that island."

A cold voice cut between us. It was Dr. Evelyn Thorne. She brushed the mud off her knees and stood up, checking her mechanical compass.

Silas threw me aside and lunged at Thorne. But she didn't back down. She stared straight up at the massive man without blinking.

"Silas Mercer." Thorne's voice was brutally rational, but her eyes held a heavy respect I had never seen in them. "Your grandson understood he was redundant data in a system that had run out of room. And he chose to stay in the trash so the rest of us could survive as the original copy. Don't cheapen what he did by taking a swing at me."

Silas's massive shoulders shook. A soldier and survivor to his core, he knew she was right. The strength drained slowly from his fists. He leaned back against the trunk of a giant redwood, slid to the ground, and buried his face in his hands. The muffled sobs of that hard old man were worse to hear than any scream.

I stumbled to my feet and looked around.

Chloe was sitting on the dirt, staring blankly into space. Captain Miller was gripping his M4 rifle, trying to maintain a defensive posture, but his muzzle was shaking aimlessly.

Then, I took in the world we were standing in.

The Nevada desert was completely gone. Instead, we stood in the middle of a towering coastal rainforest. The air was incredibly clear and clean. With every breath, the scent of rich pine and damp earth filled my lungs. The late afternoon sunlight filtering through the leaves was a blinding gold.

There were no glitching holograms, no warping gravity. Everything was maddeningly normal and peaceful.

"The membrane has closed," I murmured. "The division is over. We made it into the new universe. The B-side."

"Physics has stabilized," Thorne said, snapping her compass shut. "Magnetic North is pointing true north. Gravity is exactly one g. Atmospheric composition seems to be a ninety-nine percent match with the Earth we knew."

Thorne was a career bureaucrat down to the bone. Even grief, to her, was a luxury that could be scheduled for later. She helped Chloe to her feet and clapped the dazed Miller on the shoulder to snap him out of it.

"We have to move," Thorne ordered. "We have no intel on how vast this forest is or how the geography of this Earth has changed. We need to find high ground and establish a camp before sunset. Captain Miller, take the point."

We walked through the forest in silence. The Strykers that had carried us across the fault line were gone, along with most of our arsenal. All we had left were the torn clothes on our backs, a few weapons, and Silas's battered go-bag.

After about two hours of walking, the sun dipped below the skyline of the giant trees, and deep darkness began to settle over the forest. We set up camp at the base of a large boulder.

Silas pulled an old, hand-cranked military radio from his backpack. He turned the crank to wind the spring, then flipped the power switch.

The speaker crackled to life.

Only a clean, smooth hiss came through. No shrieking of a universe tearing itself apart, no demonic chorus of overlapping timelines. Just the faint whisper of the cosmic background. Ancient, stable, empty.

"It's clear," Silas muttered hoarsely, fiddling with the antenna. "There's no one. No signals."

I sat hugging my knees, listening to the soft static. An old David Bowie song started playing in my head: "Space Oddity." Julian used to play it on a loop in that rusty Bronco, belting out every word, off-key and unashamed.

The one about an astronaut who steps out of his capsule, looks back at the small blue planet he's leaving behind, and keeps drifting—calm, almost peaceful—while Ground Control calls his name into the static and gets no answer.

Julian had always hated the cramped lab. He wanted the stars; he wanted to understand the architecture of the universe. And in the end, instead of staying in a cage, he threw himself into the umbilical cord between two universes. By now he would have lost his physical form, scattered into particles somewhere inside that severed rift.

"Goodbye, Major Tom," I whispered, too quietly for anyone to hear. It was the only eulogy I had for my oldest friend, the greatest astrophysicist I ever knew.

As the darkness deepened, the night sky began to reveal itself through the forest canopy.

Chloe was the first to look up. She stared for a long moment, and when she spoke, her voice shook.

"Doctor... the sky is wrong."

I looked up through a gap in the branches. My breath caught. My heart stuttered, and a crushing headache clamped down on my skull, as if every blood vessel in my brain were being squeezed at once.

Captain Miller, Dr. Thorne, Silas—everyone looked up and froze.

The stars were shining. But it was not the sky we had seen all our lives.

Orion, Cassiopeia, the Big Dipper—the constellations we knew were shattered. No. Not shattered. Hollowed out.

Of the stars that used to fill the night sky, exactly half were cleanly gone, as if carved out with a knife. The once-dense band of the Milky Way was sparse, like missing teeth, and between them gaped terrifyingly massive, perfect black voids.

When a cell divides in two, the genetic material is split in half.

The mass of the universe had been split exactly in half, too, and dealt out between two universes. Those perfect gaps in the sky were scars—the outline of the other half of creation, which had shared our sky only yesterday and was now gone forever.

"What... what is this?" Miller groaned, clutching his chest, struggling for air. "I feel hollow. Like something got scooped out of me."

Chloe was weeping silently. I was, too. Not out of sadness.

It was a physical sense of loss. Every atom in the human body had spent 13.8 billion years anchored to the total mass and gravity of this universe. And half of that universe had just been cut away.

There's a name for the neurological glitch in which an amputee still feels pain, even itching, in a limb that is no longer there.

We were suffering from phantom limb syndrome on a cosmic scale. For the rest of time, every human being left on Earth would feel an inexplicable, absolute sense of loss whenever they looked up at that empty sky.

I clutched my grandfather's old biology notebook. I had survived to witness the end of this insane evolutionary mechanism, but the microscope inside my head had been smashed to pieces.

Looking up at the cold, enormous voids where half the stars had been, I was finally confronting—bone-deep, unmasked—the true face of the universe Julian had spent his whole life trying to show me.
