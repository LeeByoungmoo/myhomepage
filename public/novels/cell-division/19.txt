Episode 19: The Erased Half
The first morning in the new universe was terribly, blindingly bright.

I opened my eyes on damp moss. Every muscle in my body was screaming, and not from exhaustion. Something fundamental was off. I tried to sit up and nearly pitched forward. My weight hadn't changed. Gravity was still one g, pulling me toward the Earth's core with the same familiar force.

The problem wasn't mass. It was inertia.

I picked up a fist-sized rock and tossed it lightly into the air, meaning to catch it. But the muscle memory of a lifetime betrayed me. The rock shot up far faster and higher than I expected, sailed ten feet out of reach, and traced a bizarre arc before vanishing into the brush.

"You feel it too, Doctor."

Dr. Evelyn Thorne was sitting against a massive redwood, watching me. She was idly twirling her titanium survival knife. Even her deft movements were slightly exaggerated, overcorrected.

"I can't control my own body," Thorne said, frowning as she sheathed the knife. "My muscles put out the same force, but the resistance I should feel when I move is gone. My joints feel hollow."

"Mach's principle," I said, stumbling as I pushed myself off the ground. Even standing up used too much force; my knees snapped straight like a jackknife. "Ernst Mach proposed it over a century ago. An object's inertia—its resistance to acceleration—isn't built in. It comes from its relationship with all the other mass in the universe."

Nearby, Captain Miller woke, lurched to his feet, tripped over his own boots, and went face-first into the mud. He cursed, staring at his feet like they belonged to someone else.

I leaned on a branch instead of helping him up. "Last night we saw half the stars sliced out of the sky. Half the mass of the universe, gone. Cut that in half, and the cosmic inertia acting on our bodies drops with it."

Chloe came over, pale. "So... the phantom limb thing isn't just in our heads?"

"No. It's physical," I said, slowly opening and closing my fist. "Every nerve and muscle we have was calibrated for a universe with all its mass intact. Now half of it is gone. Our bodies still expect the resistance of mass that doesn't exist anymore, and they push accordingly. That's why we keep overshooting and stumbling."

We all looked down at our hands in silence. The scar the universe had left on us wasn't carved into our skin. It was carved into the laws of physics, so that we would feel the missing half in every movement, every breath.

"Then we adapt," Thorne said, breaking the silence. She cinched her tactical vest and stood. "The brain is plastic. Give it a few days of falling on our faces and it'll recalibrate to the new inertial frame. Until then, keep your movements small so you don't blow out a joint. Move out."

We started walking through the forest again.

Silas Mercer walked like an empty shell. The hard, sharp-edged old prepper was gone. The Remington rode on his shoulder, but the muzzle drooped toward the ground. When Julian disappeared behind that closed door, half of Silas's universe went with him. I walked beside him, but there were no words of comfort anywhere in the physics of this new world.

After about three hours of shuffling along in the awkward, bent-kneed lope of astronauts on the Moon, things began to change.

Bright light began to break through the pillars of the redwood forest. The air changed. Instead of damp moss, a salty, briny breeze drifted through the trees.

Captain Miller, taking the point, stepped out of the treeline and stopped dead.

"Doctor..." Miller's voice was flat with shock. "This isn't on any map."

We pushed through the last of the ferns behind him, and the world fell away.

The Nevada desert was gone. Where the forest ended, the land dropped away in a sheer, dizzying cliff hundreds of feet high. Below it, a deep blue ocean stretched to the horizon, heaving with huge waves and flinging up great plumes of white foam.

"The ocean..." Chloe whispered, cautiously approaching the edge of the cliff. "We were in the middle of the Black Rock Desert in Nevada yesterday. How..."

"This Earth's tectonics took a different road," Thorne said, checking her compass as she looked down at the water. "No Sierra Nevada. No coastal ranges. The Pacific never got walled off—it just kept pushing inland. Out here, Nevada is beachfront property." She snapped the compass shut. "Welcome to the new West Coast."

I stared at the vast horizon. No land. No ships. Nothing. We didn't even know whether human beings existed in this universe. If this world's humanity had torn itself apart trying to avoid quantum overlap—like the parallel convoy that ambushed us—we might be the last primates on an empty planet.

Just then, Silas pointed north along the edge of the cliff.

"There," Silas's cracked voice rang out. "A structure."

At the tip of a headland about a mile north, battered by the sea wind, stood a white, domed building. Not a military installation. Not a bunker. The dome was half open, and vines had crawled up its walls; nature was slowly swallowing it whole.

We immediately quickened our pace toward it.

As we got closer, there was no mistaking it. It was an observatory.

It looked like it had been abandoned for decades. The metal doors had rusted off their hinges, and the concrete floor was carpeted with moss and ferns. Miller went in first, rifle raised, but the place was empty. No bodies. No animals. Only time, layered thick on everything.

In the center of the room, a massive refracting telescope of steel and brass pointed up through the half-open dome like the barrel of an artillery piece.

The moment I saw the telescope, my heart sank.

*The truth is in the cage, Arthur. Not in the clouds.*

My father's voice from the night he smashed Julian's telescope rang in my ears like an auditory hallucination. The prison of the microscope that had crushed me my whole life. He had locked me in the micro-world and forbidden me the universe.

I walked toward the massive telescope as if possessed.

"Arthur, what are you doing?" Thorne asked warily.

I didn't answer. I wiped the thick dust off the brass tube with my bare hands. The metal was cold under my fingertips. I grabbed the rusted elevation crank. It let out a metallic shriek that echoed through the dome, but the gears still caught and turned.

*So please, Artie. This time, run away from the microscope.*

I remembered Julian's last smile as he dissolved into the rift. He had longed his whole life for the world on the far side of a lens like this one. And he had thrown himself away to protect it.

I took a deep breath. Then I leaned in and put my eye to the eyepiece of a telescope for the first time in twenty-six years.

It had taken us most of the afternoon to clear the debris and coax the dome shutters wider. Now the sun was sinking toward the sea, not yet fully set, but the air was so clear that the cold abyss of deep space poured straight through the glass.

The universe through the lens was far more violent, far crueler, than anything we'd seen with the naked eye.

Where galaxy clusters should have been—where nebulae should have glowed like nurseries of gas—I saw geometric cuts of unimaginable scale.

It wasn't just that the stars had gone dark. The fabric of space itself had been stripped away. The line between glowing galaxies and the void that swallowed them was razor sharp, as if laser-cut. Magnified, the universe was a grotesque collage—a masterpiece painted over 13.8 billion years, with someone's scissors run mercilessly through half of it.

I couldn't pull my eye away. I was gasping.

After a cell divides, it takes in nutrients from its environment, replaces its lost mass, and grows again. G1 phase. But the universe is a closed system. There is no outside to feed from. The erased half would stay empty—a scar that would never fill, not until the day the universe died.

"Doctor?" Chloe came up beside me and took my arm. "What do you see?"

I slowly pulled my eye away from the lens and stood up. A drop of cold sweat ran down my cheek.

My father's microscope had taught me to trust the mechanism blindly. Julian's telescope made me see—down to the bone—just how ruthless and overwhelming that mechanism really was.

Gripping the telescope, I looked around at the people standing in that empty observatory.

"I see the end of evolution," I said, and my voice echoed hollowly through the dome. "The universe copied its data and dodged physical annihilation. But the price was this. It's crippled for good. Those holes in the sky will never close."

Thorne glanced at the eyepiece without a word. Something flickered in her cold eyes. Silas sat on the floor, listening to nothing but the radio's hiss.

We survived. We survived the most violent surgery the cosmos had ever performed.

But stranded on this isolated planet, staring up at the erased half of creation, I would live with a phantom limb that never stopped aching. Being the one left to observe wasn't the blessing of survival. It was a curse: to record every bit of that loss with my own two eyes.
